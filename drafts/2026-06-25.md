# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-25

## Idea 1 — The Invisible Author

**Angle:** Six years of writing read by tens of thousands of HR professionals — under a brand, no byline. What your relationship to quality looks like when the work can't be traced back to you.

**Hook:** The piece I worked hardest on in 2023 has been read by more people than I'll ever meet. Nobody knows I wrote it.

**Outline:**
- The unsexy reality of B2B content: most of it runs under the company name. No byline, no author page, no trace back to a person. You write for the brand.
- What this demands: you can't hold the work as identity. It either stands on its own or it doesn't. Your relationship to quality has to be entirely internal — there's no external loop to validate it.
- The specific friction for someone who came from broadcast: on TV, your face was the credential. The camera vouched for you. In content, the writing is the credential, and the writing has no face.
- A question she's still sitting with: does the invisibility make the work better? Or does it reveal that visible credit was doing more load-bearing work than she thought?

**Tone:** quietly philosophical

## Idea 2 — The Wrong Tool for the Right Problem

**Angle:** Formal training in PR and Image Management showed her precisely why organizations can't "manage" their way to a good culture — the logic that works for individuals breaks down the moment you try to apply it at scale.

**Hook:** I studied image management before I ever worked in marketing. It's probably why I'm skeptical of every employer branding strategy I've read since.

**Outline:**
- What Image Management actually teaches: you can deliberately construct and shape signals — how you present, communicate, carry yourself — to influence how others perceive you. It works. At the individual level, it genuinely works.
- The organizational version of the same move: employer branding, culture decks, recognition program rollouts, CEO all-hands. The underlying logic is identical. Signal-shaping, at scale.
- The specific failure point: personal image management works because the person constructing the image is also the person experiencing it. The gap between image and lived reality stays internal. At an organizational scale, employees live in that gap, and they feel it immediately and precisely.
- What this means for the HR content she actually writes: the question is never "how do we present this better." It's "how do we build something that doesn't need to be presented differently than it actually is."

**Tone:** sharp, a little contrarian

## Idea 3 — Recognition, Turned Around

**Angle:** After years of writing about employee recognition from the manager's and organization's side, a quiet turn of the lens — what has recognition actually felt like as the employee?

**Hook:** I've written about employee recognition more than almost any other topic. I've done the research, built the frameworks, written the manager guides. What I've never written about is what it's been like to be on the receiving end.

**Outline:**
- Naming the asymmetry: professional HR content almost always holds the employee experience at arm's length, building from the outside. She's been doing this for six years.
- What shifts when you turn the lens: the moments of recognition that didn't land even when they were technically correct — the right words, the wrong attention. And the offhand observations that landed harder than any formal program.
- The thing that matters most, from the inside: specificity. Whether the person recognizing you actually saw the specific thing you did, or whether they saw the category of thing you did. Those are not the same experience.
- The reframe for her HR audience: you can design an architecturally excellent recognition program that fails because the people implementing it aren't paying close enough attention. The framework is not the work. The attention is the work.

**Tone:** honest, careful, grounded in experience rather than research
