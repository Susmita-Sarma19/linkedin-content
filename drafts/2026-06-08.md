# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-08

## Idea 1 — The Live Standard

**Angle:** Nine years of live hosting built one reflex above all others — decide and don't flinch — and it turns out that reflex is the thing most absent from digital work, where the undo button is always there.

**Hook:** "In live television, you don't get to undo the last sentence. That's not a constraint — it's actually the working condition that makes you better."

**Outline:**
- Live hosting means you make the editorial call in real time: the question that reframes the segment, the pivot when a guest goes somewhere unexpected, the sentence you choose to end on — all of it decided and committed to while the red light is on
- Digital work has infinite drafts, infinite undo, infinite refinement — which sounds like an advantage and partly is, but also produces a specific kind of paralysis: the inability to decide while uncertain
- What nine years of live TV actually trained: not speed, but tolerance for committing to a position without having all the information, and then moving forward from that position rather than holding it lightly
- The post ends with what she still catches herself doing — going back and re-opening decisions she already made, not because new information arrived, but because the undo button exists

**Tone:** Crisp, counterintuitive, quietly useful

## Idea 2 — The Double Audience

**Angle:** She writes recognition content for HR practitioners — people who build systems to make employees feel seen — who are also employees, wondering the same thing those systems are supposed to solve.

**Hook:** "The people I write recognition content for are also employees who want to feel recognized. I've never written a piece that said that out loud."

**Outline:**
- The assumed reader in most HR content is a manager or practitioner looking outward: how do I build a better recognition system for my people? The assumed reader is positioned above the problem
- But HR professionals are employees too — they work inside the organisations they're trying to improve, often without the recognition infrastructure they're building for everyone else
- What that dual position does to how she thinks about what she's writing: the sentence that would be useful to a practitioner-as-implementer is often different from the one that would land for a practitioner-as-employee; and both people are in the same seat
- She doesn't have a clean answer. The post ends with the observation that this tension is probably what makes HR content feel hollow when it doesn't account for it — it addresses the role and forgets the person in it

**Tone:** Perceptive, honest without being confessional, sits with the question

## Idea 3 — The Cluster You Don't Have

**Angle:** Nobody in Guwahati does exactly what she does — no peers in proximity, no local industry events, no echo chamber. For a long time she read that as a gap. It also turns out to be something else.

**Hook:** "There's no one in Guwahati doing what I do. That used to feel like a problem. At some point it started feeling like a different kind of advantage."

**Outline:**
- Professional clusters are genuinely useful: they give you benchmarks, colleagues who understand what you're trying to do without lengthy explanation, a sense of what's normal in your field — all real and worth having
- What they also do, quietly: they produce a layer of received opinion about how work in your field should look, what success is supposed to feel like, which directions are worth moving in — and because that opinion comes from proximity rather than deliberation, you often absorb it without noticing
- Working without that cluster means every professional position has to be earned independently: the standards she holds her work to weren't inherited from an industry cohort, they were built from first principles and kept being tested with no one nearby to confirm them
- This isn't a case for isolation. It's a more specific observation: the absence of a cluster forces a certain self-reliance in judgment — and that self-reliance is worth something she only recognized because she eventually got to compare notes with people who didn't have it

**Tone:** Grounded, quietly provocative, specific
