# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-10-30

## Idea 1 — The Credit That Goes Elsewhere

**Angle:** Writing brand content means your best work carries someone else's name — what that teaches about ownership, craft, and why you do the work at all.

**Hook:** The best thing I wrote this year has no byline. And honestly, that stopped bothering me somewhere around year three.

**Outline:**
- Most content marketing is published under a brand voice, not a personal one — the writer disappears into the institution
- Early career, that invisibility felt like erasure; later it started to feel like something else — the difference between writing for credit and writing because the work matters
- The discipline of brand writing: you can't coast on personality or style quirks; the piece has to carry its own weight, without the author as safety net
- What it taught about craft: when your name isn't there, all that's left is whether the reader was served

**Tone:** Quietly settled

## Idea 2 — What Appraisal Season Does to Me

**Angle:** Writing professionally about performance culture while living through annual appraisals creates a strange double consciousness — what that dissonance reveals that the research doesn't.

**Hook:** It's appraisal season. I write about appraisals for a living. This is the week when every confident sentence I've published gets tested against the real thing.

**Outline:**
- She spends the year writing about how recognition, feedback, and performance conversations should work — then sits across from her own manager in the same cycle
- The gap between the research (what good appraisal design looks like) and the lived experience (what it actually feels like to wait for a score) is not small
- Specific: the sentence "employees value specific, timely feedback" reads differently at 4pm on appraisal review day than it does at 10am on a Tuesday in March
- What the dual position offers: not cynicism, but precision — the claims that hold up under lived scrutiny, and the ones that don't, are not always the ones you'd expect

**Tone:** Wry, honest

## Idea 3 — What the Degree Didn't Predict

**Angle:** A Mass Communication education built on journalism ethics meets a job built around search algorithms — the gap between what her training promised and what digital content actually became.

**Hook:** My degree taught me to inform, not to perform. Nobody told me the platform wouldn't know the difference.

**Outline:**
- Mass Communication training: accuracy, source credibility, the reader's right to useful information — a framework built for journalism, applied now to HR content marketing
- The collision: SEO rewards what ranks, not necessarily what informs; the best article and the most discoverable article are often not the same article
- How she resolved the tension — not by abandoning the journalism principles, but by insisting the two can coexist when the content is genuinely useful rather than keyword-shaped
- What it means to hold a journalist's instinct inside a content marketer's role: it's slower, sometimes less viral, and harder to prove in a metrics deck — but it's what keeps the work from going hollow

**Tone:** Grounded, a little stubborn
