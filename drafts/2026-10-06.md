# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-10-06

## Idea 1 — The Distance That Made Me Deliberate

**Angle:** Being 2,000 km from the nearest industry conference forced a more intentional relationship with professional learning — one that turns out to be an advantage most people in metro cities don't develop.

**Hook:** I have never attended an industry conference in person.
For years I treated that as a gap. Now I'm not sure it was.

**Outline:**
- Being based in Guwahati means the osmosis that metro professionals take for granted — the hallway conversation, the side session, the after-party that turns into an industry connection — has never been available to her. Distance made all of it intentional.
- What replaces proximity: deep reading, deliberate outreach, recorded talks watched twice. Slower, less social, more processed. You arrive at an idea through effort rather than absorption.
- The result is a different relationship with the information. Absorbed knowledge feels like weather — you're not sure when you got it. Sought knowledge you remember acquiring.
- Not an argument that Tier-2 cities are better. An argument that the constraint built a specific muscle that people who had proximity available never had to develop.

**Tone:** Grounded, quietly contrarian

## Idea 2 — The Question I Stopped Asking

**Angle:** One podcast conversation redirected her entire approach to HR content — not by offering a better answer, but by revealing that she'd been asking the wrong question for years.

**Hook:** A guest stopped me mid-question once and said: "That's not actually what I mean."
She was right. I never asked that question again.

**Outline:**
- Running a podcast about HR means she's been inside hundreds of practitioner conversations. Most confirm what she already believed. One rewrote how she thinks.
- The question she'd stopped questioning: "What does research say about employee recognition?" A practitioner interrupted it to say — recognition isn't primarily a research problem. It's a relationship problem. The two things require different writing.
- What changed: she stopped leading with frameworks and started leading with the thing practitioners actually experience. The content is less theoretical and more useful as a result.
- The point isn't the specific insight — it's that the best editorial shifts in her career haven't come from reading. They've come from a practitioner with less patience for abstraction than she had.

**Tone:** Reflective, honest about being redirected

## Idea 3 — The Most Honest Content in HR

**Angle:** Anonymous employee reviews on sites like Glassdoor and AmbitionBox are the one place where people in the workplace say exactly what they mean — and almost nobody in HR content treats them as a primary source.

**Hook:** I spend part of every week reading anonymous employee reviews on Glassdoor and AmbitionBox.
Not to monitor sentiment. To understand what people sound like when they're finally being honest.

**Outline:**
- HR content lives in the vocabulary of the field: engagement, recognition, belonging, psychological safety. Those words do real work. They also create a sealed room — people inside the profession use them fluently; the people they describe often don't.
- Anonymous reviews break the seal. The writing is unpolished, inconsistent, sometimes bitter. It's also often the clearest articulation of what "engagement" means to someone who has never heard the term professionally.
- She reads them as editorial research. Not for company intelligence — for language. What are people actually trying to say when they say they felt invisible at work? What does recognition look like when it works, described by someone who wasn't told what to call it?
- There is more honest testimony about workplace experience in those reviews than in most HR publications. That's not a criticism of the field. It's a reminder that the people the field is for don't always get to write the field's content.

**Tone:** Observational, a little pointed, practitioner-respecting
