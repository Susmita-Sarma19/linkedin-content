# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-13

## Idea 1 — The Franchise Question

### Hook
My first job in Kolkata was helping bring Subway to the Northeast.
Five months of asking whether a format made elsewhere could actually land here.

### Angle
March 2015. Coordinator at Subway Systems India's development office for Northeast and Eastern India. The job was franchise development: site selection, franchisee support, operational standards. A global brand, designed to travel, being introduced to a region it had mostly designed for rather than from.

The logistical questions were visible. The quieter question underneath them was not: does this format — built with a specific customer, a specific urban footprint, a specific relationship to convenience in mind — actually fit the place it's been sent? Five months. Then releaseMyAd. She moved on. The question stayed.

She's been working with HR frameworks ever since — recognition models, engagement architectures, research built in one organizational context, applied broadly. The structure of the question is the same. Built to travel. Applied somewhere else. Does it fit here? The Subway stint didn't teach her to ask it. It gave her a concrete example of what ignoring it looked like from the inside, and what asking it cost to actually do.

### Source
Coordinator, Subway Systems India Pvt. Ltd (March–July 2015, 5 months), Kolkata — development office for Northeast and Eastern India. Franchise development for a global brand expanding into a regional market. Entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. The five-month Kolkata role that precedes releaseMyAd has appeared only as a timeline entry; its specific professional content — working at the intersection of a standardized global format and a regional context that doesn't share its origin assumptions — has never been examined.

### Resonance
For HR practitioners who implement nationally or globally designed recognition and engagement programs into regional, cultural, or organizational contexts that weren't their origin: the Subway question is their question. The post doesn't argue against standard frameworks. It names the check that good implementation requires — and where the cost of skipping it shows up.

## Idea 2 — The Two Months Before

### Hook
In June 2013, I was learning how a show was made.
In August 2013, I was making it.

### Angle
Two months as a production intern at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati. Observing, assisting, learning the internal logic of how a broadcast gets assembled — the rundowns, the cues, the places where a live show can go wrong and the places where it almost never does.

Then August 2013: she was offered the Talk Show Host role.

The intern phase wasn't preparation for the job in the way internships usually get framed — CV line, credential, exposure. It was the two months when nobody was watching her closely, so she was watching closely herself. She already knew what the show needed, where it was thin, what the format asked of the person at its center. When she stepped in front of the camera, she'd seen enough to know the shape of what she was stepping into.

This is not a story about being noticed. It's a more specific observation: the observation phase is the preparation phase. What you pay attention to when you're not expected to perform determines a lot about what you become when you are.

### Source
Production Intern at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (June–July 2013, 2 months), immediately followed by Talk Show Host at the same institution (August 2013–December 2022, 9 years 5 months). The two-month intern-to-anchor transition is entirely unexplored. All prior drafts examine the Talk Show Host tenure from multiple angles — the live format's discipline, the public mandate, the pre-broadcast internal state, the concurrent digital career — but none has examined the two months that immediately preceded it: the intern phase and what observing the inside of the production gave her before she was asked to stand at its center.

### Resonance
For HR practitioners who design onboarding, internship, and early talent programs: what early-career professionals pay attention to before they're expected to perform shapes what they're capable of when they are. The intern phase that looks passive is often the most active learning environment of a career — if the person is using it to read the shape of what the role actually requires. For early-career professionals: the observation phase is the preparation phase.

## Idea 3 — The Nine Months Nobody Asks About

### Hook
Between Kolkata and Vantage Circle, I spent nine months at a company called SmartCoach.
It doesn't fit the clean arc. That's probably why it's been the most useful.

### Angle
January 2018. Back in Guwahati after Kolkata. Content, branding, and advertising at SmartCoach — nine months of creative generalist work that doesn't appear in the version of her career that gets told. Not because it was bad. Because it doesn't fit the arc: TV host → Kolkata → digital marketer. SmartCoach sits in the middle of that, a smaller organization, a different kind of brief.

Branding work forces a foundational question before anything else: what is this actually for, and for whom? You can't write the tagline until you've answered it. You can't design the tone until you know whose attention you're asking for and what you're asking them to do with it.

Six years of HR content at Vantage Circle runs on the same question. She asks it before she writes anything. The question isn't a habit she developed there — it's one SmartCoach made non-negotiable under deadline, for someone else's product, with no institutional framework to lean on.

The nine-month role that doesn't fit the story gave her the one question that runs through all of it.

### Source
Creative Associate, SmartCoach, Guwahati (January–September 2018, 9 months) — content, branding, and advertising. Cited across prior drafts only as a timeline anchor (the end-date for the unrecorded gap in July 10; the first Guwahati role after Kolkata in July 12). Never examined as a standalone biographical moment. The experience of nine months of creative generalist branding work — immediately before six years of specialist digital marketing at Vantage Circle — and what it demanded, and what it left behind, is entirely unexplored.

### Resonance
For content professionals and marketing practitioners: the stint that doesn't fit the narrative often taught something the cleaner roles couldn't, because it required first-principles thinking without institutional scaffolding. For HR practitioners who design career paths and talent development: the generalist phase — in a career, on a team — isn't a detour from specialization. It's often what makes the specialization possible.
