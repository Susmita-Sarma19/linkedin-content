# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-11-23

## Idea 1 — The Audience You Didn't Choose

**Angle:** On Doordarshan, the audience didn't select your content — they inherited it. That relationship is fundamentally different from anything algorithm-optimized platforms produce, and it taught a kind of responsibility the engagement economy doesn't require.

**Hook:** On Doordarshan, nobody chose to watch my show. The television was on. That was enough for them to be there.

**Outline:**
- Doordarshan's distribution model — no personalization, no recommendation engine, no metric of active selection. The viewer inherited whatever was on at 7pm.
- The specific responsibility this creates: you're not making content for people who already wanted it. You're earning people who showed up for something else entirely.
- How this shaped her orientation to audience — not just "who is this for?" but "who else is this for?" Writing toward the person who wasn't expecting to need what you made.
- What broadcast teaches that digital never does: the difference between an opt-in audience (everyone on LinkedIn chose to be here) and an inherited audience. One rewards what already works. The other requires you to earn people who had no intention of being earned.

**Tone:** Meditative, quietly provocative

## Idea 2 — On Camera vs. On Air

**Angle:** She's hosted television interviews and podcast interviews — two forms that share almost nothing about how you hold space for a guest. The craft difference between the visual and the audio interview is worth naming precisely.

**Hook:** On television, a guest can see me react. On the podcast, they can only hear me breathe. Those are different conversations.

**Outline:**
- The TV interview: visual body language as cue and comfort. The guest watches your face while they talk. Silence looks like something — engagement, interest, or discomfort. The host's face is part of the interview.
- The podcast interview: no visual feedback. The guest has to trust the voice alone. So does the host. Every audible response — the small affirmation, the pause, the deliberate quiet — carries more weight because it's the only signal in the room.
- What she had to unlearn when she moved from camera to microphone: the nod that signals attentiveness on television is dead air on a podcast. You have to find the audible equivalent, and it's not the same gesture.
- The skill from TV hosting that the podcast needed most: knowing when to stay completely silent so the guest's thought can find its end. That discipline belongs to both formats — it just sounds different.

**Tone:** Specific, quietly expert

## Idea 3 — The Multilingual Seam

**Angle:** Four languages aren't four channels for the same thought — they're four different access points to what a thought actually is. The seams between them are where precision lives.

**Hook:** I write in English. I think in Bengali. The word I'm reaching for usually belongs to Assamese.

**Outline:**
- The multilingual mind doesn't translate — it locates. Each language carries concepts the others don't quite hold. The English word arrives first, but sometimes it's the wrong container.
- What this means in HR content: concepts like belonging, dignity, or fairness sit differently in each of her four languages. The English is most accessible to her audience. But Assamese is often where the concept is most precisely felt — closest to what the person at work is actually experiencing, even if they'd never write it down that way.
- Writing for people who are thinking in a different language than the one you're using: her LinkedIn audience includes HR practitioners across India who are reading in English and thinking in Marathi, Telugu, Bengali, Hindi. She's not writing for people who live inside the English. She's writing toward people who've arrived there from somewhere else — the same trip she makes every time she sits down to write.
- What the multilingual seam teaches about content work: precision matters more when you can't lean on shared assumption. The writer who carries multiple languages builds a different relationship to the word she's chosen — always aware there's another word in another register, wondering if it's the better one.

**Tone:** Curious, culturally grounded
