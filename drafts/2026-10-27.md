# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-10-27

## Idea 1 — The Post That Keeps Going Without You

**Angle:** An evergreen piece she wrote in 2020 still ranks on page one — but the version of herself who wrote it has moved on, complicated the thinking, and can't take it back.

**Hook:** There's a piece I wrote in 2020 that still ranks on the first page of Google. I agree with most of it. I'd write it differently now.

**Outline:**
- Evergreen content is how digital marketing builds long-term value — a well-placed piece from years ago compounds traffic without new input
- But it also means an older version of your thinking is still being read as current, with no footnote explaining what's changed
- The specific experience: reading a 2020 piece on recognition best practices and seeing where the certainty was cleaner than it should have been, where the framework has since developed edges she didn't account for
- Not a retraction — but a real question about what it means to write things that outlast the perspective they came from; the piece keeps doing its job, and the author has quietly moved past parts of it

**Tone:** Reflective, a little wry

## Idea 2 — The Industry She Built a Career In, From Outside Its Rooms

**Angle:** Over a decade in digital marketing without ever attending an industry conference — and what that required her to build instead.

**Hook:** I've been working in digital marketing for over eleven years. I have never attended an industry conference. Not one.

**Outline:**
- Industry credibility is partly built in rooms: panels, events, hallway conversations, the off-programme dinners where most of the actual networking happens — none of that exists in Guwahati
- The alternative wasn't a compromise: it was writing that had to travel instead of her, content that reached the audience she couldn't physically access, a podcast that put her voice into a format that didn't require a plane ticket
- The pandemic briefly made this the default for everyone; when things reopened, the industry's centre of gravity snapped back to the same cities — and the professionals who'd been building from the periphery were already used to it
- What you learn when you build without the infrastructure: the things you thought were necessary for professional visibility turn out to be one path, not the only one; the credential that matters is whether the work reaches the right person

**Tone:** Quietly confident, a little defiant

## Idea 3 — What I Can and Can't See Clearly From Here

**Angle:** The specific epistemological tension of writing HR best-practice content as an employee of a company that sells HR software — and what naming that tension honestly requires.

**Hook:** The company I work for builds HR software. I write about what good HR looks like. Those two facts are not separate from each other.

**Outline:**
- Vendor content operates under permanent suspicion: the expertise is real, but the interest is obvious, and readers know it
- What that position actually does to the writing: you notice certain problems more acutely — the ones that exist in the space your product was built to solve — which means other problems probably go less examined
- Six years of writing in this position has given her genuine fluency with the domain, and also shaped what feels important enough to write about; the blind spots aren't separate from the expertise, they're part of the same formation
- The honest version of vendor content writing: not claiming neutrality you don't have, but naming the perspective clearly — here is what I can see from where I stand, and here is what I probably miss

**Tone:** Candid, self-aware, a little uncomfortable in the right way
