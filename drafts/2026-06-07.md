# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-07

## Idea 1 — The Survey

### Hook
"In September 2013, I was one month into hosting a talk show. My second project that month: going door to door in villages outside Guwahati asking people if they'd watched the channel."

### Angle
She had just become a Talk Show Host at Doordarshan Kendra — two months earlier she'd been an intern, now she was on the other side of the camera. Almost immediately, she picked up a one-month research assignment under IIMC New Delhi: a campaign studying the reach and access of Bharat Nirman government advertisements in rural Guwahati. Clipboard. Doorsteps. Real households. The gap between what she assumed about audiences from inside the studio and what she found in those villages was significant — not catastrophic, just clarifying. The imagined viewer and the actual viewer were not the same person. Arriving at that knowledge early, before she'd built years of assumptions on top of it, is something she didn't understand at the time as luck. She understands it now. Most people who make content never go look for the audience. She did it in her second month, by accident.

### Source
September 2013, 1-month research surveyor under IIMC New Delhi — reach and access of Bharat Nirman advertisements in rural Guwahati. Taken on a month after formally beginning her Talk Show Host role at Doordarshan. Completely unexplored in all prior drafts; no previous idea has touched this specific fieldwork experience or the early-career gap it revealed between production and reception.

### Resonance
Content creators, marketers, and HR communicators who build models of their audience from inside the production room — this names the specific value of encountering the real audience before assumptions calcify. Also lands for anyone who did early-career work that seemed tangential and only understood it as foundational much later.

## Idea 2 — The Other Side of the Desk

### Hook
"For two years in Kolkata, my job was to get media to cover things. Before that, I'd been the media. The reversal changed what I understood about both."

### Angle
She spent her early career inside Doordarshan — understanding coverage from the broadcaster's position: what gets airtime, what doesn't, how editorial decisions are made. At releaseMyAd.com in Kolkata (2015–2017), she crossed to the other side of that desk. Her job was media relations — representing clients who needed placement, coverage, a space in someone else's platform. What she expected from that vantage: to feel like the person who used to say no is now the one asking. What she actually found: the view from both sides is genuinely different, and neither is more honest than the other. From inside media, client outreach looks like noise. From the client side, editorial indifference looks like opacity. Neither reading captures the full picture. What she took from those two years wasn't cynicism about media — it was a specific ability to hold two incompatible perspectives on the same exchange without needing to resolve them. She uses it every time she's building a brief for an external audience or trying to understand why a piece didn't land.

### Source
Media Relations Associate at releaseMyAd.com, August 2015 – July 2017, Kolkata. Her only professional role entirely outside Guwahati. Came after the early Doordarshan years and directly preceded her return and the SmartCoach / Vantage Circle transition. Completely unexplored in all prior drafts — no previous idea has touched this specific professional inversion or the Kolkata media relations period.

### Resonance
Anyone who has worked both sides of a client-vendor or media-pitch divide — and specifically, professionals in content and communications who find that they periodically have to argue both sides of the same table. The insight is not that one side is right; it's that holding both simultaneously changes the quality of your judgment. Also resonates with HR professionals who write for employers while being employees themselves.

## Idea 3 — The Substructure

### Hook
"My B.Com was supposed to be the path I didn't take. Ten years later, I noticed it had come with me anyway."

### Angle
She studied commerce for five years at Gauhati Commerce College — business, accounting, management — then pivoted entirely into Mass Communication and media. The pivot was real. But a B.Com is not simply a credential; it's a way of reading organizations. She carries it now in specific, concrete ways: she reads content briefs with an eye toward what the organisation is actually trying to solve at a cost level, not just a communication one. She understands what recognition programs cost and why that matters to a CFO's read of the employee experience budget. When she writes about the value of engagement for an HR audience, she's writing from inside a commercial logic most communicators don't have. The abandoned track wasn't abandoned — it became the substructure that makes her particular combination of skills unusual rather than generic. This isn't a lesson about everything happening for a reason. It's more specific: the things we deliberately leave behind don't always cooperate. Sometimes they become the foundation.

### Source
B.Com, Gauhati Commerce College (2007–2012). Five years of formal commercial and business training before a complete pivot into Mass Communication. The 2026-06-01 idea "The Wrong Picture" addressed the wrong-plan framing — having a detailed plan that turns out not to fit. This idea is distinct: not about planning and revising, but about how a specific body of knowledge from an "abandoned" path shows up concretely in current professional work. Unexplored in all prior drafts.

### Resonance
Professionals who've had multiple chapters and carry quiet guilt or confusion about the "detours" — this reframes an abandoned path not as wasted time but as functional infrastructure. Particularly resonant for career changers and anyone who has wondered what to make of skills they didn't think they'd use. For her HR and marketing audience specifically, the commercial literacy angle is immediately recognizable and useful.
