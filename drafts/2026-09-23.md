# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-23

## Idea 1 — The Silence After Send

**Angle:** Nine years on live television gave her an immediate, physical read on whether she was losing the room. Six years of digital content has trained her to write well without any feedback at all. That's a different skill — and she's still deciding which one she prefers.

**Hook:** On live television, I could tell in the first sixty seconds whether I was losing the room. Six years of digital content has trained me to write without that signal. I'm still deciding which one made me better.

**Outline:**
- In a studio, the feedback is immediate and embodied — the producer's voice in your ear, the guest's posture shifting, the silence that tells you something landed or didn't; that information shapes the next sentence in real time
- In digital content, the equivalent is a dashboard you look at two days later — a number, not a reaction; a pageview is not a person nodding, and a bounce rate is not a person leaving the room
- What that absence produces over six years: either discipline or drift; you write with more internal conviction because there's nothing to read externally, or you start writing for the metric instead of the moment
- The TV instinct she kept: a real person is on the other side of this sentence; the discipline she built in digital: she has to believe that without any evidence that it's working

**Tone:** Honest, a little restless

## Idea 2 — The Microphone on the Wrong Side

**Angle:** She has spent six years producing podcast episodes — drawing out HR practitioners, holding the interview, making experts sound like themselves. Then she sat down as a guest on someone else's show. Being a subject, after years of being the interviewer, rearranged what she thought she knew about both.

**Hook:** I've interviewed roughly a hundred HR practitioners over the last few years. Last year, someone turned the microphone toward me. I had forty-five seconds of genuine blankness. I hadn't expected that.

**Outline:**
- The podcast producer's skill is directional: you hold the frame for someone else, you pull the insight out of the expert, you make their thinking legible — none of that prepares you for the inversion
- As a guest she caught herself doing something she'd watched prepared guests do and had spent years trying to undo: reaching for the packaged answer instead of the real one; easier than she expected, and more embarrassing
- What surprised her most: the interviewer across from her was good, and a good interviewer is uncomfortable — they wait through the silence instead of filling it; she'd been that interviewer; she'd never been on the receiving end of it
- What she carried out of it: renewed respect for the practitioners she books, who show up without a script and still say something true; and a specific edit she made to her own interviewing next session

**Tone:** Wry, self-aware, specific

## Idea 3 — What the Advice Assumes

**Angle:** She writes employee engagement content that travels across India. From Guwahati, she can feel when a "universal" best practice was actually imagined in one kind of city — the open-plan office, the ping-pong table, the afterwork team drink. The advice is technically accurate. The world it imagines isn't universal.

**Hook:** I write employee engagement content that gets read across India. Sometimes I can feel, writing a piece, that it was imagined in a city I'm not in. The advice is correct. The world it assumes isn't universal.

**Outline:**
- "Best practices" in workplace culture are usually described as if organizations everywhere have the same infrastructure, same social rhythms, same commute patterns — the implicit image is a Bengaluru or Mumbai knowledge-work office, and it shapes the examples, the benchmarks, the "what good looks like"
- Writing from Guwahati means she notices the gap between what the advice assumes and what her readership's organizations actually look like: a manufacturing floor in Jorhat, a government-adjacent office where team drinks aren't a realistic option, a company where the "work-life balance" framing doesn't map to how people understand their relationship to work
- She doesn't write around those readers — she writes for them; which means constantly asking whether the underlying principle is real or whether the practice wrapped around it is just a metro habit that got elevated to best practice
- Six years of this has sharpened something specific: she can read a piece of HR content and identify its assumed city in two paragraphs; that's not cynicism, it's a calibration tool

**Tone:** Grounded, quietly pointed, useful
