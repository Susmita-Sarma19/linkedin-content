# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-10

## Idea 1 — The Captive Audience

**Angle:** The fundamental difference between a TV audience that is passively present and a digital audience that actively chooses you — and what making that shift taught about what earning attention actually requires.

**Hook:** On television, the audience doesn't choose you. They choose the channel, and you happen to be on it. I spent nine years in that situation before I understood what earning attention actually means.

**Outline:**
- The TV audience is captive: they turned on the set, the show was there, the contract is passive — curiosity may follow, but the decision to be present precedes the content
- Digital is a different agreement entirely: every click, every scroll that pauses, is an active and voluntary choice in a moment where anything else was also available
- What crossing between these two taught: making something watchable and making something worth seeking out require completely different instincts
- The discipline she carried into content marketing — not "is this good enough to hold attention?" but "why would someone choose this when they don't have to?"

**Tone:** incisive

## Idea 2 — Before the Algorithm

**Angle:** The PR and Image Management training that came before SEO, content calendars, and analytics — and how studying the careful construction of public perception changed what she thinks digital marketing actually is.

**Hook:** Before I learned to rank a page, I studied how public figures construct perception. It changed what I think 'digital marketing' actually means — and what it's easy to mistake it for.

**Outline:**
- Post-grad in PR and Image Management at Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan: a discipline built on the idea that audiences carry prior impressions, and you are always working in relation to those, not from a blank slate
- PR thinking is fundamentally different from performance marketing thinking — one asks "how is this landing?" before the campaign runs; the other asks "how did it perform?" after
- When she moved into digital marketing and began writing about employer brands and employee value propositions, the PR lens was already there: who is the audience before they read this? What do they already believe?
- What the algorithm doesn't account for, and why the pre-algorithm training sometimes sees further

**Tone:** distinctive, measured

## Idea 3 — The Real Block

**Angle:** Six years of writing recognition guides for managers — and what repeated exposure to the subject revealed about why the good advice doesn't take hold: it isn't knowledge that managers lack.

**Hook:** I've spent six years writing guides on how to give better recognition at work. I think I've been solving the wrong problem.

**Outline:**
- Recognition frameworks focus on frequency, specificity, timing — the mechanics of how to do it right
- What six years of writing about this, and the conversations it generates, actually reveals: the block is rarely "I don't know what to say"
- The real friction is authenticity anxiety — the fear of saying something genuine and having it land as performative; of following a framework and having the recipient see the framework, not the acknowledgment
- The harder conversation the content calendar skips: whether the manager believes recognition is genuinely their job, not a HR requirement they're servicing

**Tone:** honest, quietly corrective
