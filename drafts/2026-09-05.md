# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-05

## Idea 1 — The Audience Problem

**Angle:** A TV viewer can change the channel. An employee can't — and that asymmetry is what makes employee engagement genuinely hard, not just a box to check.

**Hook:** A TV viewer can change the channel. An employee can't — or at least, that's what we tell ourselves.

**Outline:**
- When you host a show, you earn the audience's attention every single minute. Lose them and the ratings tell you by Monday.
- In employee engagement, there's no channel to flip to. People show up, sit through the all-hands, nod at the survey link. That's not engagement — that's attendance.
- The real parallel to broadcasting: you can't mandate engagement. You have to earn it, the same way. With relevance. With honesty. With something that actually matters to the person in front of you.
- What changes when you stop treating engagement as a program and start treating it as a conversation you have to keep deserving.

**Tone:** Provocative-then-grounded

## Idea 2 — The Benchmark Trap

**Angle:** The content industry benchmarks against metro case studies and international data. But the most useful benchmark is simpler: does this actually work for the people you're trying to reach?

**Hook:** Every article I read told me good content gets X% CTR. None of them were written for an audience like mine.

**Outline:**
- Early in digital marketing, I spent a lot of energy measuring myself against benchmarks from brands with ten times the budget and audiences built in metros I'd never worked in.
- The numbers made me feel behind. The work, weirdly, was going well.
- The disconnect taught me something about how we define "good" in content — and whose definition we default to without asking.
- There's a version of this that applies to careers too. The benchmarks we use to measure our own progress are often borrowed from someone else's context entirely.

**Tone:** Quietly subversive

## Idea 3 — What Silence Actually Means

**Angle:** On live television, silence is a technical failure. In a corporate meeting, it often carries more meaning than everything said out loud — and it took her a while to stop misreading it.

**Hook:** On live TV, silence is a crisis. I carried that instinct into corporate rooms for longer than I should have.

**Outline:**
- In broadcasting, dead air is the thing you train yourself never to allow. Fill the gap. Keep it moving. Silence means something broke.
- Early in corporate life, I did the same thing — rushed to fill pauses in meetings, read hesitation as disagreement, answered questions that hadn't been asked yet.
- Eventually I learned: silence in a room can mean someone is thinking. Or processing. Or deciding whether to trust you. None of those need to be interrupted.
- The skill from TV that actually transferred: not filling silence — it was reading the room. That part took longer to unlearn and re-learn in a new context.

**Tone:** Reflective, specific
