# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-21

## Idea 1 — The Quiet Side of the Mic

**Angle:** Nine years of asking questions on television didn't prepare her to get someone else ready to answer them — and what that gap reveals about the difference between performing and producing.

**Hook:** The hardest interview I've ever had to prepare for was one I wasn't going to give.

**Outline:**
- As a host, she owned the room — could redirect, absorb silence, reshape the conversation mid-flight without anyone noticing
- As a podcast producer, her job is to hand that room to someone who hasn't been in it before
- Getting a guest ready means entering their uncertainty, not managing it from a position of control
- What she's learned about communication from the side that doesn't speak — and why it's a different education than anything broadcasting gave her

**Tone:** Quietly revelatory

## Idea 2 — The Geography of Credibility

**Angle:** What it costs — and what it quietly builds — to establish professional credibility in a field that implicitly orients itself around cities you don't live in.

**Hook:** Early in my career I noticed that certain professional rooms expected to be surprised by someone from Guwahati. I spent a long time deciding what to do with that.

**Outline:**
- Not outright exclusion — something more specific: the slight recalibration that happens before you've said anything, when the city you're from doesn't match the city the room assumed
- What you start doing, almost unconsciously, when your baseline expectation is that you'll need to earn the room twice
- Whether that pressure produces something useful — and the honest answer, which is: yes, and also that's not the point
- What changes when you stop explaining where you're from and start speaking as if the room should catch up

**Tone:** Measured, clear-eyed

## Idea 3 — Writing the Answer to Your Own Question

**Angle:** Six years writing about what employees need to feel valued at work — while being an employee — and what it does to your thinking when you are also the subject of your own research.

**Hook:** At some point I realized I'd written the answer to my own question dozens of times and had only partially applied it to myself.

**Outline:**
- The useful fiction of the "objective practitioner" — you write from the outside so the analysis doesn't get personal, and the distance is part of what makes the writing legible
- But working in HR tech, as an employee, makes you unavoidably the subject of what you study
- The moments when insider knowledge sharpens the work: you can feel when a framework misses something because you've lived the miss
- What honest practitioner writing actually requires — the willingness to include yourself in the argument, which is harder than it sounds when the argument is your job

**Tone:** Honest, a little discomforting
