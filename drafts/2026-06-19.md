# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-19

## Idea 1 — The Listening Job

**Angle:** Fifteen years of professional interviewing — first on television, then on podcast — and the skill that mattered most turned out to be the one she never practiced on purpose.

**Hook:** I've conducted hundreds of interviews across two careers. The thing that improved the most wasn't my questions. It was what I did after I asked them.

**Outline:**
- Talk show hosting trains you to ask — the question is the product, the thing that moves the segment forward. You learn to fill silence, bridge topics, keep energy up.
- Producing a podcast changes the equation. You're no longer performing alongside the guest; you're trying to get them to say the true thing, which usually means getting out of the way.
- The shift from asking to listening wasn't a technique she learned — it arrived slowly, from watching what happened when she stopped preparing her next question and actually heard the answer.
- What this transferred to: writing HR content means asking what the practitioner actually needs, not what the brief assumed they needed. The content that lands best usually started with a quieter kind of attention.

**Tone:** Precise, understated

## Idea 2 — The Same Idea, Four Times

**Angle:** Being multilingual isn't just a communication advantage — it's the experience of thinking the same concept differently depending on which language holds it.

**Hook:** I don't translate between Assamese and English. I switch entirely. And "employee recognition" doesn't mean the same thing in both.

**Outline:**
- In English, employee recognition is a process term — specific, measurable, tied to outcomes. That's the frame the research uses, the frame her content is written in.
- In Assamese, the same concept is embedded in relationship norms — acknowledgment as something owed within a structure of mutual obligation, not a program you implement.
- Four languages means four frameworks for the same idea. The work of HR content is usually to translate across the English frame. She notices when that frame doesn't quite fit.
- What this means practically: the assumption that "best practices" are culturally neutral is something she can't fully make herself believe. The concept arrived in a particular language, with that language's priorities baked in. That's worth knowing — especially when the audience isn't monolithic.

**Tone:** Intellectually alive, quietly provocative

## Idea 3 — The Woman in the Room

**Angle:** What it actually looks like to navigate professional spaces as a woman from Northeast India — not a triumph arc, not a victim narrative, but a grounded account of what she's learned to do with the data.

**Hook:** For most of my career, I have been the only person from Northeast India in the room. Sometimes I was the only woman too. I've learned to treat both as information, not invitation.

**Outline:**
- The invitation to make it a story — either of struggle or of overcoming — is always available, and she's mostly declined it. Both versions flatten the experience.
- What she noticed instead: being the only one of something in a room makes you attentive to assumptions. Who the default is built for. What gets taken as given. These are useful things to see clearly.
- Building a career in Guwahati meant building it outside the geography that most professional networks are organized around — the rooms in Delhi and Bangalore where the people who run things know each other. It required becoming useful enough that geography stopped mattering on its own.
- What she'd say to someone starting now: the fact that the room wasn't built for you is real data about the room, not about you. Knowing the difference — and keeping it clear under pressure — is the actual work.

**Tone:** Steady, candid, unflinching without being heavy
