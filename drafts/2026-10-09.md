# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-10-09

## Idea 1 — The Audience I Could See

**Angle:** She made content for nine years for an audience she could physically encounter — neighbors, market vendors, relatives. Now she writes for HR practitioners she'll never meet. The shift from a known audience to an imagined one changes the craft more than any style guide.

**Hook:** For nine years, my audience could stop me at the fish market on a Saturday to tell me what they thought.

**Outline:**
- On Doordarshan, feedback was immediate and personal — her viewers were her neighbors, the women at the market, people at the same weddings. The responsibility was specific because the audience was visible.
- In a regional language, for a regional audience, you don't need to imagine the reader. You already know what lands and what doesn't. It's instinctive in a way that formal audience research never quite replicates.
- Writing for global HR practitioners requires a different kind of craft: building a reader in your head — her organization, her manager problem this quarter, what she's tired of reading — and writing to that construction.
- Both modes produce good content. But knowing you've switched between them, and what each one demands, is its own professional education.

**Tone:** Reflective, precise

## Idea 2 — Why I Never Left

**Angle:** Building a full career without relocating from a Tier-2 city is not the absence of ambition. It's a different definition of it — one that trades network breadth for depth of work.

**Hook:** Every few years, someone asks me when I'm moving to Delhi. I've never been sure what the question is really asking.

**Outline:**
- The assumption inside the question: that career growth has a geographical direction. That being near the industry center means being near the work. These are not always the same thing.
- What staying in Guwahati actually gave her: six years on the same team, the same product, the same recurring problems — building real depth instead of refreshing the resume every two years.
- What staying required: deliberate effort to stay current without being at the conferences, in the offices, in the cities where the loudest conversations happened. Staying is not passive.
- She's not making a case against Delhi. She's making a case for being intentional about where you do your deepest work — and for not treating relocation as the default evidence of seriousness.

**Tone:** Quietly assertive, grounded

## Idea 3 — When Recognition Is Your Job

**Angle:** She's written about employee recognition for six years. What does the person who writes about recognition notice when she herself receives it — or doesn't?

**Hook:** I have written about employee recognition approximately eight hundred times. I still notice when no one mentions a piece I put everything into.

**Outline:**
- There's a strange doubling in being the expert and the subject at the same time. She knows the research on what makes recognition land. She also knows, without needing the research, when it didn't.
- What she's noticed: the recognition she actually remembers isn't usually the formal kind. It's the colleague who sent one specific message after reading a piece. The manager who quoted something she wrote in a meeting because it had become how they actually thought.
- Writing about recognition for a living gives you a particularly calibrated sensitivity to its absence — not from entitlement, but from six years of understanding exactly what the thing does and doesn't do.
- Producing the content about what matters doesn't protect you from needing it. It just makes you more precise about what kind of thing you're missing.

**Tone:** Honest, a little wry, human
