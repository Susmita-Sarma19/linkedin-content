# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-08-13

## Idea 1 — The Image Management Diploma

**Angle:** The least talked-about item on her CV — a PR and Image Management diploma from Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan — gave her a precise vocabulary for the gap between how something is and how it's perceived. That vocabulary isn't cynicism. It's the whole job, honestly named.

**Hook:** "Image management" is the phrase most people avoid. It sounds like manufacturing something you're not. That's exactly why I stopped avoiding it.

**Outline:**
- Why "image management" makes professionals uncomfortable — the implicit accusation it carries (that you're crafting perception rather than being authentic) — and why that discomfort is based on a misreading of what the discipline actually does
- What the Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan diploma actually taught: the gap between how an organization operates and how it's understood is structural, not ethical. You can close it through honesty or through performance. The diploma taught her to tell the difference.
- How that thinking runs through her HR content work six years later: every recognition program is a form of organizational image management — an attempt to communicate values through action. Done well, it's alignment. Done poorly, it's decoration. She's been writing about that distinction for years without always naming the vocabulary it came from.
- The question she's been sitting with: most of the content world has replaced "image management" with "personal branding." The words feel friendlier. She's not sure they're doing the same work.

**Tone:** Quietly provocative — the kind that names something people sense but haven't said aloud

## Idea 2 — The Global Audience From Here

**Angle:** She's spent six years writing HR content for managers and employees across fourteen countries — from Guwahati, a city most of that audience can't locate on a map. The distance isn't the problem she thought it would be. It taught her a precision about human universals that proximity might have cost her.

**Hook:** My readers are in Toronto, Singapore, Bangalore. I'm in Guwahati. I've been writing for them for six years. The gap I expected to be the problem turned out to be the method.

**Outline:**
- What it means to write about workplace culture for an audience you can't observe, in contexts you haven't inhabited, without defaulting to abstraction — the specific discipline of making something useful without making it generic
- What living outside the metro corridor taught her about her audience: she's never been in a WeWork. She's never done a performance review in a glass-walled office. That absence forced her to write from principle rather than from scene-setting — which turns out to travel better than scene-setting does
- The thing that surprised her most about writing across geographies: the human part of the content — what people need to feel seen at work, what recognition looks like when it's real — is more consistent across contexts than the industry language suggests. The structures differ. The feelings don't.
- What Guwahati gave her that a metro base might not have: enough distance from the dominant professional culture to notice what was local convention and what was actually universal. That distinction matters if you're trying to say something that lands in fourteen countries.

**Tone:** Grounded, globally specific — confident without overreaching

## Idea 3 — The Longest Subject

**Angle:** She has been writing about the same topic — employee recognition and engagement — for over six years. Long enough to have used up the obvious takes, the counterintuitive ones, and most of the nuanced middle ground. What's left is harder to write and more worth saying.

**Hook:** I've been writing about the same subject for six years. I ran out of easy takes around year two. I ran out of the counterintuitive ones by year four. I didn't know what that left until I kept going.

**Outline:**
- What happens to a writer who stays in one topic long enough: the first phase is breadth (all the angles, all the research, all the adjacent ideas). The second is depth (the same things from closer in). The third is something harder — you start to see where the field itself is avoiding something, which is different from not knowing about it
- The specific thing she sees now that she couldn't see at year one: employee recognition research is excellent at measuring outcomes and almost silent about what recognition actually feels like from the inside. The gap between the metric and the felt experience is where almost everything interesting lives — and it's also where most content, including hers for a while, stops short
- What staying in one subject long enough reveals that switching subjects can't: the difference between a trend and a shift, the arguments that keep returning in different clothes, the questions the field can't agree on even after a decade — those only become visible if you're still there when the conversation comes back around
- The part she hasn't figured out yet: after six years, the subject she writes about most confidently is not the most important thing happening in it. The most important thing is the one still too complicated to write well. She's working on it.

**Tone:** Reflective, writer-specific — honest about what long-form expertise actually costs
