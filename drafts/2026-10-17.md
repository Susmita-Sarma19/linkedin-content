## Idea 1 — The Degree Nobody Expected

### Hook
My undergraduate degree is in commerce. Not journalism. Not mass communication. Commerce.
Most people who know my work assume the reverse.

### Angle
The typical HR content communicator comes through journalism or humanities and spends years building business literacy on the job — learning what "ROI" actually means to the person who has to justify the recognition program budget, understanding what an organization is actually trying to optimize for when it launches a "culture initiative." She started with that foundation and layered communication on top of it.

For the first half of her career, the commerce degree felt like a detour — she left it for mass communication because business felt like the wrong frame for what she wanted to do. Now she reads it differently. When she writes a business case for employee recognition, she isn't translating practitioner language into business language: she knows both, because she built them in opposite sequence from most people in her field. The unusual education isn't a qualifier. It's the explanation.

### Source
Bachelor's degree, Business/Commerce, Business Management, Gauhati Commerce College (2007–2012) — five years of undergraduate education, completed before her Master's in Mass Communication at Gauhati University (2012–2014) and her PG Diploma in PR/Image Management (2014–2015). This credential appears on her LinkedIn profile and has never been explored across any prior draft or the ideas bank. It is the beginning of her formal education, and no draft has asked what that sequence — commerce first, then communication — actually gave her.

### Resonance
For HR content professionals and B2B communicators: the education that felt like a wrong turn is often the one that explains what you do unusually well. The person who came through commerce before communication isn't a diluted version of a communications specialist — they're a communicator with a business foundation most practitioners in their field acquire slowly, through proximity to people who already have it. That sequence rarely gets named, because the degree you lead with is never commerce.

## Idea 2 — The Language Beneath the Language

### Hook
I have written roughly two million words in English for work.
The thoughts that became those words started in Assamese.

### Angle
She is fluent in four languages. Professionally, she works in English — all published content, all client-facing communication, the podcast, the blog. But the internal process isn't in English. The idea arrives in Assamese first, gets shaped and tested there, and is then translated into the professional register. This isn't a translation problem — her English is fluent. It's a register problem. The language that holds the original thought isn't the language of the finished piece.

The ideas bank frames four languages as an advantage: sharper listening, a wider reader model, better instinct for tone. That's true and incomplete. The other thing four languages means is that you're always one remove from the most honest version of the sentence. The most precise thing she has ever written about whether recognition makes someone feel seen probably existed in Assamese first. What published is a translation. Something stayed behind.

She hasn't resolved this. But after years of professional writing almost entirely in English, it's worth naming — what it means to think in one language and work in another, every day, without the gap ever closing.

### Source
Assamese as first language; Bengali, Hindi, English all at full professional proficiency. Professional content work conducted entirely in English across six-plus years at Vantage Circle. Distinct from "The Language Advantage" in the ideas bank (multilingualism as professional asset) and from any prior draft — none has examined the experience of doing sustained professional cognitive work in a language that is not the language of thought. The specific detail is biographical: Susmita is from Guwahati, Assam; her first language is Assamese; her professional output is English. That gap exists every working day.

### Resonance
For multilingual professionals working in a non-first-language professional environment: the fluency is real, and so is the distance. What lands in the published piece is the version that survived translation, not the original. Most multilingual professionals have never tried to name what stays behind in the switch — it becomes background after a while, no longer visible as a cost. It's worth naming, even without a resolution.

## Idea 3 — What I Came Back To

### Hook
I left Guwahati in 2015 for Kolkata.
In 2017, I came back. Nobody treats the return as the interesting part of the story.

### Angle
When she moved to Kolkata in 2015, it followed legible professional logic — expand the geography, access larger markets, build a bigger career. Two and a half years in: a coordination role at a regional development office, then a media relations role at a national ad-placement company. Both careers visible, both growing.

She came back in 2017. That's where most professional narratives soften, or end with "personal reasons."

The honest version: what she came back to was real. A broadcaster she could return to on screen. A company already building from Guwahati — Vantage Circle — that needed exactly her skillset. Within two years of returning, she had the career she'd been building in parallel in both cities: digital marketing at a growing HR tech company, nine more years ahead on Doordarshan. None of it required staying in the metro. All of it required coming back.

The counter-narrative to "you have to leave to grow" isn't that you should stay. It's that coming back can be the decision, not the fallback. For her, the return was the hinge. Everything since happened from here.

### Source
Returned to Guwahati from Kolkata in approximately late 2017, following roles at Subway Systems India (Kolkata, March–July 2015) and releaseMyAd.com (Kolkata, August 2015–July 2017). Joined Vantage Circle, Guwahati, in September 2019. Continued at Doordarshan Kendra through December 2022. The decision to return — and what it unlocked — is entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. The September 13 draft ("The Room Where Home Was a Market") examines what it was like to be in the Kolkata office making decisions about her home region; this is specifically about the return itself, as a professional choice, and what it made possible.

### Resonance
For professionals from smaller cities or non-metro geographies facing the choice of staying in a metro or returning home: the return rarely gets framed as a career decision. It gets treated as a personal or lifestyle choice, which flattens what it actually takes and what it can produce. For Susmita, the return isn't the quiet chapter before things picked up — it is when things picked up. That distinction is worth making clearly, because the framework that says ambition requires a metro is wrong for some people, and the person who came back and built something has a specific right to say so.
