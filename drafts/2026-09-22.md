# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-22

## Idea 1 — The Invisible Audience

**Angle:** She writes HR content for practitioners, but the people her work is actually about — employees — will never read it. What it means to hold an audience you can't measure as the real accountability.

**Hook:** I write for HR managers. But the person I'm always thinking about doesn't read my articles.

**Outline:**
- Her B2B content audience is practitioners — managers, HR leads, business owners — but the subject of every piece is the employee experience: whether people feel seen, valued, not invisible
- This creates a layered accountability: earn the practitioner's attention in order to reach, indirectly, the worker they manage
- The measure of whether the content worked isn't the page view — it's whether a manager does something different next Tuesday because of something they read
- That outcome never shows up in any dashboard; she writes toward it anyway, because the belief that animates the job is that what happens at work matters to the people inside it

**Tone:** Purposeful, precise

## Idea 2 — The Language You Don't Translate

**Angle:** Four languages, and the discovery that certain professional realities only exist fully in one of them — code-switching isn't just vocabulary, it's about what you're capable of noticing.

**Hook:** Some things I understand first in Assamese. They translate. Some things only exist in Assamese. They don't.

**Outline:**
- Four languages, one professional register: English is where the work lives on screen, but it's not where everything gets understood first
- Code-switching isn't just finding the right word — certain workplace dynamics, certain textures of team trust or low-grade disrespect, are named more precisely in one language than another; English often generalises where Assamese is specific
- What this produces professionally: a persistent awareness that the "universal" vocabulary of work was built in someone else's first language; the frame carries assumptions the speaker may not notice and the listener feels without being able to name
- And the gift of it: when you've operated in four registers, you read a piece of communication for which audience it's actually built for — and which ones it's not quite reaching

**Tone:** Curious, quietly unsettling

## Idea 3 — The Benchmark Problem

**Angle:** Building a digital marketing career from a Tier-2 city means benchmarking yourself against industry standards on a screen, not against the people in the next desk — which changes ambition in ways that are hard to evaluate.

**Hook:** I never had a peer group to benchmark myself against. I'm still not sure whether that was a problem or an advantage.

**Outline:**
- The standard career development story assumes proximity — a peer group in the same city, the same kind of room, people running the same race at the same time; Guwahati doesn't provide that for digital marketing
- Her benchmarks were set by industry case studies, conference talks, newsletter writing from people in Bengaluru and Mumbai — a selection that shows the best outcomes, not the median room, and comes with resource bases she didn't always have
- Six years at one company in one city, building depth instead of breadth: it doesn't look like the career that gets celebrated on LinkedIn, where the winning moves involve new titles and new companies every two years
- The Northeast professional making benchmarks from what the internet says the industry looks like: that's not a perfect proxy. Neither is the metro peer group. The question is which distortion you can actually see and which one you can't.

**Tone:** Honest, a little wry
