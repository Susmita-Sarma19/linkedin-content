# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-22

## Idea 1 — The Last Show

**Angle:** The Doordarshan exit had no ceremony. By December 2022, the transition had already happened quietly — the official end was reality catching up to something that had settled.

**Hook:** In December 2022, I hosted my last show at Doordarshan. I didn't announce it. There was nothing to announce. You don't always know the thing is ending until after it ends.

**Outline:**
- Nine years and five months, running simultaneously with Vantage Circle for the last three — by the end, one had become the center of gravity and the other something she was completing rather than building
- The last show wasn't marked: no farewell broadcast, no final episode framed as such — the chapter closed the way most long professional relationships actually close, with a decision rather than a ceremony
- The "she left TV for digital" narrative is backward — by December 2022, the transition had already happened; the official end was just reality catching up
- The difference between stopping because you're finished and stopping because you're ready: those two feelings are different, and it took until after to understand which one it was

**Tone:** Quietly completing, honest

## Idea 2 — The Observer's Credential

**Angle:** Six years writing about employee recognition from a content role, never an HR role — what that kind of authority is, what it isn't, and why naming the distinction is not undermining but respecting.

**Hook:** Six years of writing about employee recognition. I'm not an HR practitioner and have never been. I used to frame that quietly. I've started being direct about it instead.

**Outline:**
- The content professional who covers a domain without holding a credential in it: the authority is real, and also different from practitioner authority — not inferior, but not the same
- What six years gives: pattern recognition across many organizations, because you're reading the whole field rather than living inside one instance of it
- What it doesn't give: the judgment that comes from accountability — from having the outcome be yours; that's a different kind of knowing, and it's not substitutable
- Naming what kind of authority you hold isn't doubting what you know; it's respecting the practitioner on the other end who has to decide whether to trust it

**Tone:** Candid, quietly confident

## Idea 3 — The 2019 Version

**Angle:** A piece she wrote in year one at Vantage Circle is still ranking and still being read — the experience of watching your earlier self keep reaching new readers, representing a version of you that six years have moved past.

**Hook:** A piece I wrote in 2019 is still being read. An HR manager somewhere opened it this week — I can see it in the traffic. They have no idea what I knew then versus what I know now. I do.

**Outline:**
- Year one content: synthesizing research accurately, with the best sources available; the work still ranks and an HR practitioner who finds it today gets 2019's understanding delivered as if it were current
- What she notices going back to read it: not wrongness, but thinness — the gap between program design and lived employee experience was described by the research she was citing, but she didn't yet know it the way she knows it now
- Most professionals' early work recedes; for the content writer, it stays on the page — still ranking, still reaching, still representing
- The temporal distance between who wrote something and who reads the dashboard showing it's still being read: that's a specific accountability the long-career writer carries

**Tone:** Reflective, a little reckoning

## Idea 4 — The Invisible Audience

**Angle:** She writes HR content for practitioners, but the people her work is actually about — employees — will never read it. What it means to hold an audience you can't measure as the real accountability.

**Hook:** I write for HR managers. But the person I'm always thinking about doesn't read my articles.

**Outline:**
- Her B2B content audience is practitioners — managers, HR leads, business owners — but the subject of every piece is the employee experience: whether people feel seen, valued, not invisible
- This creates a layered accountability: earn the practitioner's attention in order to reach, indirectly, the worker they manage
- The measure of whether the content worked isn't the page view — it's whether a manager does something different next Tuesday because of something they read
- That outcome never shows up in any dashboard; she writes toward it anyway, because the belief that animates the job is that what happens at work matters to the people inside it

**Tone:** Purposeful, precise

## Idea 5 — The Language You Don't Translate

**Angle:** Four languages, and the discovery that certain professional realities only exist fully in one of them — code-switching isn't just vocabulary, it's about what you're capable of noticing.

**Hook:** Some things I understand first in Assamese. They translate. Some things only exist in Assamese. They don't.

**Outline:**
- Four languages, one professional register: English is where the work lives on screen, but it's not where everything gets understood first
- Code-switching isn't just finding the right word — certain workplace dynamics, certain textures of team trust or low-grade disrespect, are named more precisely in one language than another; English often generalises where Assamese is specific
- What this produces professionally: a persistent awareness that the "universal" vocabulary of work was built in someone else's first language; the frame carries assumptions the speaker may not notice and the listener feels without being able to name
- When you've operated in four registers, you read a piece of communication for which audience it's actually built for — and which ones it's not quite reaching

**Tone:** Curious, quietly unsettling

## Idea 6 — The Benchmark Problem

**Angle:** Building a digital marketing career from a Tier-2 city means benchmarking yourself against industry standards on a screen, not against the people in the next desk — which changes ambition in ways that are hard to evaluate.

**Hook:** I never had a peer group to benchmark myself against. I'm still not sure whether that was a problem or an advantage.

**Outline:**
- The standard career development story assumes proximity — a peer group in the same city, the same kind of room, people running the same race; Guwahati doesn't provide that for digital marketing
- Her benchmarks were set by industry case studies, conference talks, newsletter writing from Bengaluru and Mumbai — a selection that shows the best outcomes, not the median room, and comes with resource bases she didn't always have
- Six years at one company in one city, building depth instead of breadth: it doesn't look like the career that gets celebrated on LinkedIn, where the winning moves involve new titles and new companies every two years
- The Northeast professional making benchmarks from what the internet says the industry looks like: that's not a perfect proxy. Neither is the metro peer group. The question is which distortion you can actually see and which one you can't.

**Tone:** Honest, a little wry
