# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-22

## Idea 1 — The Inside View

**Angle:** She markets employee recognition software and has been on the receiving end of recognition — the strange loop of selling something you've experienced reveals what recognition actually needs to do, and why most companies get the structure right and the words completely wrong.

**Hook:** I've spent six years writing about why recognition matters. I still flinch when I get a bad version of it.

**Outline:**
- She sells recognition software — which means she's thought professionally, systematically, about what recognition is supposed to do for employees
- She's also an employee, which means she knows what it actually does when it lands and when it doesn't
- The gap isn't in the platform or the cadence — it's in the specificity. Generic recognition tells you that the system is working. Specific recognition tells you that someone was paying attention.
- What "good job on that project" communicates vs. "the way you handled the client on Tuesday changed how they see us" — they are not versions of the same thing
- The irony she's come to live with: companies that invest in recognition infrastructure often solve the delivery problem completely and the language problem not at all

**Tone:** Wry, insider, quietly honest

## Idea 2 — The Staying Decision

**Angle:** She actively chose to build her career in Guwahati — not by default, but as a deliberate trade-off that required professional strategies metro-career advice never accounts for, and produced a version of success that didn't require erasing where she came from.

**Hook:** Everyone who has built a career from a Tier-2 city knows the moment. Someone asks where you're based, and before you can answer, their face does the math.

**Outline:**
- The unspoken premise in most career advice: ambition and staying are in tension. That if you're serious, you eventually go where the opportunities are.
- She went to Kolkata, came back, and chose to stay. Not because options disappeared — because she decided the cost of leaving outweighed what leaving was supposed to give.
- What staying required: building professional relationships in a smaller talent pool, finding community in online spaces instead of industry events, making peace with being outside the rooms where decisions happen for an industry mostly headquartered elsewhere
- What staying gave: depth in a market that wasn't crowded, the company that actually found her, a career that doesn't require pretending she's from somewhere else
- The quiet argument the staying decision makes — and who it's for, and who it isn't

**Tone:** Clear-eyed, quietly defiant, grounded

## Idea 3 — Unmaking Yourself the Voice

**Angle:** Moving from talk show host (you are the voice) to podcast producer/interviewer (others are the voice) required actively dismantling skills she'd spent nine years building — and the professional discipline of making yourself smaller so someone else can be heard turned out to be harder than anything broadcast had prepared her for.

**Hook:** I spent nine years learning to be the voice in the room. Then I started producing a podcast and spent the next six years unlearning it.

**Outline:**
- In a talk show: you are the voice. Guests exist to serve the narrative you're building. Your job is to be interesting, to steer, to know where the segment is going.
- In a podcast: the guest is the voice. Your job is to create conditions in which they can think clearly — and then stay out of the way.
- The specific broadcast skills that actively got in the way: knowing how to fill silence before it gets uncomfortable, steering a conversation back toward your point, making yourself interesting so the audience doesn't leave
- What interviewing for a podcast actually requires: genuine curiosity that doesn't already know the answer. The ability to let a thought go somewhere you hadn't planned. Asking a question because you don't know what they'll say, not because you know what you want them to say.
- She was better at one-way broadcast than she expected. She was worse at interviewing than she should have been. The gap between those two things was the whole education.

**Tone:** Craft-focused, reflective, a little wry about the irony
