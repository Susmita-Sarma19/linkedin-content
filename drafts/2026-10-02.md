# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-10-02

## Idea 1 — The Mentor Who Never Knew

**Angle:** The most formative professional education she received happened at Doordarshan without anyone using the word "mentorship."

**Hook:** My best mentor never called it mentorship. She probably doesn't know I think of her that way.

**Outline:**
- A senior producer at Doordarshan who spent 15 minutes before every recording finding the thing the guest was actually worried about — never the scripted angle, always the unresolved one
- She never framed it as stakeholder management or editorial strategy. She called it homework. She did it every time without exception.
- Susmita runs the same pre-brief before every content briefing now — the conversation that isn't recorded, where the real brief emerges; she learned it through proximity, not instruction
- The education was invisible until she looked back at it — and now she wonders how many people around her are quietly learning from what she does, without her knowing it either

**Tone:** Warm, quietly precise

## Idea 2 — When Recognition Travels Badly

**Angle:** Global employee recognition programs often fail locally not because of execution, but because the framework assumes everyone experiences acknowledgment the same way.

**Hook:** The recognition program worked perfectly in the rollout. Nobody told me why certain teams had stopped using it three months in.

**Outline:**
- She writes recognition content for a living, which means she also sees when programs that look right on paper don't land with the people they're for
- The assumption most global programs make: that public acknowledgment, individual-level visibility, and peer-driven recognition carry universal appeal — they don't; some cultures experience public recognition as isolating rather than honoring
- Her multilingual background gives her an early-warning instinct for when a framework is mono-cultural dressed up as universal; the word for "recognition" in four languages doesn't travel the same emotional distance in each one
- Not a critique of recognition programs — an argument that the programs that actually work were designed to travel, not just translated

**Tone:** Specific, quietly authoritative

## Idea 3 — The Content Nobody Pitches

**Angle:** The most useful HR content never gets approved because it tells the truth about organizational dysfunction — and there's a specific editorial skill in writing around that without becoming useless.

**Hook:** There's a piece I've been turning over for two years. I know it would be the most useful thing I could write. I also know why it will never get published.

**Outline:**
- She produces HR and employee engagement content professionally, which means every piece passes through a filter: what the organization can say, what the brand can credibly claim, what won't create problems with the clients who recognize themselves in it
- The space between "what we can publish" and "what practitioners actually need to know" isn't a gap — it's a chasm; the most honest insights about why recognition programs fail, why engagement surveys don't move anything, why managers disengage before employees do, none of this makes it into thought leadership
- What she's learned to do: write toward the thing practitioners recognize as true without being able to say on behalf of their own company; the piece that feels like she read their mind
- That's the actual editorial skill in HR content — not making things sound good, but making them land as honest

**Tone:** Candid, a little uncomfortable, precise
