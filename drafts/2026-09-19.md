# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-19

## Idea 1 — The Metric Nobody Tracks

**Angle:** She works inside HR tech, surrounded by engagement scores and eNPS dashboards — and the practitioners she interviews keep describing the same unmeasured thing: they could feel the problem before any number said so.

**Hook:** The most useful thing I've heard in six years of interviewing HR practitioners isn't in any of our product dashboards. It's "I could tell something was off before any metric said so."

**Outline:**
- She produces content for an HR tech company — recognition software, engagement tools, measurement systems; the industry's promise is that data makes the invisible visible
- But in dozens of practitioner interviews, the gap she hears most is between what the dashboard shows and what a manager already knew
- The metric that mattered was the one that arrived after the fact; the signal that actually moved someone to act was qualitative, relational, right in front of them
- What this produces as a content problem: she's been writing about measurement for six years while increasingly convinced that the most important capacity isn't measuring — it's noticing

**Tone:** Quietly skeptical, insider-critical

## Idea 2 — The Outside View

**Angle:** She has spent six years researching "the workplace" from Guwahati — a city where most of the professionals she writes for would never locate themselves. The distance isn't a limitation. It's epistemologically useful.

**Hook:** I've spent six years writing about workplace culture. I've never had an office commute. I've never had a watercooler. My entire professional understanding of "the workplace" was built from research, interviews, and controlled distance. I'm not sure I'd trade it.

**Outline:**
- The specific position: producing HR content for a global-ish audience while working from a Tier-2 city in Northeast India, outside the metro professional bubble
- What being near-outside produces — she's read about office culture, interviewed practitioners who live inside it, never had the particular blindness that comes with full immersion
- The metro-centric assumption she regularly has to catch herself making, and what it takes to un-make it: asking the question one layer earlier than is comfortable
- Not an argument for remote work or Tier-2 cities in the abstract — a specific observation about what being outside a system does to how clearly you can describe it

**Tone:** Thoughtful, a little contrarian, precise

## Idea 3 — When the Wrong Thing Got Recognized

**Angle:** The recognition failure she hears most from practitioners isn't "we don't recognize enough." It's "we recognized the visible outcome and missed the person who made it possible."

**Hook:** In six years of covering employee recognition, the failure mode I keep hearing isn't that companies don't recognize enough. It's that they recognize the wrong thing. The metric hit got celebrated. The person who kept the metric from missing got nothing.

**Outline:**
- She's interviewed recognition program designers, HR heads, and managers across industries; the most consistent frustration isn't absence of recognition — it's misaligned recognition
- What "visible outcome" recognition looks like: the award goes to the person whose name was on the deliverable; the person who solved the silent problem three weeks earlier doesn't register
- The distinction between effort-recognition and outcome-recognition — and why outcome-recognition is easier to systematize, which is exactly why it dominates
- What she'd change in how recognition programs get designed: build in a question that surfaces the invisible contribution, not just the one that closed in the dashboard

**Tone:** Specific, practitioner-facing, a little sharp

## Idea 4 — The Receiver Side

**Angle:** In her first month as a television host, she spent her days in villages outside Guwahati asking people whether they'd seen what she was putting on air — and learned early that a distribution number tells you where the signal went, not what happened when it arrived.

**Hook:** In September 2013, I had been a television host for exactly one month. That month, I spent my days in villages outside Guwahati, asking people whether they'd seen what we were putting on air.

**Outline:**
- She joined Doordarshan in August 2013; the next month she was part of an IIMC research campaign studying reach and access of Bharat Nirman advertisements in rural Guwahati
- In village after village: the gap between what a transmitter technically reaches and what people actually receive — she went and asked them directly
- Month one of a broadcasting career installed a question she's never stopped carrying: reach data tells you where the signal went, not what happened when it arrived
- Every piece of content she writes now has that question underneath it — whether the reader it found was the reader it needed to reach

**Tone:** Grounded, quietly formative

## Idea 5 — The Public Broadcaster's Measure

**Angle:** Nine years at Doordarshan — where the metric was access, not revenue — followed by six years in B2B content where success is funnel-mapped. The shift changed what she does. It didn't change the underlying question she asks.

**Hook:** Doordarshan doesn't optimize for ad revenue. For nine years, that was my normal. Moving into a world where every piece of content lives inside a funnel was an adjustment. Not in the direction I expected.

**Outline:**
- Doordarshan's mandate: reach, not revenue — programming into Assam and the Northeast because there are people there, not because the audience is profitable
- The shift to B2B digital content: attribution, conversion tracking, search intent, funnel design — a different model for what content is for
- What she learned to do well in the commercial model, and what she kept from the Doordarshan years: when a piece reaches someone who needed it and the funnel doesn't register it, did it still work?
- She carries the question without requiring it answered — and it's the one that keeps her from mistaking dashboard performance for the whole picture

**Tone:** Reflective, values-grounded, not nostalgic

## Idea 6 — The Room the Research Wasn't Built For

**Angle:** The recognition research she cites was largely built on large enterprises with dedicated HR infrastructure. Most of the people reading what she writes don't work there. Six years of writing across that gap produced a specific editorial instinct.

**Hook:** Most of the studies I cite were conducted in organizations with dedicated HR teams, L&D budgets, and enterprise-level recognition platforms. Most of the people reading what I write don't work there.

**Outline:**
- The recognition research base is real — and largely built on 500+ employee organizations with HR infrastructure, Western settings, digital tools already in place
- The practitioner at a 120-person company, the manager with no platform, the HR generalist who owns "employee experience" as a fraction of their role — they read the same frameworks from a different room
- Every piece she produces passes through a quiet editorial check: does this advice survive contact with an organization that doesn't look like the study sample?
- What six years of writing across the gap produces: an instinct for where frameworks need translation, not just explanation — translation being the harder work

**Tone:** Dry, editorially precise, practitioner-facing
