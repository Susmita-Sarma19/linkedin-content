# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-11-11

## Idea 1 — The Recognition Gap

**Angle:** Recognition programs are designed by the people who would love to receive them — and that's usually the first flaw.

**Hook:** Every recognition program I've seen was built by someone who would have loved to receive it. That's usually where the design goes wrong.

**Outline:**
- Recognition programs assume a universal experience of "feeling valued" — plaques, shoutouts, points, leaderboards — but those formats carry their designer's preferences, not the recipient's
- Six-plus years in HR tech means she's seen the gap: the program gets high participation metrics and low employee trust simultaneously
- Recognition that lands isn't the most elaborate version — it's the most specific one; the difference between "great job this quarter" and knowing what someone actually did and why it mattered
- The question underneath most recognition frameworks: who was in the room when this was designed, and who wasn't

**Tone:** Provocative — sharp but not cynical

## Idea 2 — The Language You Think In

**Angle:** Switching between four languages daily isn't just multilingualism — each one brings a different version of yourself to the room.

**Hook:** I draft emails in English. I think through problems in Assamese. I argue with myself in Bengali. I've never fully sorted out which one is closest to what I actually mean.

**Outline:**
- Code-switching is usually framed as a professional advantage: reach more people, signal adaptability. That's real but it's the surface version
- The deeper thing is how the language changes what you're able to say — not just the words, but the register, the relational distance, what you can get direct about and what requires softening
- Working in HR content, writing for a wide audience, she's carrying this awareness into every draft: who is the reader, what's the relationship, and what register actually reaches them
- Multilingualism isn't just a tool for bigger reach — it's training in the idea that the way you say something is inseparable from what you're saying

**Tone:** Reflective, quietly curious

## Idea 3 — The Invisible Audience

**Angle:** Live TV showed her what an audience looks like. Digital work asked her to believe in one she can't see.

**Hook:** On live TV, the floor manager's signal told me when something wasn't landing. Digital content doesn't give you that signal. At some point you have to decide — before you write a word — that you believe someone is at the other end.

**Outline:**
- In broadcast, the audience's presence is physical: a studio, a floor crew reading the room, a producer's cue. The feedback loop is immediate and human
- In digital, you work with analytics, impressions, engagement rates — which tell you what happened but not whether it meant anything to the person who read it
- The habit she carried from TV: writing as if the reader is in the room. Not performing for a crowd — writing for a specific someone
- The best content she's seen treats the invisible audience as real before there's any evidence. The worst content treats the reader as a metric to move

**Tone:** Warm, philosophical
