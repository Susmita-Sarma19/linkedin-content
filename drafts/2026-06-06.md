# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-06

## Idea 1 — The Archive

**Angle:** The podcast she's been building for years is, without having planned it, becoming a historical record of how HR practitioners actually thought during this decade — most content is made for reach, this one turned out to be documentation.

**Hook:** "I started the podcast to give HR voices a platform. I didn't realize until a few years in that I was also building an archive."

**Outline:**
- Episode 1 was not conceived as documentation — it was made for the listener who needed it that week; what it turned into is something else
- Each episode is timestamped thinking: what HR managers worried about in 2021 is not what they worry about now, and that distance is data
- The difference between content made for reach (optimized for the moment it goes out) and content that becomes more valuable over time (optimized for a listener who hasn't arrived yet)
- She keeps making episodes for the current listener — but she's started thinking about who will open the feed in 2035

**Tone:** Quietly revelatory, long view, unhurried

## Idea 2 — Before the Algorithm

**Angle:** A mass communication degree in the era before content marketing was a job title gave her a theory of audience that predates the algorithm — and that foundation is the thing platform-native learning doesn't include.

**Hook:** "I studied mass communication before content marketing was a job title. The curriculum didn't teach me SEO or scheduling tools. It taught me something harder to find in a course now: why communication works, before you learn how to make it perform."

**Outline:**
- What formal media education actually covers: rhetoric, the history of how messages travel, the structure of trust between a sender and a receiver — the mechanics under the platform, not on top of it
- The algorithm teaches you to optimize for what performs; communication theory teaches you to understand why anything reaches anyone at all — those are different questions, and the second one is slower to acquire
- What she brought into digital work that peers who came up natively digital often hadn't had to think about — not a superiority claim, a structural difference in starting point
- The credential nobody asks about on a marketing brief, and what it quietly contains

**Tone:** Intellectually grounded, quietly authoritative, not defensive

## Idea 3 — The Steady One

**Angle:** Employee recognition frameworks are built around inflection points — new hires, big wins, difficult recoveries. They almost never account for the person who has simply been excellent for years without requiring anything. That person is the hardest to recognize and the most likely to leave without warning.

**Hook:** "The employee recognition research is mostly about moments. New hires adjusting to the role. Top performers at the end of the quarter. Employees who turned something around. Nobody writes much about the person who has simply been excellent for four years and never asked for anything."

**Outline:**
- Recognition infrastructure is built for visibility: the new arrival who needs welcoming, the standout who needs celebrating, the person who struggled and recovered; these are the inflection points the system was designed to catch
- The consistently excellent person generates none of those — they don't surface in dashboards, they don't require intervention, they show up every week and deliver; the system has nothing to trigger on
- What this costs: steady excellence is invisible until the resignation letter arrives, and by then the window to name what they were is already closed
- Six years writing frameworks and the gap she keeps coming back to: recognizing someone reliably requires noticing them when nothing is wrong — and that's the discipline the frameworks don't teach

**Tone:** Perceptive, measured, a little uncomfortable for the reader who recognizes themselves in it
