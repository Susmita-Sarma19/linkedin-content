# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-10-23

## Idea 1 — The Listener's Edge

**Angle:** Nine years in front of a camera taught her that the best communicators aren't the ones who talk well — they're the ones who know when to stop.

**Hook:** Nine years on camera, and the most useful thing I learned was how to shut up.

**Outline:**
- TV hosting means you're always "on" — but the interviews that actually worked were the ones where she stopped filling silence and let the guest breathe
- That restraint doesn't come naturally; it has to be practiced against every instinct to perform
- It transferred directly to marketing: listening to what an audience is actually asking before deciding what to make
- Employee engagement has the same problem — recognition falls flat when it's generic, and it's generic because nobody stopped to listen first
- Real communication starts with restraint, not output

**Tone:** Quietly provocative

## Idea 2 — The Guwahati Lag

**Angle:** There's usually a 6-12 month delay between a trend hitting metros and it reaching Guwahati — and she stopped treating that lag as a weakness years ago.

**Hook:** Guwahati gets everything a little late. I stopped being embarrassed about that a long time ago.

**Outline:**
- When a marketing trend explodes in Delhi or Bangalore, it shows up in Guwahati a few months later — and by then, you can see which ideas actually survived first contact with reality
- That lag forced a different relationship with trends: she watches, doesn't chase
- It's made her better at knowing what to build for the long term vs. what to borrow for a campaign
- The Northeast perspective isn't a disadvantage in a noise-saturated industry — it's a filter
- There's a kind of clarity that comes from being slightly outside the centre of things

**Tone:** Dry, confident, a little wry

## Idea 3 — The Language of Feedback

**Angle:** Switching between four languages at work revealed something she didn't expect: the same feedback doesn't land the same way across all of them — and most managers don't know this.

**Hook:** I've given the same feedback in four languages. It landed four different ways.

**Outline:**
- When you work across Bengali, Hindi, Assamese, and English, you notice that directness isn't universal — what's blunt in one language is completely normal in another
- The word "feedback" itself carries different emotional weight depending on the context it arrives in
- This changed how she thinks about employee recognition and manager communication: the container matters as much as the content
- A recognition message that sounds warm in English can read as stiff in translation — and most HR systems are built entirely in one register
- The real skill isn't finding the right words; it's finding the right register for the person in front of you

**Tone:** Observational, specific, warm with a sharp edge
