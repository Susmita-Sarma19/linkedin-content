# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-08-06

## Idea 1 — The Bengali Middle

**Angle:** She's Bengali in Assam — culturally in-between in her own city — and that in-between position shaped her as a communicator more than being from either tradition fully would have.

**Hook:** I can host a talk show in Assamese. I grew up speaking Bengali at home. Neither of those things fully explains how I learned to move between them — or what that movement made me good at.

**Outline:**
- Being Bengali in Guwahati is a specific kind of cultural adjacency that's harder to name than the outsider/insider binary suggests — not an outsider, not the default, something in between that requires constant translation
- What hosting a Doordarshan show in Assamese as a Bengali speaker actually required: not just fluency, but cultural reading — the register, the humour, what can be assumed and what needs to be said
- When you've spent your whole career navigating a cultural in-between, you stop assuming your audience shares your defaults. That instinct doesn't turn off when you cross into pan-India professional spaces.
- What she'd tell someone who calls her "culturally aware": it isn't a philosophy she adopted. It's a survival skill that became a professional asset. The biography came first.

**Tone:** Specific, unhurried, quietly corrects a misread


## Idea 2 — The Survey She Fills Out Honestly

**Angle:** For six years, she's written content about why employee engagement surveys matter and why you should answer them honestly. Once a year, she fills one out herself. Those two things are not always compatible.

**Hook:** Once a year, I get an employee engagement survey in my inbox. I've spent six years writing about why you should fill it out honestly. I'm still figuring out whether I do.

**Outline:**
- The specific cognitive trap of being the person who writes about psychological safety, then answering questions about psychological safety in your own organization
- She knows what the "right" answer signals, what the honest answer costs, and what the response data will be used for — she holds all three simultaneously when she opens the form
- The year she noticed she was answering to protect the program she'd built arguments for, not to accurately report what her Tuesday felt like — that was the moment the practitioner and the respondent stopped being the same person
- What this revealed: knowing the research doesn't make you a more honest respondent. It makes you a more sophisticated one. That's a different thing. Not always better.

**Tone:** Uncomfortably honest, dry, earns trust by going first


## Idea 3 — The Flood Calendar

**Angle:** She's been building a digital marketing career in a city that floods every year. The logistics — and what they've quietly built in her — don't appear in any HR content she's written about resilience.

**Hook:** Guwahati floods. Not sometimes — reliably, every monsoon season, for weeks at a stretch. I've been building a career in the middle of that calendar for years. Nobody in the 2020 work-from-home conversation thought to ask whether some people were already doing it, by necessity, long before it had a name.

**Outline:**
- What the annual flood calendar does to project timelines, content pipelines, and team availability — and the informal contingency thinking that becomes second nature before you've named it as a skill
- Remote work before remote work: the years of managing deadlines during periods when roads were impassable and power was intermittent built a specific relationship to disruption that the 2020 discourse treated as newly discovered
- "Resilience" when the disruption is annual and geographical, not exceptional — a different operating posture than what most resilience content imagines, because the uncertainty isn't an event, it's a season
- The thing HR content on business continuity consistently omits: the physical conditions of working life in climate-volatile regions, and the invisible expertise those conditions accumulate in the people who work through them every year

**Tone:** Grounded, a little dry, not resentful — just naming what's actually there
