# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-08-14

## Idea 1 — The Room You Can't Feel

**Angle:** Nine years on live television meant Susmita could feel the room in real time — the moment attention shifted, the pause that meant she'd landed something. Digital content replaces that feedback with analytics, months later, depersonalized. What does it cost to work without the room?

**Hook:** For nine years, I could feel when I'd lost the room. In digital, that feedback shows up three months later — as a ranking.

**Outline:**
- What live broadcast feedback actually felt like: not data, but a felt sense — the shift in a guest's posture, the pause that told you a question landed, the quality of silence before an answer that would matter
- What digital feedback looks like in contrast: precise, delayed, depersonalized — time on page tells you something, but not who stayed, or why, or whether the piece changed anything for them
- The specific disorientation of writing for an audience you'll never meet: you develop proxies — detailed reader personas, practitioner interviews, comments on social — but none of them replicate the room
- A reframe she's arrived at: the analytics don't replace the room. They're a different kind of knowing. One tells you what happened. The other helped you shape what you were saying while it was still happening. Both matter. Only one is available to her now.

**Tone:** Contemplative, craft-grounded

## Idea 2 — What the Expert Notices

**Angle:** She writes about employee recognition for a living — the research, the theory, the what-makes-it-work. That expertise doesn't make her less aware of the moments when recognition doesn't land for her. It makes her more precise about exactly what's missing.

**Hook:** I've written hundreds of thousands of words about what it feels like to be recognized at work. The expertise doesn't make the absence easier to carry. It makes it sharper.

**Outline:**
- What you expect when you study something carefully: that understanding it will make you less affected by it. What actually happens when that something is recognition.
- The specific texture of the meta-awareness: knowing exactly what good recognition looks like — specific, timely, tied to the work that mattered — and noticing clearly when the version you receive falls short of the thing you've described in print
- What this taught her that six years of practitioner interviews didn't: the person who built the framework still needs the thing the framework is supposed to provide. Expertise doesn't exempt you from the human condition it's examining.
- A reframe for HR teams designing recognition programs: the colleagues who know the most about recognition are often the ones most sensitive to its absence. That sensitivity isn't a personality trait. It's what happens when understanding and experience run together long enough.

**Tone:** Honest, quietly revelatory — personal without oversharing

## Idea 3 — The First Draft Discipline

**Angle:** Live television trained Susmita to get the sentence right before it left her mouth — there was no edit, no revision, no second take. That discipline made her written first drafts unusually clean, and her relationship to the revision process unusually complicated.

**Hook:** Nine years of live broadcast taught me to get it right before it goes out. Digital content offers a revision function I'm still, somewhat reluctantly, learning to use.

**Outline:**
- What the live broadcast editing process actually is: an internal loop running in real time — phrasing, adjusting, deciding — before words reach the microphone. Not improvisation. A discipline built from the condition of no-take-two.
- How that carries into written work: first drafts that are structurally more sound than most, less dependent on revision to find the argument — and a specific impatience with using drafts as exploration rather than refinement
- The thing the revision process offers that broadcast never did: the ability to improve after the fact, to find a better version of the thing you already said. Her still-incomplete comfort with that freedom.
- What she'd say to a writer moving from broadcast into written work: a draft isn't a failure to get it right the first time. It's a different mode of thinking, one broadcast doesn't teach and doesn't need. Learning to draft exploratively is a skill you have to build from scratch. The live discipline doesn't transfer. You have to decide to let it go.

**Tone:** Specific, observational — craft insight earned from two different media
