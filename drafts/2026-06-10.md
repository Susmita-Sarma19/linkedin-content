# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-10

## Idea 1 — The Partial Map

### Hook
Thirteen years of content work trained a specific kind of judgment. It turns out that judgment runs in one direction — toward the work, not the people doing it.

### Angle
When you spend over a decade as an individual contributor, you get very good at evaluating the work: what a strong draft looks like, where a brief is missing its real question, when a piece is technically correct but hasn't found its point yet. That judgment is real and hard-won. Then you become a manager — and the first thing you discover is that knowing what good looks like doesn't automatically transfer into knowing how to help someone else get there. The instinct of a strong IC is to close the gap yourself: step in, sharpen the thing, move on. The job of a manager is to build the judgment in someone else, which requires a completely different kind of patience and a willingness to hold back when holding back is uncomfortable. She's been Assistant Manager since April 2025. She's still learning the difference between expertise pointed at work and expertise pointed at people. The post doesn't offer a resolution — it names the transition honestly, because the honest account is more useful than another piece of advice about "letting go."

### Source
Promoted to Assistant Manager in April 2025, after over a decade as an individual contributor across five roles. The first explicit people management role in a career built entirely on craft. Completely unexplored across all prior drafts — no previous idea has examined the IC-to-manager transition, the specific dissonance it creates for content practitioners, or the present tense of the shift.

### Resonance
Senior individual contributors recently promoted to management — the single most common career transition and one where almost all the writing is prescriptive rather than honest. The post will land for anyone who has been technically excellent at a craft and then discovered that technical excellence is a partial map, not a complete one, for leading people who do that craft.

## Idea 2 — What We Were Up Against

### Hook
My job for five months was persuading people in the Northeast to open a Subway franchise. I grew up in the Northeast. I knew exactly what we were up against.

### Angle
In early 2015 she spent five months in the Kolkata development office of Subway Systems India, coordinating franchise expansion across Northeast and Eastern India. Her job was to extend a standardized global product into markets where the cultural fit was genuinely uncertain — and she happened to know those markets from the inside. The Subway system doesn't change: the training, the margins, the operational standards are the same wherever you open. What changes is the local equation — what the landlords want, what the footfall patterns are, whether a cold sandwich makes sense in the local food culture, whether the brand carries any meaning in a city where nobody's seen one before. Holding both of those realities — the corporate requirement and the local truth — without flattening either was the actual job. She does the same thing now. Global HR engagement frameworks meet Indian organizations with specific histories, specific skepticisms, specific manager cultures. Translation between what the research says and what will actually work in a room in Guwahati is the hidden work under the content. She didn't know in 2015 that she was learning a foundational skill. She was just trying to get the territory to make sense.

### Source
Coordinator, Subway Systems India Pvt. Ltd, March–July 2015, Kolkata. Development office for Northeast and Eastern India franchise expansion. Five months, entirely unexplored across all prior drafts — no previous idea has touched this role or the specific insider-outsider tension it created.

### Resonance
Professionals working at the intersection of global standards and local markets — consultants, HR practitioners rolling out multinational programs to regional teams, content writers localizing frameworks for Indian audiences. The post names the hidden work of translation without romanticizing it: you hold two incompatible frames and find the overlap. That's not a special skill — it's just what the job actually requires.

## Idea 3 — Writing from One Remove

### Hook
I've been writing about employee experience for six years. I'm not an HR manager. I've never been one. I used to think I should apologize for that.

### Angle
She is not an HR practitioner. She writes authoritatively about employee recognition, engagement, and workplace culture for an audience of people who do those things professionally every day. That position — writing from one remove, claiming a kind of authority she didn't build from direct practice — is something she has never quite named directly. What the position costs: she can describe the research on psychological safety, but she has never felt its absence in a team she was responsible for. She can write frameworks for managers, but she's never been the manager who had to use them when her own job was uncertain. The deficit is real. What the position gives: she reads the research carefully because she can't rely on practitioner instinct. She notices things the practitioners have normalized. She translates between what the literature says and what a manager in a regional Indian company is actually carrying on a Tuesday morning — because she has to, because the shortcut of lived experience isn't available to her. The apology she stopped making was for the distance. The post is about why the distance is part of the work, not a flaw in the credentials.

### Source
Six years of HR and employee recognition content at Vantage Circle (2019–present) as a digital marketer, not an HR practitioner. The epistemic position — writing authoritatively about a domain she observes rather than practices — is entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. Previous ideas addressed the content itself (what recognition should look like, the headwind, the recognition gap) but assumed her authority. This is the first idea to examine the nature of that authority honestly.

### Resonance
B2B content professionals, thought leadership writers, and anyone who writes authoritatively about a domain they don't formally practice — a very common position that is almost never discussed directly. For her HR audience specifically, the post names something they already sense: the best writing about their work often comes from people who are watching carefully from the outside, which is a different relationship to expertise than they have with their own.
