## Idea 1 — The Most Recognized People in the Room

**Angle:** Susmita writes content that tells managers to recognize their teams — but the research she works with also says managers are typically the least-recognized people in any organization. She lives in that gap daily.

**Hook:**
I spend a lot of time writing about how managers should recognize their teams.
I think about that sentence more than I let on.

**Outline:**
- She writes manager-facing content about recognition — how to do it well, what makes it land, why frequency matters more than formality
- The research she works with consistently shows that managers receive less recognition than any other group in the organization — they're the ones expected to give it, so nobody thinks to give it back
- The person most responsible for creating a recognition culture is often the one running on empty from the act of doing it
- This isn't a call for programs. It's a reminder that the system has a structural blind spot that no software solves — and the content we produce mostly looks away from it

**Tone:** Quietly pointed

## Idea 2 — The Word That Doesn't Exist Here

**Angle:** Four languages means four ways of structuring meaning — and four sets of gaps. She encounters this when translating global HR concepts into content for Indian workplaces: "psychological safety," "belonging," "recognition" don't map cleanly onto how Indian professionals actually think or talk about work.

**Hook:**
"Psychological safety" doesn't have a direct translation in any of the four languages I speak.
That's not a language problem. It's a signal about whose experience the concept was built for.

**Outline:**
- She works in four languages — Bengali, Assamese, Hindi, English — and translates not just between them but between the concepts the words carry
- Some of the foundational HR vocabulary she works with (belonging, recognition as a formal practice, psychological safety as a named thing) emerged from research contexts where those words already existed as professional concepts
- In her daily content work, she's routinely writing those concepts into a context where neither the word nor its professional meaning is part of the default workplace vocabulary — the idea has to be built before it can be used
- The friction is real and productive: a concept that has to earn its vocabulary is one you understand more carefully than one you inherited

**Tone:** Intellectually curious, grounded

## Idea 3 — When I Stopped Writing for the Algorithm

**Angle:** Moving from public broadcasting (no metrics) to digital content (all metrics) put Susmita in a sustained tension between her mass communication training and the performance frameworks of content marketing. The piece she's most proud of from the past year didn't perform. The one she second-guessed reached 40,000 people. What that taught her about who she's actually writing for.

**Hook:**
My most-read post last year was the one I almost didn't publish.
My least-read post was the one I was most sure about.
I've been thinking about what that means ever since.

**Outline:**
- Her mass comm training was about the relationship between communicator and audience — attention, clarity, what makes something land for a specific person with a specific need
- Content marketing adds performance measurement to that equation: impressions, engagement rate, shares, search volume, time on page
- The metrics are not wrong. But they measure the audience that arrived, not the audience the piece was for — and in HR content, the piece that reaches 40,000 generalists may matter less than the one that reaches 80 practitioners with the specific problem it named
- She's learned to use the metrics without letting them edit her before she writes — the algorithm is a fact about distribution, not a guide to what's worth saying

**Tone:** Reflective, professionally honest
