# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-23

## Idea 1 — The Language You Draft In
**Angle:** The language you reach for when an idea is still half-formed tells you something about where the work actually lives — and four languages mean four different places it can start.
**Hook:** The first real draft of anything I write happens in Assamese. The published version is always in English. Somewhere between the two, I make choices I don't always notice making.
**Outline:**
- When an idea is still unformed, she reaches for Assamese — the language closest to unprocessed thought, the one without a professional register to maintain
- The translation into English isn't just vocabulary; it's the moment she decides what the idea means in a context designed for someone who might be anywhere
- Something gets gained (precision, reach) and something gets quietly left behind — a texture that English doesn't quite hold
- What multilingualism reveals that goes unsaid: you don't just communicate differently in different languages. You think differently. The choice of language is a choice about what kind of idea this is allowed to be.
**Tone:** Intimate, wondering

## Idea 2 — After the Recording Stops
**Angle:** Three years of interviewing HR practitioners for the podcast has revealed one consistent pattern: the most honest thing they say comes after the recording is off.
**Hook:** The best part of every podcast interview happens after I stop the recording. I've been trying to understand what that means.
**Outline:**
- Consistent pattern across dozens of interviews: the answer that arrives once the mic is off is almost always more specific, more uncertain, more useful than the polished version that preceded it
- The prepared answer is defensible; the post-recording answer is true — and there's a specific professional culture that produces this split
- What HR practitioners are trained to say publicly about their own work vs. what they'll actually say when the transcript isn't running
- What this means for the kind of content that actually helps people — and what it means for her own LinkedIn voice, which is technically always on the record
**Tone:** Observational, gently pointed

## Idea 3 — The Specific Person
**Angle:** The most common failure in employee recognition isn't ignoring people — it's recognizing the role instead of the person who held it.
**Hook:** Generic recognition isn't neutral. After six years of writing about this, I've come to think it's often worse than saying nothing.
**Outline:**
- Recognition that's technically correct but emotionally off: the praise that names the output but misses why it mattered to the specific person who produced it
- The gap between "you did good work" and recognition that names the particular texture of what someone brought — the part that wasn't in the brief
- Why thoughtful managers still get this wrong: recognizing people individually at scale means breaking the template, which feels inefficient and opens you to getting it wrong in a visible way
- What specificity actually requires — not more effort, but a different kind of attention; the kind that notices what's particular about a person before the moment to say so arrives
**Tone:** Direct, earned impatience
