# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-11

## Idea 1 — The Degree Nobody Asks About

### Hook
I have a business degree. Nobody in my current field knows that.
It turns out to be where I learned to think about audiences before anyone was calling it content strategy.

### Angle
Five years of commerce, accounting, and business management at Gauhati Commerce College (2007–2012). Then a pivot: a master's in mass communication, a PG diploma in PR, and a television career. The business degree quietly disappeared from the professional narrative. Nobody asks about it. It doesn't appear in the version of her career that gets told.

But commercial logic — how markets behave, what makes an offer viable, why the same product lands differently in different contexts — was already in place before she learned the word "content marketing." When she moved into HR tech at Vantage Circle and started writing about recognition software, she didn't have to construct a business case from scratch. She already had the instinct for it. She'd been trained to read an industry before she was trained to write about one.

The degree that doesn't fit the story is often the one quietly shaping how you read it.

### Source
Bachelor's degree in Business/Commerce and Business Management, Gauhati Commerce College (2007–2012) — five years of formal commercial and business education that precede her entire media and communications career. This credential appears in her LinkedIn profile but is absent from all prior drafts, which have focused exclusively on the mass communication and media background. The specific effect of a business education on how she later approached content strategy, audience economics, and the commercial logic of HR technology communication has never been examined.

### Resonance
For professionals who've taken nonlinear academic paths: the qualification that doesn't fit the narrative often turned out to be the one preparing you for something the "right" degree didn't cover. For her HR audience: the employee whose background looks tangential frequently carries exactly the cross-domain literacy the role needed — and may not even recognise it as an asset because nobody framed it that way.

## Idea 2 — The Theory That Arrived Late

### Hook
I enrolled in a course on public relations and image management in 2014.
I'd been on live television for a year. The classroom version and the lived version were not the same subject.

### Angle
Doordarshan began in August 2013. The PG Diploma in PR and Image Management at Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan followed in 2014–2015. She was studying the principles of how public image works while actively building one, live, every week.

The classroom version is legible. Image management has frameworks: message clarity, audience targeting, consistency of tone. They're accurate. The practitioner's version is messier. On camera, you don't manage your image by applying a principle — you make a judgment in the moment and then live with it until the next broadcast. The theory arrived after the practice had already started forming habits.

Some of what she learned in the classroom reframed what she'd been doing. Some of it she'd already arrived at without the vocabulary for it. And some of the clean frameworks she set aside — not because they were wrong, but because the experience had already taught her what the framework was averaging away.

What she carries: formal training is useful, but it arrives in sequence with practice, not before it. You study the framework; the practice teaches you where it ends.

### Source
PG Diploma, Public Relations/Image Management, Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan's Rajendra Prasad Institute of Communication and Management (2014–2015) — studied while she was already an active Talk Show Host at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (August 2013 onwards). The specific experience of formal academic training in image and communications management running concurrently with live professional practice in exactly that domain has never appeared in any prior draft.

### Resonance
Professionals who've done formal training in a field they were already practising — and found the gap between the textbook and the work illuminating in both directions. For HR learning and development practitioners: the sequence matters. Training before practice produces one kind of knowledge; training concurrent with or after practice produces another. Designing programmes for one sequence and expecting the other is one of the most common failure modes in professional learning design.

## Idea 3 — The Blog Nobody Assigned

### Hook
I've been producing content professionally for over a decade.
I also have a blog nobody gave me a brief for. I keep them very separate. That turns out to matter.

### Angle
susmitype.wordpress.com. No keyword brief. No content calendar. No performance metric. The audience is whoever finds it.

Six years of HR content production trains something in a writer. It builds the instinct to ask, before you start: what does this need to do, who is it for, what will they do with it. That's professional discipline, and it's genuinely useful. But it can also colonize your thinking — until every piece of writing is first evaluated as an asset, and the brief arrives before the observation.

The personal writing space does something different. It lets the observation arrive first. The topic is what she noticed, not what the quarter requires. The form is what fits the thought, not what the platform recommends. The piece is finished when it feels right, not when the deadline lands.

This isn't a creative-freedom-versus-professional-constraint story. Both are real disciplines. What the unassigned space trains is the instinct she trusts most in professional work: the one that notices something true before asking whether it's useful. You can't build that instinct inside an assigned brief. You can only carry it into one.

### Source
Her personal WordPress blog (susmitype.wordpress.com), running alongside her professional content career at Vantage Circle (2019–present) and before it. Entirely unexplored in any prior draft.

### Resonance
Content professionals, internal communicators, and anyone who writes for a living and keeps something unmanaged alongside it. For her HR audience: the employee who maintains a practice outside work isn't dividing their attention. They're maintaining the part of their attention that returns to work renewed. The unassigned space feeds the assigned one, in ways that only show up if you stop treating them as competing.

## Idea 4 — The Four-Language Filter

**Angle:** Being quadrilingual isn't a resume line — it's a daily editorial test that sharpens what's actually worth saying.

**Hook:** The ideas that survive translation into all four of my languages are the only ones I trust to publish.

**Outline:**
- Working in English professionally while thinking through Bengali, Assamese, and Hindi — code-switching is so automatic it's mostly invisible, until an idea gets stuck in transit
- What happens when a phrase "sounds right" in English but can't be translated without losing something: either the translation reveals the idea was always vague, or it reveals something precise that the English was obscuring
- HR content is particularly susceptible to this — "psychological safety," "employee experience," "authentic leadership" travel badly across languages and that difficulty tells you something about whether the concept is real or decorative
- Publishing in a language that isn't your first language of thought forces a discipline: you have to know what you actually mean before you can commit to how you're saying it

**Tone:** Quietly precise — linguistically curious, not performative about being multilingual

## Idea 5 — The City That Doesn't Make the List

**Angle:** Six years building a career in HR tech from Guwahati — a city that never appears in the "future of work" conversation — and why that distance has been clarifying rather than limiting.

**Hook:** Nobody names Guwahati when they talk about where the future of work is being figured out. I've been doing that work from here for six years. I've stopped waiting to be mentioned.

**Outline:**
- Reading HR thought leadership and marketing content that assumes you're in Bengaluru, Mumbai, or Delhi — the conference circuit, the networking dinners, the office-culture discussions that presuppose a metro context
- What it means to apply those frameworks from outside the frame: you don't have the social pressure to agree with whatever the room is currently agreeing with, because you're not in the room
- The specific advantage of Guwahati as a vantage point — a city with its own working culture, its own relationship to work-life, its own language of professional life — that the metro thought leadership tends to smooth over or simply not see
- The work doesn't require the city. But the city shapes what you notice, what you question, and who you're actually writing for

**Tone:** Dry and grounded — confident without being combative, a little wry

## Idea 6 — The Tuesday Conversation

**Angle:** After six years of writing about recognition frameworks, the single pattern she keeps returning to: the moments that land almost never follow the schedule.

**Hook:** Recognition programs are built on quarterly schedules. The moments that actually land almost never are.

**Outline:**
- Six years of writing the research-backed case for structured recognition: formal awards, peer programs, anniversary milestones — they matter and the data shows it
- The pattern that doesn't fit the framework: the acknowledgments employees remember and repeat aren't usually the ones that were planned — they're a sentence in a one-on-one, a specific observation offered without occasion, something that was true on a Tuesday and said on a Tuesday
- What the scheduled structure misses isn't intent — most recognition programs are designed by people who genuinely care. What it misses is specificity and timing: acknowledgment that arrives because it's earned, not because the calendar said so
- This isn't an argument against programs. It's an argument for what has to live alongside them: managers who notice, specifically, when something is worth naming — and who say it then, not at the next review cycle

**Tone:** Practitioner-warm — the quiet certainty of someone who's read the research and then watched it play out in rooms
