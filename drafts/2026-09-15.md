# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-15

## Idea 1 — The Insight That Wasn't in the Brief

**Angle:** Her prep sheet for a podcast episode is thorough — six pages, a question arc, timestamps. By minute five of the recording, she's abandoned half of it. The guest's real insight is never in the brief.

**Hook:** My prep sheet for a podcast episode runs six pages. By the fifth minute of the recording, I've abandoned half of it. The guest's real insight is never in the brief.

**Outline:**
- She books HR practitioners and CHROs as podcast guests — experts, not performers; the prepared guest gives the packaged answer
- What live television taught her: the real practitioner knowledge sits just below the surface of the official answer, in the aside, the hedge, the "honestly"
- The six-page prep sheet isn't wasted — it earns the guest's trust fast enough that they drop the keynote version and say the actual thing
- The interviewer's skill is not the question on the sheet; it's knowing which unscripted sentence to pull on

**Tone:** Craft-specific, warm

## Idea 2 — The Piece That Didn't Get Written

**Angle:** The most useful editorial judgment she's built over six years is knowing when not to write the piece. That decision is invisible, uncelebrated — and the thing that keeps the rest honest.

**Hook:** The most useful thing I've learned in six years of content work is when not to write the piece. Nobody celebrates that decision. It's invisible. It's also the one that keeps the rest honest.

**Outline:**
- A brief arrives — the logic is sound, the topic real — and something is editorially wrong: the question it answers isn't the question the reader is holding
- Year one you execute the brief; year three you have opinions; year six you negotiate it, or you say clearly what's missing
- The "worth writing at all?" question arrives slowly, after the "how to write better" question is mostly answered
- The piece that didn't get written is invisible in every performance review — which doesn't make it less important

**Tone:** Dry, assured, a little contrarian

## Idea 3 — The Sentence That Starts with "Three Years Ago"

**Angle:** Long tenure at one organization produces a kind of knowledge that has nothing to do with subject-matter expertise: contextual memory. Why a policy runs the way it does, which argument didn't land before the current framing was found. That knowledge lives in people, not documents.

**Hook:** Someone on my team asked why we structure a certain piece of content the way we do. My answer started with "three years ago..." I didn't realize how much of my job lived in that sentence.

**Outline:**
- Contextual memory: the ability to locate a current decision inside its history, invisible until someone asks
- What year six produces that year one couldn't: the "three years ago" sentence that carries institutional infrastructure
- That knowledge doesn't transfer easily — and its loss is slow to notice; the organizational chart doesn't represent it
- Not a sentiment about loyalty: a description of a specific professional asset that doesn't appear on a skills assessment

**Tone:** Reflective, quietly authoritative

## Idea 4 — The Outside Expert

**Angle:** She has spent six years writing authoritatively about HR and employee recognition — and she has never been an HR professional. That outsider position isn't a gap. It's what lets her ask the foundational questions practitioners stopped asking years ago.

**Hook:** I've spent six years writing about HR. I've never been an HR professional. That's not a confession. It might be the point.

**Outline:**
- What an outsider sees clearly: assumed vocabulary, jargon masking muddy concepts, frameworks that make sense in a slide and fail in a real org
- Six years of interviewing practitioners builds a different literacy — shaped by questions, not lived experience
- The editorial question before every piece: "Would someone who's never heard this explain it this way trust it?" — she's always been that person
- The work of not mistaking familiarity with the subject for being the subject; staying in the observer's seat while knowing the material cold

**Tone:** Quietly confident, a little surprising

## Idea 5 — The Year the Second Career Ended

**Angle:** For nine years she held two full professional lives simultaneously — Doordarshan TV host and digital marketer. In December 2022, one of them ended. What happens when half of your professional identity closes cleanly, without failure?

**Hook:** For nine years I had two careers running at the same time. Not a job and a side project. Two full-time professional lives. When one of them ended, I didn't feel relieved. I felt smaller.

**Outline:**
- The specific discipline of holding two careers — what had to give, what didn't, the year-round rhythms
- What Doordarshan gave her that digital can't replicate: live presence, an audience she could feel, no take-two
- The strangeness of closing a nine-year chapter without failure — it ended cleanly, which turns out not to make it easier
- Where she landed: not missing it exactly, but carrying it; how the TV work shows up in every podcast interview she produces now

**Tone:** Reflective, honest, not sentimental

## Idea 6 — The Idiom That Doesn't Translate

**Angle:** She thinks in Assamese at the edges — the proverb that lands perfectly, the idiom that catches exactly what she means. Then she writes in English and something gets left behind. Six years of writing across a language gap has taught her what the translation costs, and occasionally what it earns.

**Hook:** There's an Assamese phrase I reach for when I'm trying to explain how recognition fails at the manager level. The English translation I've found for it is accurate. It's also about 40% as precise.

**Outline:**
- The experience of thinking in one language and writing in another — not a limitation, but a craft problem worth naming
- Where Assamese captures something about people, work, or community that requires a paragraph in English to approximate
- What happens in the compression: sometimes cleaner, sometimes safer, sometimes less true
- The reader she's writing for can't always follow her all the way back to the source; the question she sits with is how close she can take them

**Tone:** Specific, a little intimate, culturally rooted
