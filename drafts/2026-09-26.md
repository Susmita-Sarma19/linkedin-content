## Idea 1 — The Commerce Graduate Who Makes Content

### Hook
I spent five years studying commerce before I ever held a microphone or typed a headline for a living.
Nobody asks about that. Maybe they should.

### Angle
Gauhati Commerce College, 2007 to 2012: five years earning a Bachelor's in Business and Commerce. Not media. Not communication. Not anything that, on paper, leads to a talk show or a content calendar or a podcast.

Then came the pivot — Mass Communication at Gauhati University, then broadcasting, then nine years on air, then six years in digital marketing. The commerce training sits in the background of all of it, unannounced.

But it didn't disappear. It shows up now as an instinct she can't fully attribute to any media school or studio: the reflex to ask what a piece of content is actually for inside a business. Not "what is this saying" — but "what decision is this meant to change, what cost did it carry to produce, and what's the measure of whether it worked." Those are business questions. She learned to ask them before she ever learned to make anything.

Content work often treats the commercial architecture as someone else's concern — the strategy team's, the CFO's, the brief's. The practitioner who spent five years inside that architecture before becoming a practitioner tends to disagree.

### Source
Bachelor's degree in Business/Commerce, Business Management, Gauhati Commerce College, 2007–2012 — the only multi-year credential on Susmita's profile that has never appeared in any draft across the full archive. It predates every media, communication, and marketing role by years. No prior idea has examined what five years of commerce and business education builds in a person who then builds an entire career in media and content — or what that foundation quietly supplies to her current work.

### Resonance
For HR and content professionals who entered their field from an "unrelated" degree: the background that doesn't match the job title rarely goes away. It compresses and resurfaces as instinct. The practitioner who asks why the content budget is what it is, what the conversion rate of a well-written piece should be, or whether the recognition program can justify its own cost of delivery — that practitioner often has a reason, even if they stopped listing it at the bottom of their CV.

## Idea 2 — Buying and Being Bought

### Hook
For two years, I was paid to decide where other companies' advertisements would run.
During the same years, I was one of the voices those advertisement decisions were placed around.
You learn things from that position that neither side teaches you on its own.

### Angle
August 2015 to July 2017: Media Relations Associate at releaseMyAd.com in Kolkata, building media plans for clients across the country. Her job was to match budgets to channels — deciding which publications, which time slots, which reach numbers justified the spend. Newspapers, radio, television. The commercial architecture of Indian media, from the inside of the buying desk.

Simultaneously: still a Talk Show Host at Doordarshan Kendra in Guwahati. Still on screen. Still the talent whose programme occupied the time slots that media planners were deciding were worth a client's money or weren't.

She was both things at once. The talent and the buyer. The voice and the spreadsheet that priced what the voice was worth to someone else's campaign.

That dual position does something to how you understand media. When you've made the decision about where a message runs — and know what went into it, what was measured, what was approximated, what was assumed — you stop reading a broadcast the same way. And when you've been on the other side of that calculation, you stop treating placement as neutral background. It's a decision. Someone made it, with logic that had nothing to do with you, and your voice ran inside it.

### Source
releaseMyAd.com, Media Relations Associate, August 2015–July 2017, Kolkata — building media plans for clients nationally — running simultaneously with Talk Show Host role at Doordarshan Kendra, Guwahati (August 2013–December 2022). No prior draft has examined the media-buying role, its content, or the specific position of holding commercial media planning experience at the same time as being broadcast talent. Distinct from "The Room Where Home Was a Market" (Sep 13, about being the Northeast person inside a metro organization) and all prior Doordarshan/Kolkata ideas. This is about occupying both sides of the media transaction simultaneously — creator and buyer, talent and purchaser — and what that dual position builds.

### Resonance
For content and marketing practitioners: understanding the economics of the thing you're creating changes how you create it. The writer who has sat in the media-buying meeting knows which content decision had nothing to do with craft and everything to do with budget timing. The broadcaster who has built the media plan knows the programme isn't floating free in the schedule — it's a line item in someone else's spreadsheet. That knowledge is not cynicism. It's specificity. It lets you work more honestly with the actual constraints of the business you're inside.

## Idea 3 — The Writing No One Assigned

### Hook
I have a blog I started years ago.
No keyword research. No content calendar. No brief from anyone.
It's also the place where I find out what I actually think before I know how to make it useful to anyone else.

### Angle
The professional content life runs on assignments: audience personas, keyword targets, publication cadence, performance dashboards. Every piece has a job to do, a metric to hit, an approved direction it's going in before the first draft opens.

The blog exists outside all of that.

susmitype.wordpress.com — her own, personal, unoptimized record of writing that started before anyone was paying her to write and has continued through six years of producing HR content professionally. No SEO brief shapes it. No editorial calendar governs it. No performance metric attaches to it.

What happens there is different. Without the optimization structure, she has to locate her own judgment about whether something is worth saying. Without the audience persona, she has to decide for herself whether she's actually said the thing she meant. Without the metric, she can't route the question of whether it worked through a dashboard. The only data available is her own sense of whether it landed — which turns out to be harder, and more useful, than it sounds.

Six years of professional content work can build fluency inside an optimization system. The writing outside it builds something else: the editorial instinct that operates before the brief, the voice that exists under the strategy, the habit of asking what you actually think rather than what the search intent requires.

### Source
Personal blog, susmitype.wordpress.com — listed on her LinkedIn profile. The only personal creative output listed on her profile and the only biographical element across all 124+ drafts that has never been examined. The specific relationship between maintaining a personal writing practice and working professionally in content — what the unassigned writing builds in a practitioner, what it preserves that the brief-driven work can't, and why it continues — is entirely unexplored.

### Resonance
For content professionals who only write to briefs: the writing no one assigned is where the practitioner's own voice survives the optimization process. It's where you discover what you actually believe about a subject before you know how to make it keyword-ready. A practitioner who maintains a personal writing practice alongside their professional one is doing two different kinds of work — and the unassigned kind is often what keeps the assigned kind from becoming hollow.
