# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-28

## Idea 1 — The Scaffolding Problem

### Hook
The hardest sentence I write: "This platform will help your employees feel recognized."
Not because it's untrue. Because recognition is something a person does for another person. Software reduces the distance. It doesn't close it.

### Angle
She has spent six years writing about recognition, belonging, and engagement — the most genuinely human moments in working life — as content and marketing for a company that builds technology to help produce those moments. The platform is real. The research behind it is real. The software creates infrastructure that makes good recognition more possible in organizations where it doesn't happen naturally. All of that is true. What is also true: the moment of feeling recognized — specifically seen for the work underneath the deliverable, not the deliverable itself — is irreducibly human. A platform can prompt it, track it, make it more consistent. It cannot perform it.

The discipline of that negotiation, sentence by sentence, has been her professional reality for six years. The content that works is not the content that papers over the gap. It's the content that names it clearly — this is what the technology does, and here is the thing the technology cannot do, and the gap between those two is where a manager's judgment lives. The content that overclaims produces a specific kind of skepticism in the HR practitioners who read it: they've seen the gap. They don't trust writing that hasn't.

What she's come to understand about her own job: the honest case for recognition technology requires being honest about what recognition technology isn't. That honesty is not the concession — it's the credibility. The practitioners who trust the content are the ones who found the sentence that acknowledged what the platform can't do.

### Source
Six years of content and marketing work at Vantage Circle, a recognition and employee engagement technology company — writing specifically about recognition, belonging, and engagement as both human experiences and as problems that software is designed to help solve. The specific craft challenge of writing about deeply human workplace moments for an HR tech company — honoring the human moment while making the technology case, and what it requires to do both honestly — is entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. "The Recognition Lab" (June 16) examined the theory-experience gap from the employee's perspective. "The Two Jobs of a Single Word" (June 26) examined vocabulary erosion. "Expert Without Credentials" (June 23) examined what six years of content expertise represents. None has examined the HR tech content challenge itself: the negotiation between what the platform does and what only a person can do, and what writing about that gap honestly requires.

### Resonance
HR technology practitioners and content writers in the employee experience space — the people who know the gap between "the platform will help" and "the person still has to show up." For her broader audience: the post names something live in how technology is written about generally — the content that overclaims produces skepticism; the content that names the limit produces trust. Clearest in HR tech, where the claim and the limit are in permanent proximity.

## Idea 2 — The Year Both Jobs Were True

### Hook
In April 2020, one of my jobs required a studio. The other required a laptop.
Both were still running. I was in Guwahati, doing both.

### Angle
March 2020. Lockdowns. The defining professional question of the year was: studio or laptop? She had two answers, simultaneously. Doordarshan Kendra was a state broadcaster with live programming obligations — the camera didn't stop because of a pandemic; the studio found its protocols and kept going. Vantage Circle was a digital-first HR tech company — fully laptop, fully remote, suddenly producing content about employee wellbeing and remote work for an audience that had never needed it more urgently.

She ran both through 2020, 2021, and into 2022. The professional world was reorganizing itself around a single axis: physical or digital, in-office or remote, studio or home screen. She was answering yes to both, in the same city, sometimes on the same day. The irony that lived inside this: at Vantage Circle she was writing about how organizations could take care of their people during the disruption. At Doordarshan she was inside an institution managing the disruption in real time, in a physical studio that had no remote option.

The parallel produced a kind of double clarity. She understood what the digital pivot felt like from the inside — the recognition gaps that open when teams go remote, the human cost of distance, the things that break when presence becomes a screen. And she knew, separately, what it meant to still have a room, a camera, a physical presence required of her, in the same year, in the same city. The future of work she was writing about was not the only future she was navigating. Holding both, she understood each differently.

### Source
The overlap of Talk Show Host, Doordarshan Kendra, Guwahati (August 2013–December 2022) and Digital Marketing Executive / Web Content Writer, Vantage Circle (September 2019 onward), running simultaneously through the 2020–2022 pandemic period. "The Double Life" (June 24) addressed the general three-year overlap — different professional identities and registers running simultaneously. This is entirely distinct: the COVID-specific dimension of the bifurcated careers, in which the physical-vs-digital question that defined the pandemic professional moment had two opposite answers for the same person simultaneously. The experience of producing remote work and employee wellbeing content at Vantage Circle while maintaining a physical studio career at Doordarshan — a double vantage point on the defining professional disruption of that period — has not appeared in any prior draft.

### Resonance
Professionals who held structurally incompatible professional realities during the pandemic years and found that the incompatibility produced an unusual double clarity. For her HR audience: the person writing about the future of work in 2020–2022 while living a bifurcated version of the exact tension that period created has a vantage point that single-career experience doesn't build. Remote work policy looks different from someone who was simultaneously not remote.

## Idea 3 — The Audience That Didn't Know I Left

### Hook
For nine years I hosted a show that real people watched.
I never knew a single one of them. And when I left, they didn't get a notification.

### Angle
Broadcast doesn't give you your audience. It gives you the camera, the rundown, the hours of practice at making a room work on live television — but the people watching, across nine years and five months, stayed entirely behind a screen she couldn't see through. No analytics. No comments. No names. The viewers were real; the relationship was strictly one-directional and strictly anonymous from her side.

When she moved to LinkedIn, the audience became legible. Someone would read a piece and write: this was my Tuesday. Someone would disagree with a specific sentence and explain why. The feedback loop she had never had arrived, and it was genuinely strange — not because she'd been performing into nothing, but because she'd been performing into a populated darkness so long that a face in it was surprising every time.

What she thinks about sometimes: those nine years of viewers didn't follow her to LinkedIn. They don't know she writes here. Some of them may have tuned in for the time slot, years into her Vantage Circle career, and found someone else. There was no announcement, no forwarding address, no notification. The most sustained audience relationship of her early professional life ended, and neither side experienced the ending with any ceremony. The viewers' relationship with the show didn't require her to be in it — the slot would fill. Her relationship to the audience required them to not be knowable. Nine years of that is a specific kind of professional intimacy: deep on one side, structurally absent on the other.

She doesn't think this is a loss, exactly. She thinks it's one of the stranger true things about the work she did.

### Source
Nine years and five months as Talk Show Host, Doordarshan Kendra, Guwahati (August 2013–December 2022) — live broadcast, strictly one-directional audience relationship with no feedback channel — followed by six years of LinkedIn content and podcast work at Vantage Circle (2019–present), where the audience is visible and responsive. "The Room That Talks Back" (June 20) examined the craft and discipline implications of the feedback loop difference. "The Quiet Ending" (June 25) examined the professional identity dimensions of Doordarshan ending without ceremony. "The Invisible Record" (referenced in June 25 source) examined the demonstrability gap. This is entirely distinct: not the craft implications of feedback, not the identity implications of ending, but the specific relational character of a broadcast audience — real people who had a nine-year relationship with her work that was structurally impossible to reciprocate, and who have no forwarding address. The fact that those people don't know she's somewhere else is not a failure. It is just the specific truth of what a broadcast audience relationship is.

### Resonance
Anyone who has worked in a medium where the audience is real but invisible, then moved to one where the audience talks back — and noticed, in the transition, what the old relationship actually was. For her HR audience: the post touches something live in how organizations think about employee voice. The structural difference between communication that can only flow one direction and communication that can't is exactly the gap that recognition technology exists to close.
