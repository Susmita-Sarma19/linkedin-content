# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-28

## Idea 1 — The Other Diploma

**Angle:** Her PR and Image Management training from Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan quietly shapes how she thinks about digital marketing — but nobody in her field has ever asked about it, and the frame it built doesn't show up in any job title.

**Hook:** There's a credential on my resume nobody has ever asked about in a job interview. I use what it taught me almost every day.

**Outline:**
- PR training builds a different frame than marketing does: publics, not audiences; perception, not metrics; image as something that accumulates, not something you launch
- Digital marketing without that frame optimizes for reach and conversion. With it, you also ask: what does this add up to over time?
- The practical difference: a campaign can convert and still erode something. PR training makes you track both.
- Six years of content work later, the credential nobody asks about is the one that tells her when a piece of writing is technically correct but slightly wrong about something harder to name

**Tone:** Wry, quietly precise

## Idea 2 — Both Sides of the Research

**Angle:** She writes about employee engagement and recognition while being an employee herself. The researcher and the subject are the same person. That double position produces a kind of knowledge — and a kind of accountability — that the frameworks don't account for.

**Hook:** I write about employee recognition. I am also an employee. Some days the gap between those two sentences is very small. Some days it's not.

**Outline:**
- Writing about recognition requires understanding it abstractly: frameworks, literature, manager behaviors, organizational signals
- Experiencing recognition — or its absence — is an entirely different data set. Specific, immediate, sometimes wordless
- The friction: when she writes "employees who feel seen perform better," she's writing about a population and also about herself. That's a strange thing to sit with
- What the dual position has taught her: the research is right, and also insufficient. The human version is messier than the model assumes — and knowing that from the inside makes the writing more careful, not less confident

**Tone:** Honest, gently ironic

## Idea 3 — The Research That Wasn't Written About Us

**Angle:** Most of the employee engagement literature she draws on was produced in Western contexts, large enterprises, urban professional cultures. She applies it to HR audiences across South and Southeast Asia. Something is always being translated. Translation always loses something.

**Hook:** I work in HR content. Most of the research I cite was not written about us.

**Outline:**
- "Engagement" as a concept carries embedded cultural assumptions: individualism, comfort with direct feedback, recognition as public visibility, career as personal project
- In Northeast India — and across many South Asian professional contexts — these assumptions shift: collective belonging patterns differ, deference and hierarchy shape feedback dynamics, recognition preferences are not uniform
- Six years at that gap: the literature is valuable and it requires translation, not just citation. Applying Western engagement frameworks directly is not neutral. It has a cost.
- For HR practitioners designing programs in diverse geographies: the framework is a starting point. The people you're designing for are the endpoint. The gap between them is the actual work.

**Tone:** Measured, quietly challenging
