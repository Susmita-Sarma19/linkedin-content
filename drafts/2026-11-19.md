# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-11-19

---

## Idea 1 — The November Spike

**Angle:** Recognition content trends every November — not because continuous recognition culture has arrived, but because companies are sprinting to use it before the year runs out.

**Hook:** Every November, articles about employee recognition go viral. Not because something changed. Because the year is running out.

**Outline:**
- Six years of content analytics show a seasonal spike: recognition content peaks in November–December, flattens in March–April
- The irony: the content being consumed is almost entirely about *continuous* recognition — the kind that shouldn't need a year-end trigger
- What the spike reveals about where organizations actually are versus where the frameworks assume they are
- Writing good HR content means writing for where companies are, not where the best-practice literature says they should be — if you write only for the ideal, you lose the reader in the real

**Tone:** Wry, observational

## Idea 2 — The Word That Doesn't Travel

**Angle:** Having four professional languages means some ideas arrive with texture in one language and lose something real when translated into the language of the meeting.

**Hook:** There's a word in Assamese for the feeling of being unhurried but fully present. I've spent years trying to write that quality into content in English. I still don't know if it arrives.

**Outline:**
- Professional multilingualism isn't just switching languages — it's the experience of some ideas forming most precisely in a language that isn't the meeting's language
- What translation drops: not the fact, but the register, the assumed context, the specific shade of meaning that the word was built to hold
- How this shapes her approach to writing "universal" content — the awareness that every piece of content carries the assumptions of the language it was written in
- What four languages have taught about the limits of any single one: the reader who understands the text may not receive the idea underneath it

**Tone:** Reflective, precise

## Idea 3 — Writing Rooms I've Never Been In

**Angle:** Six years of writing about workplace culture from Guwahati — for readers in offices she's never walked through — has forced a particular kind of practitioner intuition built from distance, not immersion.

**Hook:** Almost everything I've written about workplace culture has been read in offices I've never set foot in. The people I write for work in environments I can only observe from outside.

**Outline:**
- The real constraint: writing credibly about day-to-day office culture without being inside any particular office culture — no metro commute, no conference hallway, no casual observation of the environments she describes
- How she built practitioner intuition without proximity: interviewing, reading reader correspondence and comments carefully, understanding the gap between what pieces she writes and which ones get shared inside teams
- What distance makes possible that immersion sometimes obscures: pattern recognition without the bias of one specific office culture, one city's workplace norms, one company's assumptions about what "normal" looks like
- The Guwahati vantage isn't a gap to apologize for — it's a different kind of observation point, and six years of content produced from it have found readers who needed something that didn't assume they were in the same room as everyone else

**Tone:** Quietly confident
