## Idea 1 — The First Survey

### Hook
In September 2013, my first paid research project was a survey in rural Guwahati.
The question: were government advertisements actually reaching people?
The answer, mostly: no.

### Angle
Fresh out of Mass Comm studies, she was commissioned under IIMC, New Delhi, to survey rural communities around Guwahati on whether Bharat Nirman government advertisements were landing. She was counting yeses and nos — not theorising about content strategy. But that month planted something: a permanent discomfort with the gap between what a broadcaster sends and what an audience receives. She didn't have language for it in 2013. She has spent thirteen years since developing it. Every piece of HR content she writes at Vantage Circle gets held up against the same basic question from that survey: does this actually reach the person it's for? The question is older than her career in marketing, and it comes from a month most people would call a footnote.

### Source
"Research Campaign on Reach and Access of Bharat Nirman Advertisements (Under IIMC, New Delhi)" — September 2013, rural Guwahati area. This is the earliest professional experience in her timeline, predating Vantage Circle by six years and her full-time Doordarshan role by months. Completely unexplored in any prior draft or ideas bank entry. No prior idea touches the origin point of her current sensitivity about content that travels — or doesn't.

### Resonance
For content professionals, HR communicators, and marketers: the foundational question of the field — does this message actually reach its audience? — is one most people arrive at through campaign analytics or A/B tests. She arrived at it through a clipboard and a village. That's a different angle into a familiar problem, and it's grounded in a specific Northeast India experience that rarely appears in content strategy conversations.

## Idea 2 — The Overlap Years

### Hook
From September 2019 to December 2022, I had two jobs.
Not one main and one side project. Two full ones, running at the same time.

### Angle
For three years and three months, she filmed talk show segments at Doordarshan and produced HR content at Vantage Circle — simultaneously. Two formats with different audiences, different registers, different creative demands, different deadlines. Managing the overlap required learning to shift completely between modes: on-camera presence in one room, analytical writing in the next. What she didn't name at the time is that those three years were her most intensive communication training — not a course, not a workshop, but two full careers running in parallel, each making demands the other couldn't meet. When Doordarshan ended in December 2022, the transition to Vantage Circle full-time didn't feel like a pivot. It felt like a graduation from something she'd been living for three quiet years. The pivot is the story people tell. The overlap is where it actually happened.

### Source

The three-year overlap period has not appeared in any prior draft or ideas bank entry. Prior ideas treat the Doordarshan-to-Vantage Circle shift as a career transition; none examine the years when both ran simultaneously — which is where the real formation was happening.

### Resonance
For professionals managing transitions, dual roles, or career pivots in progress: the overlap period is almost never the story people tell. The narrative is always "I left X and joined Y." But significant career shifts are often lived in parallel first, and the overlap years are where the actual calibration happens — invisible while it's occurring, only legible in retrospect. This names an experience many people are living without recognising it as meaningful in itself.

## Idea 3 — Eight Years of Education

### Hook
I spent five years studying Commerce before I decided I was in the wrong room.
Then two years in Mass Communication. Then one in PR.
Eight years to find where I actually belonged.

### Angle
A Commerce bachelor's (2007–2012), a Mass Comm master's (2012–2014), a PR/Image Management diploma (2014–2015): on paper, it looks like someone who couldn't decide. From the inside, each stage was a narrowing. Commerce gave her the analytical frame. Mass Communication gave her the medium. PR gave her the application. None of it was wasted, even the parts that seemed furthest from where she eventually landed. The eight years weren't indecision — they were calibration in real time, ruling out what didn't fit, getting closer to something she could only name in retrospect. That kind of long-arc navigation is uncomfortable while you're inside it. From outside it looks like drift. From the other side, it looks like the only route that could have worked.

### Source
Education section of Profile.md: Gauhati Commerce College (2007–2012), Gauhati University Mass Communication (2012–2014), Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan PR/Image Management (2014–2015). The three-degree educational arc is completely unexplored in any prior draft or ideas bank entry. All previous ideas treat her career from her first professional role onward; the years of educational formation that preceded it have never been examined.

### Resonance
For anyone on a non-linear educational path, or watching someone they care about take one: the indirect route to professional identity is common, rarely celebrated, and usually experienced as failure or stalling while it's happening. A reframe from someone who came out the other side — that the detour was actually navigation — is the thing most people in that situation need to hear, and almost never do.
