# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-20

## Idea 1 — The Two-Month Internship

### Hook
In June 2013, I was a production intern at Doordarshan.
In August 2013, I was the Talk Show Host. Two months apart. The camera stayed the same.

### Angle
She spent her internship on the production side — watching how the floor worked, how a live segment was built, what a host needed to do before the camera went on. Two months of that gave her the structure. What it couldn't give her was what the role felt like from inside it.

When she became the host, the first few episodes were probably careful imitations of what she'd observed. The first month is always shaped by what you watched. But the job doesn't stay an imitation — the role adjusts to the person filling it, and she had to find out what it looked like when it was hers.

What the intern-to-host arc reveals: preparation comes in two distinct forms. One is what you can observe and absorb before you're in the role. The other is only available from inside it. The internship is for the first kind. The first day on air is the beginning of the second. Nine years of the second kind followed.

### Source
Production intern at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati, June–July 2013 (2 months), followed immediately by Talk Show Host role, August 2013 (9 years 5 months). The speed of this transition — intern-to-host in a single month, in the same institution — has not appeared in any prior draft. Prior drafts have examined the TV years from many angles (the unarchived career, concurrent careers, craft differences between broadcast and podcast) but none has examined the entry moment itself: the compressed path from watching how something works to being the one in front of the camera.

### Resonance
For anyone who stepped into a role before they felt ready — and found that readiness is only ever tested from inside, not established from outside. For her HR audience on onboarding and career development: observation prepares; doing is different knowledge. The person who shadowed a manager for a month still has a first day as a manager. What she carried from those two months wasn't readiness. It was orientation. The rest came from doing it.

## Idea 2 — The Franchise Coordinator

### Hook
For five months in 2015, my job title was Coordinator at Subway Systems India.
I had a master's in Mass Communication and two years of television behind me. I took the job anyway.

### Angle
March to July 2015. The Subway franchise development office for North East and Eastern India, in Kolkata. She was a trained media professional — credentials in Mass Comm and PR, two years of on-camera work at Doordarshan — and she was coordinating for a fast-food franchise expansion team.

The role lasted five months. It ended cleanly. She moved to releaseMyAd as a Media Relations Associate and began what would become a career in media buying and eventually digital marketing.

What the Subway months produced wasn't a neat career lesson. It was something more specific: a five-month view of how a completely different kind of organization runs — its operational logic, its communication patterns, its relationship to the brand it represented. When she moved into media-buying at releaseMyAd, she had a frame of reference her peers with only media backgrounds hadn't built. She'd seen the organizational structure on the other side of the rate card. The credentials she carried in 2015 pointed toward media. The Subway role pointed somewhere else. Following the less obvious option for five months produced the specific asymmetry of knowledge she'd use for the next decade.

### Source
Coordinator, Subway Systems India Pvt. Ltd., March–July 2015 (5 months), Kolkata — the development office for the Subway franchise in North East and Eastern India. Entirely unexplored in any prior draft. The biographical anomaly of a media professional with journalism and PR credentials taking a five-month role as a franchise coordinator — and what that organizational detour contributed to her later professional perspective — has not appeared in any form across all available drafts.

### Resonance
For professionals who've held a role that doesn't fit the rest of their resume and have had to work out its logic retrospectively. For her HR audience on career paths and talent development: the nonlinear credential is often the most informative one. The person whose work history crosses sector or function has, by necessity, built perspective that single-track careers can't generate. The anomalous five months isn't noise in the data. It's the signal that doesn't appear in a linear trajectory.

## Idea 3 — The Language You Think In

### Hook
I've been writing HR content in English for six years.
I don't think in English. That gap hasn't closed. I've stopped waiting for it to.

### Angle
She writes professionally in English. Her emotional register is Assamese and Bengali. The concepts she works with daily — recognition, belonging, engagement, psychological safety — she first encounters in the English of research literature, applies in English-language content, and sometimes revisits in Assamese, at which point they occasionally reveal themselves as less settled than they appeared on the page.

The multilingual content professional has a distinct relationship to her own material: the concept that can't survive translation into another language is often a concept that hasn't been fully stress-tested. She's been running that test on her own writing for six years, quietly, every time she steps away from English and thinks about what she just wrote. Some of it holds. Some of it turns into something more honest.

The gap between writing in English and thinking in Assamese isn't a deficit she's managing. It's become an editorial instrument. If she can't understand what she wrote in the language she thinks in, something is wrong — not with the translation, but with the original idea.

### Source
Her daily professional practice: producing all HR content and podcast work in English while speaking Assamese, Bengali, Hindi, and English across different life and work contexts. The specific experience of writing for a professional audience in a language that is not the one she thinks in — and what that internal translation produces as an editorial practice — has not appeared in any prior draft. "The Translation Test" (July 17) examined what happens when HR jargon fails to survive translation to another language, revealing concept design flaws. "The Language Advantage" (ideas bank) examined four languages as a communication advantage broadly. Neither examined the writing experience itself: what it means to write for an English-reading professional audience when your reflective and emotional register is different.

### Resonance
For multilingual writers and content professionals who work in a language that isn't the one they feel in — and who've found that the gap between those two is not only navigable but generative. For her HR audience: the practitioner designing recognition or belonging programs for a multilingual workforce is designing for exactly this gap, whether or not they've named it. The person who has lived it writes those programs differently than the person who hasn't.
