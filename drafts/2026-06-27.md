# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-27

## Idea 1 — The Edit That Didn't Air

**Angle:** What gets cut from a broadcast segment is rarely the bad stuff — it's the too-specific stuff, the thing that was true but couldn't travel. Six years of writing digital content has taught the same lesson.

**Hook:** The best segment I ever recorded for Doordarshan never made it to air.

**Outline:**
- It wasn't cut because it was bad — it was cut because it was too particular for a general audience slot; it assumed a listener who wasn't there
- Every medium has invisible constraints you only locate by hitting them
- In digital content, the same cuts happen: the most precise sentence in a draft is often the first thing to go in a revision
- What you eventually learn to ask isn't "is this good?" but "is this right for this room?" — and those are different questions

**Tone:** Wry, grounded

## Idea 2 — The Institutional Memory

**Angle:** Six years at one company makes you the person who remembers why the current initiative was tried before — and what happened. That knowledge is invisible on a resume and invaluable in a meeting.

**Hook:** I've been here long enough to remember why we stopped doing something we just decided to start doing again.

**Outline:**
- Institutional memory isn't nostalgia — it's knowing the history of decisions that left no paper trail
- What that kind of knowing actually looks like in practice: recognizing a problem by its shape before it's named
- What organizations quietly lose when turnover is high — not just people, but the context that makes decisions legible
- The one condition that keeps this useful rather than obstructive: it has to be offered, not wielded

**Tone:** Dry, observational, a touch ironic

## Idea 3 — The Question Nobody Asks

**Angle:** After years of writing interview best practices for HR practitioners, there's one question she's never seen in a framework that she now relies on most. The meta-position is worth naming: the content writer advising on hiring is not a neutral observer.

**Hook:** I've written about better interviews more times than I've sat in one. That's a conflict of interest worth naming before I say what I actually think.

**Outline:**
- What structured interviews are built for — consistency, bias reduction — and what that architecture is quietly bad at surfacing
- The thing her years in content and broadcasting taught her to look for instead: not what someone says about their work, but how they talk about something that went wrong
- Why that question works: not as a trick, but because it accesses a different kind of honesty than a prepared answer can
- No framework at the end — just the reframe: an interview is less about what someone knows and more about how they hold uncertainty

**Tone:** Self-aware, a little contrarian, honest
