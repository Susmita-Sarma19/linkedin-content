# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-05-30

## Idea 1 — The Listener in the Room

**Angle:** Running a podcast taught her that the most important skill in content — and in work — isn't speaking clearly, it's listening without already planning your next line.

**Hook:** The worst podcast hosts I've heard all have one thing in common: they're not really listening to their guest. They're waiting.

**Outline:**
- When you produce a podcast, you stop treating conversation as performance and start treating it as excavation — you're looking for the thing the guest hasn't said yet
- This changed how she writes: she stopped projecting what she *thinks* an audience wants and started sitting with what they're actually asking
- It changed how she works with teams — listening to what's unsaid in a brief, a feedback session, a meeting
- The insight: the best content, the best collaboration, almost always starts with someone genuinely shutting up and paying attention

**Tone:** Warm, quietly sharp


## Idea 2 — Writing for People You'll Never Meet

**Angle:** She writes HR content designed to help managers recognize and retain employees she will never know — and there's something both humbling and clarifying about that invisible reach.

**Hook:** I write content that, if it works, makes someone feel seen at work. I will never know if it did.

**Outline:**
- Her job is to write things managers say to employees — appreciation messages, recognition frameworks, engagement copy — for real humans she has no access to
- Most content creators measure impact through analytics. She measures it by imagining a Monday morning somewhere, a manager reading what she wrote, an employee actually feeling it
- The distance between writer and reader is usually a gap to close. In HR content, it's a feature — you write it, someone else delivers it, someone else receives it
- The reflection: purpose in work doesn't require you to see the result. Sometimes you just have to trust the chain.

**Tone:** Reflective, quietly moved


## Idea 3 — What Live TV Taught Me About Mistakes

**Angle:** Nine years of live broadcasting rewired her relationship with imperfection — and the lesson is the opposite of what corporate culture usually teaches.

**Hook:** I made mistakes on live television. The camera kept rolling. The show ended. Nobody died.

**Outline:**
- In live TV, there is no undo. You mispronounce something, the teleprompter skips, a guest goes completely off-script — and you recover, in real time, on camera
- That experience made corporate "mistakes" feel strangely small in comparison: an email sent too early, a post with a typo, a campaign that underperformed
- The culture in most workplaces treats every mistake as evidence of something broken. Live TV taught her it's just what happens when humans do things
- The real skill isn't avoiding errors — it's learning to read the room after one and keep moving without making it the whole story

**Tone:** Punchy, grounded, slightly contrarian
