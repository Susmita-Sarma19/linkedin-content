# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-05

## Idea 1 — The Return

### Hook
"I moved to Kolkata in 2015. I came back to Guwahati in 2018. Most people who heard that story understood it as a retreat. It wasn't."

### Angle
From 2015 to 2017, she worked in Kolkata — first at a Subway franchise development office, then at a media buying firm. She did what career advice prescribes: go where the industry is. She had colleagues, a city, the faint hum of proximity to something larger. Then she came back.

Not in defeat. She'd answered the question she was asking. What Kolkata gave her was specific: the experience of being inside an industry cluster long enough to understand what it offered — and what it didn't. She returned knowing the difference between those two things. That difference is what made Guwahati a position rather than a default.

The post sits with this quietly: you can't know where your best work happens until you've stood somewhere else long enough to feel the comparison. The return was a conclusion, not a retreat.

### Source
Subway Systems India (Kolkata, March–July 2015) and releaseMyAd.com (Kolkata, August 2015–July 2017) — the biographical fact of having left Guwahati, spent roughly 2.5 years in a larger city, and returned. The "Women Who Stayed" framing in the ideas bank asserts the choice; this grounds it in the specific act of having tested the alternative.

### Resonance
Professionals who left smaller cities for metros and came back — and are still fielding the implicit question about whether it worked out. The post reframes the return without making it a manifesto, and without pretending the Kolkata years were formative only in retrospect. Quiet and specific in a way that lands precisely because it doesn't overexplain.

## Idea 2 — The Image Manager's Blind Spot

### Hook
"I can tell you exactly how perception is shaped, how trust is built, how a professional reputation travels. That's what I trained in. It took me ten years to aim those tools at my own career."

### Angle
She has a postgraduate diploma in Public Relations and Image Management. The academic foundation for understanding how institutions and people are perceived — the mechanics of reputation, the craft of shaping how others read you.

She spent the next decade writing about employee recognition: how companies should make people feel seen, valued, specifically named. Applying the logic of perception to other people's experiences, every week.

What she didn't do, for a long time, was run the same analysis on herself. Not negligence — it's that expertise in a domain and applying that expertise to your own case are genuinely different skills. When your craft is perception, you're probably also very good at not noticing how you're being perceived until the moment you have to care.

The post ends here: the cobbler's children problem is real, but it's also specific. It's not that you don't know. It's that knowing is not the same as looking.

### Source
PG Diploma in Public Relations/Image Management from Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan (2014–2015), followed by 6+ years writing employee recognition and engagement content at Vantage Circle. No prior idea has drawn on the image management credential or the gap between training in perception and applying it to yourself.

### Resonance
Practitioners of communication, branding, HR, or marketing — anyone whose professional expertise is fundamentally about how others show up, and who recognizes the specific dissonance of being slower to apply it inward. Universal, but rarely named this precisely. It's not a confession; it's an observation with enough specificity to feel earned.

## Idea 3 — The Formal Ending

### Hook
"On paper, I worked at Doordarshan for nine years and five months. The part the resume doesn't capture: for the last three of those years, I'd already moved on."

### Angle
She joined Vantage Circle in September 2019. She officially ended her tenure at Doordarshan Kendra in December 2022. Three years of overlap — and the story those years tell depends entirely on which end you read from.

The transition narrative says: she was careful, kept one foot on familiar ground while testing new territory. The ending narrative says: December 2022 was not when she left Doordarshan. It was when she let the paperwork catch up to something already true.

The formal ending and the actual ending rarely coincide. That gap is worth understanding — not as a judgment on the years in between (she was present; the work was real), but as an honest description of how professional endings actually work. They're not events. They're processes with a final administrative step at the far end.

The post doesn't resolve into a lesson. It ends where the observation does: endings have dates, but they rarely happen on them.

### Source
Doordarshan Kendra August 2013 – December 2022 (9 years 5 months); Vantage Circle from September 2019 — a 3+ year overlap. The specific biographical fact that she formally closed the Doordarshan chapter three years after she had, practically, already moved into the next one. "The Long Middle" (June 3) covers the experience of carrying both; this is a different angle — the structure of the ending itself, and what it means to declare something over that you already knew was.

### Resonance
Anyone who has held a role past the point of genuine engagement — for loyalty, security, or simply not having found the right moment to make it official. The post doesn't judge that delay; it names its honest structure. Career transition narratives almost always focus on the arrival. This one focuses on what endings actually look like from inside them, which is quieter and more true.
