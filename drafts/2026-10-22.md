# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-10-22

## Idea 1 — The Silence Skill

**Angle:** The most valuable thing nine years of live television taught her wasn't how to speak — it was how to stop. In a field saturated with advice about communicating more, the discipline of intentional silence is both underrated and transferable.

**Hook:** The most useful thing I learned in nine years of live television wasn't how to talk. It was when to stop.

**Outline:**
- In broadcast, silence is a technique: it lets the guest's answer settle, signals to the viewer that something mattered, keeps the host from filling space that should belong to the other person
- She carried this into writing and podcasting — the edit that preserves white space, the paragraph break that gives the reader a moment to arrive somewhere before moving on
- Most workplace communication advice is additive: more touchpoints, more updates, more visibility, more presence
- The professionals she's watched communicate most effectively aren't the loudest ones — they're the ones who've learned to pick their moment, and who trust the weight of silence to do something that more words wouldn't

**Tone:** Quietly provocative

## Idea 2 — The Recognition Irony

**Angle:** She's spent six years making the case that people need to feel seen at work. She writes from the position of someone who knows, firsthand and undramatically, what it's like when the work itself is largely invisible. That position makes her more precise — not more resentful.

**Hook:** I've written about the importance of recognition more times than I can count. I genuinely don't know if anyone ever found one of those articles useful.

**Outline:**
- Content that ranks, performs, and serves its purpose rarely comes back to you. The person who wrote it doesn't hear about it. This is structurally different from what the research says people need — it's not malice, it's just how the medium works
- Writing about recognition from inside that dynamic is clarifying: she has a working definition of what it means to contribute something real and not know whether it landed
- That experience doesn't make her cynical about the subject. It makes her more careful about what she's actually arguing. Recognition isn't about applause — it's about being seen doing something that matters
- For anyone in a supporting function, a behind-the-scenes role, a team that makes other people's work visible: the absence she's describing is probably familiar, and it's worth naming honestly

**Tone:** Wry and warm

## Idea 3 — What Distance Gives You

**Angle:** Working from Guwahati for companies serving national and global clients means operating from outside the frame that most of her industry builds its assumptions inside. Geographic distance from the center turns out to be a form of intellectual distance — useful for seeing what's assumed rather than what's true.

**Hook:** I work in HR tech. I live in Guwahati. The two things seem unrelated. That's what makes the combination work.

**Outline:**
- Most conversations in her industry take shape where her industry's people are: the metro conference circuit, the LinkedIn feeds of professionals in Bengaluru and Mumbai and Delhi, the research built for organizational contexts those cities recognize
- From Guwahati, she reads those conversations from outside the room where they were written. She notices the assumptions that would go unquestioned if she were embedded in them — about what a "normal" workplace looks like, what access to tools and training is assumed, what the employer-employee relationship is presumed to be
- Distance from the center isn't a gap to overcome. It's a different angle of vision. The professional who stands outside the frame and can name it has information the person inside doesn't
- The practical version: she questions frameworks that would simply feel true to her if she were working from within the ecosystem that built them. That habit of questioning is the thing the geography gave her

**Tone:** Grounded, quietly confident
