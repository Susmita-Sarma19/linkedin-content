# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-10-18

## Idea 1 — The Test I Run on Every Sentence

**Angle:** Writing in English while thinking in four languages gives her a built-in clarity check that no editorial tool replicates — if she can't say it plainly in Assamese, the idea probably isn't clear yet.

**Hook:** There's a test I run on almost every sentence I write. I've never told anyone about it.

**Outline:**
- When English phrasing starts to feel abstract or inflated, she tries restating it in Assamese — not translating word for word, but checking whether the idea lands in a language that doesn't dress things up the same way
- If it doesn't, the problem is almost never vocabulary — it's that the idea itself is still forming
- In HR writing especially, the gap between corporate English and clear language is where practitioners stop reading and start skimming
- Multilingualism isn't just a communication skill — it's an editing instinct, a way of pressure-testing clarity that no spellcheck or readability score can replicate

**Tone:** specific, quietly surprising

## Idea 2 — The Recognition I Don't Write About

**Angle:** She has written hundreds of pieces on employee recognition frameworks. What she hasn't examined in public is whether she — as an employee who also writes the content practitioners read — actually feels recognized at work.

**Hook:** I've published hundreds of pieces about employee recognition. I couldn't tell you with certainty whether I feel recognized at work.

**Outline:**
- She can name the research in her sleep: recognition should be specific, timely, sincere, tied to values — the conditions are well-documented and she has laid them out clearly for six years
- But what has actually made her feel seen at work has almost nothing to do with formal programs — a manager who remembered a small detail, a colleague who gave credit unprompted, a conversation that arrived at the right moment
- The gap between recognition infrastructure and recognition experience is one practitioners understand intellectually; she understands it from both sides simultaneously — as the person who writes the frameworks and as an employee living inside one
- That dual position occasionally makes her reconsider how the frameworks are framed — and whether the gap is a design failure or just an inherent limit of trying to systematize something human

**Tone:** honest, slightly dissonant

## Idea 3 — Before I Had a Reason to Post

**Angle:** The LinkedIn posts that have actually built her real professional relationships weren't strategic. They were written early on, before she had an audience, before she had a reason — and that absence of strategy is exactly what made them useful.

**Hook:** The most useful professional writing I ever did was back when publishing it had no real purpose.

**Outline:**
- When you post before you have an audience, you write differently — less performed, less shaped for an imagined reader, more willing to follow a thought to where it actually leads
- Those early posts — written when nobody was forming an impression — established the real habits: which topics she returned to without being told to, which phrasing felt right, what she was genuinely curious about
- The strategic posting that came later was built on a foundation she laid without strategy at all
- The takeaway isn't a method — it's an observation: the most durable professional writing she's done wasn't planned, it was just regular, honest, and unglamorous enough that nobody noticed until it accumulated

**Tone:** reflective, mildly subversive
