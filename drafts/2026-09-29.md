# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-29

## Idea 1 — The Feedback Lag

**Angle:** Moving from live TV — where audience reaction is instant — to digital content, where you wait days for analytics to tell you what landed.

**Hook:** On live television, I could feel within thirty seconds if something wasn't working.
Digital marketing gave me a 72-hour wait and a bounce rate.

**Outline:**
- The studio audience is one of the most honest feedback mechanisms that exists — energy in the room, silence, laughter, restlessness. Nine years of reading it becomes instinct.
- In content marketing, you publish and then you wait. The delayed signal changes how you work — you stop trusting your gut and start trusting data, which isn't always the same thing.
- What she had to unlearn: the assumption that good work feels right in real time. What she had to learn: how to read deferred feedback without losing confidence in the process between creation and response.
- The irony — the analytics are richer in digital, but the signal is slower and colder. Both are incomplete pictures of whether the work actually landed.

**Tone:** Quietly observational

## Idea 2 — The Work Nobody Reads

**Angle:** The invisible labor behind HR content — the research, the practitioner relationships, the editing passes — and why naming it matters for how we value content work.

**Hook:** The post takes twenty minutes to read.
It took three weeks to be worth reading.

**Outline:**
- What goes into an HR content piece before a word gets written: reading research, talking to practitioners, understanding what's been said before and what the gap actually is.
- The published piece is the smallest part of the process. The draft that gets cut, the angle that didn't hold up, the expert who redirected the entire premise — none of that appears in the byline.
- This isn't a complaint. It's a clarification: content work is often measured by output volume and engagement metrics, and neither captures what made the output worth engaging with.
- A specific observation from working in HR tech: the most useful content she's ever written started with a conversation that wasn't billed as research. That's not inefficiency — it's the actual method.

**Tone:** Precise, a little dry

## Idea 3 — What Six Years Knows

**Angle:** Staying at one company long enough to become institutional memory — and why that kind of knowledge is invisible until it disappears.

**Hook:** I've outlasted three rebrands, two team restructures, and one product pivot I watched happen in real time.
That's not loyalty. That's a different kind of expertise.

**Outline:**
- At six-plus years, you stop being the person who needs context and start being the person who provides it. You know why a decision was made in 2021. You know which approach was tried and didn't hold.
- That knowledge doesn't live in a document. It lives in the person who was there.
- The professional development conversation rarely names this as expertise — it tends to reward the person who moved fast and accumulated varied titles, not the one who accumulated depth. But the two are different things.
- What she's noticed: institutional memory is most visible to organizations in the moment it leaves. By then it's already gone.

**Tone:** Calm, a little pointed
