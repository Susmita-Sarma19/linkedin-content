# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-08

## Idea 1 — The AI Draft Problem

### Hook
In 2023, I gave an AI the same brief I'd been working from for three weeks.
It came back with something usable in four minutes. I sat with that for a while.

### Angle
She has been a content practitioner in HR tech for six years. In 2023, generative AI became capable of producing structurally sound HR content from a brief. The experience was disorienting — not because it threatened the job, but because it revealed something she hadn't articulated about what the job actually is.

The tool produced words. Organized, plausible, topically accurate. What it didn't produce was the editorial instinct that notices when a claim about recognition technology overclaims — when the sentence is technically defensible but slightly dishonest about the gap between what the platform does and what the person still has to do. That instinct doesn't live in the brief. It accumulates in the practitioner: from reading the research carefully enough to know when a new study doesn't actually support the headline it's traveling under, from interviewing enough practitioners to know the difference between what the framework recommends and what actually happens in the room.

The AI made the blank page less frightening. It also clarified that the blank page was never what she was doing. She was already on the other side of it before the writing started. That's the part that didn't transfer.

She's still using the tools. She's more precise now about what she's using them for.

### Source
Her role at Vantage Circle (2019–present), specifically the 2023–present period when generative AI tools became ubiquitous in content workflows. Entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. Distinct from "Writing on the Moving Surface" (July 7), which examined producing HR content during the Great Resignation disruption — this is about the disruption to the content practitioner's own role, and what the test of an AI-generated draft clarified about where the job actually lives. Neither the tech-utopian nor the tech-dystopian reading — a practitioner's honest account of what the experiment showed her.

### Resonance
Content professionals, communicators, and HR practitioners who have sat with the same question: after the tool produces the draft, what exactly is left? The post doesn't answer it as a manifesto. It answers it as a specific observation — from someone who tested it, found the tool useful, and had to think carefully about what "useful" meant and what it didn't cover. That's a more trustworthy answer than most of what circulates on this topic.

## Idea 2 — What Doesn't Go to Air

### Hook
For nine years, I hosted a thirty-minute episode every week.
The work to get there took about three days. I've thought a lot about what it means that nobody counted the three days.

### Angle
Nine years of weekly broadcast. Each episode visible, complete, traceable. The invisible work — the research into the guest's field, the question development across multiple drafts, the structural thinking about how thirty minutes should breathe, the pre-call to understand where the conversation might go, the rejected approaches that made the final one land — none of it appeared on screen. It accumulated, week after week, entirely off the record.

What the broadcast years clarified about recognition: institutions tend to see what went to air. The episode. The project that shipped. The proposal that landed. What doesn't appear in the record is the preparation, the thinking, the attempts that didn't work but made the one that did possible. She spent nine years making things whose visible surface was thirty minutes and whose real labor was substantially longer. Nobody acknowledged the three days. That wasn't an oversight — it was how the accounting was structured.

She now writes, at Vantage Circle, about what makes recognition land or fail. The specific failure she understands most directly is this one: acknowledging the deliverable and missing the work. The thirty-minute episode and the three days behind it are always two different things. Recognition programs that only see the first one miss most of what they were designed to acknowledge.

### Source
Nine years and five months as Talk Show Host, Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (August 2013–December 2022) — specifically the ongoing invisible labor between episodes across a sustained nine-year run. Entirely distinct from "The Two Months Behind the Camera" (July 4), which examined the production internship before she was on screen, and from "The Discipline of Staying Out of the Way" (June 27), which examined podcast self-effacement. Neither examined what accumulates invisibly across a nine-year production run, or what that reveals about how recognition programs tend to miss most of the work they're designed to acknowledge.

### Resonance
HR practitioners and people managers who design recognition around visible output — and everyone who has done the three days of work behind a thirty-minute result and felt the difference between being seen and being acknowledged for what's on the record. The post names the structural gap from someone who lived inside it for nine years before writing about it professionally. Strong for recognition program designers who know their frameworks are accurate and still sense something they're not quite catching.

## Idea 3 — The Year Recognition Was Theoretical

### Hook
The year I knew the most about recognition, I also got a raise.
I noticed they were completely separate events. I'm not sure I was supposed to notice that.

### Angle
She has written the research-backed case for why compensation and recognition are distinct events serving different psychological needs. The articles are accurate. The frameworks hold. The data is sound. She knows this not just professionally, but in the sense that she's tested it against enough practitioner experience to trust it.

Then she had the moment — as an employee — of receiving a raise without a word about the work behind it. No conversation about the thinking underneath the output, the sustained effort, the specific things she'd worked through. Just the adjustment in the payslip.

This isn't a grievance. The raise was real and appropriate. What it wasn't was recognition — and she knew, with professional precision, exactly why those were different. What she didn't anticipate: knowing it theoretically didn't prepare her for standing inside it. Six years of writing about the gap between compensation and acknowledgment had been accurate. It had also been, in the way research-backed writing tends to be, impersonal. Then she was the evidence.

What she carries from that: the practitioner who has felt the thing they write about writes it differently. Not with more certainty — the research is still the research. But from closer range. She thinks it shows in the work. She believes writing about the gap between pay and acknowledgment is more honest when you've been in it.

### Source
Six years as a content and marketing practitioner at Vantage Circle, producing HR content about recognition and compensation as distinct employee experience events, while simultaneously being an employee who experienced that distinction in her own working life. Distinct from "The Fishbowl" (July 6), which examined the general epistemological position of writing about employee experience while being an employee. Distinct from "The Scaffolding Problem" (June 28), which examined the limits of recognition technology. Neither examined the specific moment when theoretical expertise becomes personal knowledge — when something you've written about accurately becomes true in your own experience, and what the convergence changes in how you write about it afterward.

### Resonance
The broadest resonance of the three — every professional who has been paid fairly and still felt invisible knows the gap the post is describing. For HR practitioners who design recognition programs or write about the distinction between compensation and acknowledgment: the post names what it adds to the work when the writer has been inside the framework they're explaining. Not as evidence of suffering — as a different quality of understanding. The framework was always right. Being inside it changes how you hold it.
