# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-11-16

## Idea 1 — What the Survey Doesn't Ask

**Angle:** Six years writing about employee engagement has taught her that the things organizations measure are rarely the things that actually make people stay or leave.

**Hook:** The most important thing about how someone feels at work doesn't appear in any engagement survey I've ever written.

**Outline:**
- Engagement surveys are well-designed instruments measuring the wrong things — they capture what's easy to quantify, not what moves people
- The gap between "satisfaction score" and actual belonging: employees learn to answer the question they're being asked, not the one they'd actually answer if someone asked differently
- After six years writing frameworks for recognition and engagement, she can see the distance between the program and the person — the feeling of being seen doesn't follow from a points balance
- What she's found interviewing practitioners: the moments that matter most are almost never the ones the program designed

**Tone:** quietly challenging

## Idea 2 — The Content Nobody Respects

**Angle:** HR content is the most used and least regarded category in professional publishing — everyone reads it, nobody admits it's hard to write well.

**Hook:** HR content is probably the easiest kind of content to write badly. I've been trying to write it well for six years. The gap between those two things is larger than I expected.

**Outline:**
- HR advice is everywhere: listicles, frameworks, manager tips, culture playbooks — almost none of it is honest about what managers are actually facing
- The trap specific to this content category: it's written about people's real working lives, which means platitude is a kind of lying — a feel-good sentence that lets the reader off the hook without helping them
- What makes HR writing actually useful: the reader is managing real people, navigating real conflict, making decisions that affect someone's livelihood — they need precision, not motivation
- She writes for practitioners who've heard every framework and are still standing in the same room with the same problem. That's the reader she's trying not to let down.

**Tone:** candid, self-aware

## Idea 3 — Guwahati Kept Not Being the Problem

**Angle:** She spent years assuming her location would eventually cap what she could build — it didn't, and the reason isn't the one people usually give for why remote work "changed everything."

**Hook:** I kept waiting for Guwahati to become a limitation. Six years into a fully digital career, I'm still waiting.

**Outline:**
- The conventional story: remote work democratized careers, location stopped mattering, Tier-2 city professionals now have equal access
- What actually happened: the access changed, but the credibility assumptions didn't — the question of where you are becomes the question of who you are in professional spaces where certain cities still read as signals of seriousness
- What didn't change: having to build authority with no ambient infrastructure — no industry events, no proximity networks, no casual corridor conversations with people who'd think of her for opportunities
- What she built instead: a body of work that travels without her. That, it turns out, is not a workaround. It's the actual thing.

**Tone:** grounded, quietly certain
