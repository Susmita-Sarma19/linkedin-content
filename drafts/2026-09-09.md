# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-09

## Idea 1 — The Interviewer's Instinct

### Hook
I spent nine years asking other people questions for a living.
The training that gave me was not what I expected to carry into writing.

### Angle
Nine years of hosting a talk show isn't mainly training in how to speak — it's training in how to ask. Specifically: how to ask a question that opens someone up rather than one that gives them room to stay closed. A question with the right shape pulls the guest forward. The wrong one, however polished, lets them retreat into the prepared answer.

She didn't design this as a transferable skill. But when she started writing HR content, the same instinct arrived uninvited. The best content she writes doesn't deliver information at readers — it locates the question they haven't thought to ask yet and asks it first. The reader follows because the question is theirs.

Six years of writing. Nine years of hosting. The method is older than the job it's being used in.

**Tone:** Precise, quietly surprising

## Idea 2 — The Month Before Everything

### Hook
In June 2013 I was an intern at Doordarshan — learning the production side, staying out of frame.
Two months later I was the person on screen. I'm still not entirely sure what changed in that room.

### Angle
June to July 2013: production intern at Doordarshan Kendra, Guwahati. Learning how a broadcast works from the side of the room that sets it up — the equipment, the crew, the structure that stays invisible when the camera rolls. Not auditioning. Not in frame. Learning the machinery.

August 2013: Talk Show Host at the same institution.

She didn't know in June that she'd be hosting by August. She wasn't preparing for it. The two months she spent understanding how a programme is built from the inside turned out to be the most direct preparation possible — not because she planned it that way, but because someone in that institution saw something and made a decision she wasn't expecting.

What that experience lodged: readiness and opportunity don't arrive together on a schedule. Sometimes the preparation happens before you know what it's for. And sometimes the people watching can see what you can't — that you're already closer than you think.

**Tone:** Warm, quietly revelatory

## Idea 3 — The Degree I Don't Lead With

### Hook
My Master's is in Mass Communication.
I've never once led with it in a professional context.
There's a reason for that — and it's not that the degree was wrong.

### Angle
Gauhati University, 2012 to 2014: a Master's in Mass Communication. Two years studying how messages form, how audiences are constructed, how media structures meaning. The theory of her field, formally taught.

She never leads with it. In two careers — nine years in broadcast, six years in digital marketing — the credential has not once been the first thing she mentions.

What the Master's taught was real: the vocabulary for how communication works, the theoretical architecture that explains why some messages land and some don't. What it couldn't teach — what only the live television work revealed — was what it feels like when the message doesn't arrive. The theory describes the gap between sender and receiver. The practice drops you into it.

She doesn't lead with the degree because the formation that made her capable of the work didn't happen inside a classroom. It happened after, in the rooms the classroom prepared her to walk into. The degree gave her the map. The camera taught her the terrain.

**Tone:** Honest, precise

## Idea 4 — The Simultaneous Life

**Angle:** For nine years, she wasn't a TV host who later became a digital marketer — she was both at the same time. The career narrative assumes sequence; her lived experience was simultaneity. What it actually means to hold two distinct professional identities in parallel, not one after the other.

**Hook:** Everyone tells my career as a before and after. TV host, then digital marketer. Doordarshan, then Vantage Circle. The story of a transition.
The actual years didn't work that way.

**Outline:**
- For nine years, she walked into the Doordarshan studio and also wrote digital copy, built content calendars, studied SEO — these were not sequential lives but simultaneous ones
- The career narrative loves a turning point because it implies a clean decision; simultaneity is messier and more honest — she wasn't leaving one thing for another, she was building in two registers at once
- What the double-life produces: the TV work kept the digital writing from becoming too internal, too written for screens; the digital work gave her a vocabulary for what the camera had been teaching her all along
- The question nobody asks about career transitions: what if the "old" work and the "new" work ran together long enough that calling one old is wrong
- Careers aren't always before-and-after — sometimes they're both-at-once until one becomes the center of gravity, without ceremony

**Tone:** Quietly corrective

## Idea 5 — The Work That Disappears Into the Work

**Angle:** Podcast production is credited to the host and the brand. The producer's contribution — the structure, the questions, the coaching, the edit — is designed to become invisible in the finished episode. What it means to build real expertise in a form where the measure of success is that you can't be found in the output.

**Hook:** The best episode I ever produced — you'd have no way of knowing I was involved.
That's not a complaint. That's the job. But it took me a while to understand what kind of discipline that actually requires.

**Outline:**
- The visible roles in a podcast: the host, the guest, the brand. The producer is the shape the conversation takes — the silence in the right place, the follow-up that opens the real answer, the edit that removes the moment the guest almost said something important and then didn't
- Expertise that's designed to be invisible is a specific kind of expertise: you can't point to the moment it worked; you can only hear when it didn't
- This produces a particular relationship to credit — and to craft. The work isn't waiting to be attributed; it's waiting to be good. Those aren't the same accountability
- Six years of HR podcast production at Vantage Circle: what she's learned about how conversations become useful versus how they sound useful — these are different things, and the edit is where you find out which you have
- The invisible work is still real work. The question is whether you can value a skill you can't display

**Tone:** Craft-focused, a little wry

## Idea 6 — The Feedback That Arrives Too Late

**Angle:** The most honest information an organization gets about its culture arrives in exit interviews — after the person has already decided to leave. She writes about recognition and engagement for a living, and this structural lag is the most underexamined design problem in the field.

**Hook:** The most honest thing an employee ever tells their organization is what they say on their way out.
We've built an entire industry around preventing that conversation. But we haven't spent nearly enough time asking why it only happens then.

**Outline:**
- Exit interviews are when engagement research finally gets honest signal — not survey scores, not pulse responses, but the specific account of what the environment felt like to live inside when no performance consequence was attached to saying it accurately
- The recognition and engagement industry is almost entirely forward-looking: how to build the program, how to run the cadence, how to measure participation. Exit data is backward-looking and therefore treated as remediation, not design input
- What she notices after years of reading the research: the gap between what engagement surveys measure and what exit interviews reveal is not noise. It's the part of the experience that wasn't safe to say while still employed
- Psychological safety isn't just a culture metric — it's a data quality problem. If honest signal is only available at exit, the entire feedback loop is broken at source
- The uncomfortable question for any organization running a recognition program: what would this year's exit interviews tell us that our engagement scores won't?

**Tone:** Incisive, a little uncomfortable
