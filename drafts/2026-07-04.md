# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-04

## Idea 1 — The Two Months Behind the Camera

### Hook
I spent two months building the show before I was ever in it.
I didn't know at the time that was the better order.

### Angle
June to July 2013. Production intern at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati. Two months watching the floor manager read cues, understanding how the technical director held the run, learning what made a segment breathe and what made it seize. She was invisible — clipboard, headset, the room's machinery. Not the face of anything.

August 2013, she stepped in front of the camera for the first time.

She wasn't walking into a mystery. She'd already been the room. She knew what the director needed from the talent, where the guest had to be positioned before the opening beat, what the clock meant when it moved a certain way. The camera didn't surprise her — she had spent sixty days watching it from the side that doesn't appear on screen.

What she understands only later: the instinct has never left. In podcast production she builds the structure before she records. In content strategy she frames the series before she writes the first piece. She still learns the machinery before she's in it. It might be the thing that stayed most.

Two months behind the camera before anyone puts you in front of one. She recommends it.

### Source
Production Intern at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (June–July 2013), immediately preceding her Talk Show Host career (August 2013–December 2022). This two-month period — learning the show from inside its production infrastructure before becoming its face — has never appeared in any prior draft. Distinct from "The Discipline of Staying Out of the Way" (June 27, podcast production and skilled self-effacement) and "The Sixty Seconds Before Live" (July 2, the internal state before going live). Neither examined the formative experience of having been the production before being the talent.

### Resonance
Professionals put into roles before they understood the infrastructure — and the ones who did the transition differently, spending time in the system before they were in the seat. For her HR and L&D audience: what well-designed onboarding and role-shadowing actually gives you is this. The room stops being a mystery before you're expected to run it.

## Idea 2 — The Blog Nobody Assigned

### Hook
I have a blog I've kept for years. No brief. No audience target. No SEO. Nobody asked for it.
I'm not sure I'd still be writing without it.

### Angle
The professional content calendar is real. Nine years of weekly broadcast episodes, then six years on a content schedule: she has made things on deadline her entire working life. That discipline is not the question.

The blog at susmitype.wordpress.com is different in its conditions. There is no deadline. There is no brief. There is no downstream metric that says whether it worked. It exists because she opened a window and started writing, and that was the whole condition for it.

What holding both practices together has taught: professional writing gets sharper and more purposeful over time. It knows what it's for before the first sentence. It goes directly. The blog writing is something else — it's where she finds out what she thinks before she knew she was thinking it. The professional piece arrives with a question it needs to answer. The blog piece sometimes doesn't know what it's about until the third paragraph, and that's not a problem with the piece; that's what the piece is for.

The purposeless piece isn't inferior writing. It's a different kind of examination — the kind that can't happen when someone is waiting.

She doesn't know what it would cost, over six years of professional production, to let that go. She suspects it would show up in the work, eventually, as a particular kind of flatness. Some part of the practice needs to stay unassigned.

### Source
Personal blog at susmitype.wordpress.com, listed in her LinkedIn profile, maintained alongside six years of professional content production at Vantage Circle and nine years of broadcast work. Has never appeared in any prior draft. Distinct from "The Content Without a Name" (July 2, about unattributed organizational content) and "What Starts Before the Idea Does" (July 3, about the creative psychology of recurring production). Neither addresses the personal writing practice as its own parallel thread — distinct from professional work in its conditions, its purpose, and what it keeps alive.

### Resonance
Content professionals, writers, communicators who have maintained — or quietly let lapse — a personal practice alongside the professional one. For her HR practitioners: the unlogged version of the work is the same category. The mentoring conversation that generated no report, the feedback that wasn't in the form. Some of what matters most is what nobody assigned.

## Idea 3 — What the Degree Prepared Me For

### Hook
My master's degree in Mass Communication prepared me for a media landscape that mostly doesn't exist anymore.
That turns out to be fine.

### Angle
Gauhati University, 2012–2014. Mass Communication as an academic discipline was built when the paradigm was broadcast and print — when the audience was a public rather than a user, when distribution was institutional rather than algorithmic. The curriculum was designed for one version of the industry.

She has practiced, almost entirely, in a different version.

SEO was not in the syllabus. Content calendars, platform analytics, bounce rates, A/B subject lines — none of it. What the degree did teach: audience theory. Message design. The foundational question that precedes every format decision: what is this doing to the person who receives it? What lives in the gap between what you intend and what lands?

Those questions were written for television and newspapers. They still run underneath everything she does in digital.

The most durable part of any training is usually the part that asks the oldest question in the field, not the part that teaches the current tools. The tools change. What communication is trying to do between people — the conditions that make a message land versus merely arrive — doesn't change at the same rate.

Twelve years into a career, she finds herself using the analog part of a media education inside a digital one. The surface of the degree is dated. The foundation of it is still moving. She suspects that's true of most serious training, if you look far enough underneath it.

### Source
MA in Mass Communication, Gauhati University (2012–2014), followed by a career built in digital channels at Vantage Circle (2019–present) and nine years of broadcast at Doordarshan (2013–2022). The gap between the media paradigm her education was built for and the paradigm she actually practices in has never been examined in any prior draft. Distinct from "The Commerce Detour" (June 30, about the BCom degree as an unexpected asset) and "The Degree Nobody Asks About" (July 1, about the PR/Image Management PG diploma). This is specifically about Mass Communication as a discipline — what its foundational questions give you when the platforms it was designed for have largely shifted form.

### Resonance
Professionals whose formal training feels dated at the surface and finds, on examination, that the deepest principles transferred anyway. For her HR audience: the same pattern holds in management theory, recognition frameworks, and organizational behavior research — models built for different workplace contexts, yet the underlying understanding of what acknowledgment does between people outlives the forms it was first described in. What was foundational tends to stay foundational. What was procedural doesn't.
