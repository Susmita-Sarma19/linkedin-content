# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-04

## Idea 1 — The Number and the Story Beneath It

**Angle:** Six years of writing about employee engagement data has taught her that the metric is the invitation into the human story — not the replacement for it.

**Hook:** An engagement score of 72% tells you something is wrong. It tells you nothing about what.

**Outline:**
- HR tech produces numbers. Good ones. Numbers that matter, that move, that get presented in board decks.
- But the score is the beginning of the inquiry, not the answer. Writing about it as though it's the answer is the most common failure mode in engagement content.
- What she's learned in six years: data is the surface tension. The real story lives in what broke under it — and the content that earns attention is the content that knows the difference.
- The practical version: you can write engagement content that makes HR leaders feel like their numbers are understood, or you can write content that makes them feel like the people are understood. Only one kind gets saved.

**Tone:** Grounded, slightly sharp

## Idea 2 — The Discipline of Distance

**Angle:** Building a career in digital from Guwahati — where the industry always seems to be happening somewhere else — forces a professional presence that proximity never had to develop.

**Hook:** I've never been to most of the conferences in my field. I've also never needed to be in a room to be in the conversation.

**Outline:**
- The geography of professional visibility: industry events, informal networks, hallway introductions — concentrated in metros, rarely passing through Guwahati.
- What you build instead when you're structurally never in the room: a written presence, a body of work, relationships that form through screens and sustained attention rather than shared airspace.
- The irony: "remote-first" became a philosophy for companies after 2020. Professionals from Tier-2 cities were already doing it by necessity, years earlier, without anyone naming it a strategy.
- What distance taught her about presence that doesn't depend on proximity — and why that version of visibility may actually travel further than the one built on being in the right city at the right time.

**Tone:** Quietly confident, a little wry

## Idea 3 — The Calendar That Wasn't Mine

**Angle:** Writing global HR content from Northeast India means noticing which cultural moments get treated as universal — and which ones don't show up at all.

**Hook:** Every employee recognition guide I've read assumes you know what month the year ends in. Most of them are wrong about that for more of the world than they think.

**Outline:**
- Global HR content has a default calendar. December as the peak, Q4 as the marker, a specific set of holidays as the shorthand for "celebration at work." It's presented as universal. It isn't.
- Writing about recognition and belonging from Guwahati: Bihu doesn't appear in most engagement content calendars. Neither do Eid, Pongal, Onam — unless someone has deliberately put them there.
- The specific position she works from: she notices the gaps because she lives in them. The content that centers one region's rhythm as everyone's rhythm is usually invisible to the people writing it.
- What this taught her about the actual scope of "culture at work" — and how much of what gets published as global HR content is, quietly, generalized from one place and applied everywhere else.

**Tone:** Specific, culturally grounded, gently provocative
