# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-16

## Idea 1 — The Recognition Lab

**Angle:** She writes about employee recognition while being an employee herself — which means she is simultaneously the content expert on recognition theory and the experimental subject. Six years of knowing the research hasn't made the experience of recognition any more predictable.

**Hook:** I know the research on employee recognition better than most people you'd ask. I'm also an employee. Those turn out to be two very different kinds of knowing.

**Outline:**
- The research is clear on what good recognition requires: specific, timely, genuine, tied to the work — she can recite it. Knowing the framework and receiving something that actually lands are not the same experience.
- The gap shows up in both directions: moments of recognition that checked every box and still felt hollow, and offhand acknowledgments that had no ceremony at all and stayed with her for weeks. The variables the research tracks aren't the ones her memory kept.
- Working at a recognition technology company makes this personal. The product she helps market exists to solve a real problem — she believes that. But she has also felt, from the inside, how easy it is to implement the mechanism and miss the point entirely.
- What writing about recognition for six years and receiving it as an ordinary employee has clarified: the research describes the conditions. It doesn't guarantee the feeling. The gap between those two is where the hard work lives — and nobody's model closes it all the way.

**Tone:** Wry, precise, disarmingly honest

## Idea 2 — What the Camera Trained

**Angle:** Nine years of broadcast television build a specific kind of embodied knowledge — the body in frame, the voice in the room, the physical discipline of filling a space with intention. That knowledge now lives inside a job where none of it is visible. What happens to physical professional expertise when the medium changes and the body disappears from the work?

**Hook:** For nine years my job required my body. I knew how to use a camera, a room, a pause. Then I moved to a job where none of that could be seen — and discovered I was still using it.

**Outline:**
- Broadcast training is physical: where to stand, how to hold stillness without going blank, what you do with your hands in the half-second before speaking, how to make a space feel occupied. These are real skills, built slowly through repetition, and they don't go abstract — they live in the body.
- Moving to written content meant working in a medium where none of that physical apparatus is available. No camera, no room, no voice. And yet: sentence rhythm is still pacing. Paragraph breaks are still breath. The decision to end early is still knowing when the room is full.
- She didn't consciously translate the broadcast skills — they arrived already translated. The physical discipline of television had already converted itself, somewhere along the way, into structural discipline on the page.
- What she didn't expect: the most useful thing the camera trained wasn't how to perform. It was when to stop. Knowing when something is complete is a skill that has nothing to do with the medium — and almost everything to do with having learned it somewhere you couldn't fake it.

**Tone:** Quietly philosophical, a little surprising

## Idea 3 — The City Nobody Markets To

**Angle:** Guwahati doesn't appear in most marketing personas. She has spent her career writing marketing content while knowing she lives outside the frame that most marketing assumes. The view from the unconsidered city.

**Hook:** I make marketing content for a living. I also live in a city that most marketing has quietly decided isn't worth talking to.

**Outline:**
- Marketing personas are built on data, and the data reflects where attention and spend already exist. Tier-1 cities — Delhi, Mumbai, Bangalore — anchor the archetypes. What you assume about your reader (their commute, their reference points, the way they make decisions, what they aspire to) comes from those anchors. Guwahati doesn't anchor anything.
- She's been writing digital marketing content for six years and knows how the personas work. She also knows what it feels like to consume marketing that wasn't designed with you in mind — the small wrongness of content that almost fits, the reference that lands slightly off, the aspiration pointed at someone else's version of arrival.
- The professional upside of this position: you notice when content is making assumptions because you're always on the outside of the assumption. She reads drafts differently when she can feel the city it was written for. That instinct is specific and trained and not something the personas measure.
- The thing she's still figuring out: how much of that awareness stays sharp if she ever stopped working from Guwahati. Some forms of knowledge are site-specific. She's not sure this one travels.

**Tone:** Dry, clear-eyed, a little pointed without being aggrieved
