# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-27

## Idea 1 — The Listener Who Doesn't Know They're the Subject

**Angle:** Making a podcast for HR practitioners means making it for someone who is also, sometimes, the unrecognized employee — and that double identity is where the real listening happens.

**Hook:** Every podcast episode I produce for HR practitioners is technically for the person running the program. But I've started to wonder how many people listen because they're still waiting for the program to reach them.

**Outline:**
- The stated audience for HR content is practitioners — managers, L&D leads, people ops professionals building recognition frameworks
- But the actual listenership is more complicated: some of those people are also employees who've never been on the receiving end of something that worked, building programs they themselves haven't experienced
- That split identity changes how you make the content — not simplification, but precision about which room you're actually speaking into
- The episodes she's proudest of are the ones where the HR professional and the employee in the same person could both leave with something

**Tone:** Perceptive, quietly humanizing

## Idea 2 — The Guwahati Working Day

**Angle:** Being based in Northeast India — in a timezone that's ahead of everywhere else in the country — means her workday has always started in a different context than the colleagues she's collaborating with, and she stopped pretending otherwise.

**Hook:** My working day starts about an hour before the rest of India's. That's not a scheduling detail. It's a description of what it means to be Northeast in a national industry.

**Outline:**
- Northeast India observes a de facto different temporal rhythm — the sun rises earlier, local offices open earlier, the cadence of a Guwahati morning is structurally different from a Bangalore or Delhi morning
- In a nationally distributed work culture that treats "India Standard Time" as a neutral baseline, the Northeast offset reads as a lag when it's actually a lead — and the cultural assumption built into that framing is worth naming
- For six years of remote-first digital work, she's been in meetings where "morning session" means 11am for Mumbai and she's been at her desk since nine; this isn't resentment, it's observation
- What it did: it made her very precise about context — because her context was never assumed to be the default, she had to learn to state it, and that habit became part of how she communicates

**Tone:** Grounded, clear-eyed — not a grievance, an observation that holds a larger truth

## Idea 3 — Recognition Is Not a Program

**Angle:** Six years of writing about employee recognition taught her that the research on what makes people feel seen points to something no platform can automate — and most recognition programs are quietly designed to avoid that conclusion.

**Hook:** I have spent six years writing about employee recognition. The finding that shows up in nearly every study, worded differently each time, is the same: people want to feel that someone noticed. Not a system. Someone.

**Outline:**
- Employee recognition research consistently points to specificity and immediacy as the variables that determine whether recognition lands — the generic "great job" at the end of a quarter does something different from a specific observation made close to the moment
- The technology that exists to scale recognition is real and useful — but the thing it scales is delivery, not attention; you can automate a certificate, you cannot automate the quality of observation that makes the certificate mean something
- After six years of writing this content, she's come to think the industry's real challenge isn't building better programs — it's convincing managers that noticing is the job, and that it takes longer than two minutes a week
- The most useful thing she's written in this space wasn't a framework or a listicle; it was a single concrete description of what it feels like to do good work in a room where no one says anything

**Tone:** Honest, practitioner-precise — someone who has written around the subject long enough to name what most of the content avoids
