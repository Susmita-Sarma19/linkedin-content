## Idea 1 — The Survey That Went Nowhere

### Hook
In September 2013, I knocked on doors in rural Guwahati to ask people if they'd seen a government advertisement.
Most of them hadn't. That was the finding. That wasn't what surprised me.

### Angle
September 2013: fresh into her first month as a Talk Show Host at Doordarshan, she was also a field surveyor for an IIMC, New Delhi research campaign — measuring the reach and access of Bharat Nirman advertisements in rural Guwahati. Bharat Nirman was a large-scale government infrastructure scheme with a significant national advertising campaign. IIMC was studying whether the message was actually arriving. She was the person on the ground.

The gap between what the campaign was designed to do and what households had actually received was visible one door at a time. Not a data gap — a human gap. The ad existed, the media placement was real, the production value was high. The family at the last house hadn't seen it. The family at the one before that wasn't sure.

That survey took one month. What it lodged stayed longer: communication designed for an audience is not the same as communication received by one. She'd spend the next decade in roles built on that distinction — hosting conversations that had to actually reach the person in the chair, writing content that had to land for a practitioner in a different city, producing podcasts where the edit determined whether the idea arrived intact. The finding from the Bharat Nirman survey was about a government ad campaign. The question it raised applies everywhere.

### Source
Surveyor on the IIMC, New Delhi research campaign on reach and access of Bharat Nirman advertisements, Guwahati (rural), September 2013. This biographical detail appears on her CV and has not appeared in any prior draft. It is her earliest piece of professional fieldwork, running concurrently with the first month of her Doordarshan hosting role.

### Resonance
For communicators working at any scale — the design of the message is only half the work. The other half is accounting for the distance between where the message starts and where it actually lands. Most of us operate at the transmission end and trust reach data to close the loop. She learned early, in person, that the loop doesn't always close.

## Idea 2 — The Two Years I Sold Ad Space

### Hook
For two years in Kolkata, I was on the selling side of media.
Before that, I had spent two years studying how media works. The gap between those two things was the most honest education I got.

### Angle
2014: she finished her master's in mass communication at Gauhati University — two years of studying how media structures meaning, how audiences are constructed, how messages travel through systems. 2015: she joined releaseMyAd.com in Kolkata as a Media Relations Associate. Her job was to sell advertising placement across publications.

The mass communication curriculum has a precise understanding of advertising: how it functions ideologically, what it does to editorial independence, the economics of attention. It teaches you to read media critically. It does not teach you what advertisers actually care about when they're deciding where to spend, or why a placement in one paper moves inventory in Assam and a placement in another doesn't. That part was learned in the field.

Two years at releaseMyAd rewired how she reads a media environment. She was no longer at the point of transmission — she was in the market architecture that decides what gets transmitted and where. The editorial value of a publication and its commercial value to an advertiser are related but not identical. Understanding both, simultaneously, from the selling side, is not what mass communication programs prepare you for. It's what made the subsequent content work feel different — she could read the business logic of a media decision, not just the editorial one.

### Source
Media Relations Associate at releaseMyAd.com, Kolkata (August 2015–July 2017, 2 years). This role, which immediately followed her PG Diploma in PR and Image Management (2014–2015), has not been explored in any prior draft. It is distinct from the Subway Systems idea (The Market Study on My Hometown), which focused on the Northeast India market research angle. This idea concerns the specific experience of working in media sales after formal training in mass communication theory.

### Resonance
For anyone who's crossed from the editorial or creative side of media to the commercial side, or vice versa: fluency in both changes what you see in each. The writer who understands how placements get bought reads content differently. The media buyer who understands what it costs to make something worth reading prices differently. The two languages don't merge — but knowing both is not nothing.

## Idea 3 — The Year the Theory Was the Job

### Hook
In my second year of a Mass Communication master's, I was already a practicing Talk Show Host.
The professor teaching me broadcast theory had never hosted a show.
I had. The classroom started to look different.

### Angle
Her master's in Mass Communication at Gauhati University ran from 2012 to 2014. Her Talk Show Host role at Doordarshan Kendra began in August 2013. From that point until she finished the degree the following year, she was simultaneously a full-time graduate student in mass communication and a practicing broadcast professional at a national station — not sequentially, but in the same calendar weeks.

What the second year of the master's offered: theoretical architecture. Communication models, media analysis, the language for understanding what happens when a message travels from a sender to a receiver. She had access to frameworks that could name what she'd been experiencing on air since August. The classroom was, for the first time, explaining the job she was already doing.

What the job offered: the second year's theoretical coursework arrived with an immediate test that no assignment could replicate. She could read about how a live interview creates the conditions for disclosure — or she could have just done sixteen of them. The theory was useful precisely because the practice was already there to ground it. And the practice felt different once she had the vocabulary.

Most academic experience of communication theory is preparatory — it precedes the application. Her second year ran the other way: the application preceded the classroom by two months, and from then on she was learning both at once. The sequence that textbooks describe — study, then do — didn't hold for her. What she found instead was that the two, running simultaneously, illuminated each other in ways neither could have done alone.

### Source
Gauhati University Master's in Mass Communication, 2012–2014. Doordarshan Kendra Talk Show Host role beginning August 2013. These two facts appear together on her CV; the specific experience of being a practicing broadcaster during the second year of her communication theory degree has not appeared in any prior draft. This is distinct from "The Degree I Don't Lead With" (which examines the credential as an artifact she rarely cites) and from any prior idea about the master's.

### Resonance
For anyone holding professional experience and formal education at the same time: when the study and the practice run simultaneously, each makes the other legible in ways it can't alone. The theory has a test. The practice has a vocabulary. Whether or not the sequence is conventional, the overlap is something — if you let the two talk to each other rather than keeping them in separate compartments.
