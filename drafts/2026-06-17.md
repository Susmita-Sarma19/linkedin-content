# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-17

## Idea 1 — The Survey That Didn't Land

### Hook
In September 2013, I spent a month in rural Guwahati asking people whether they'd seen a government campaign. Most of them hadn't. That was the research finding. It became something else too.

### Angle
Fresh out of a master's in mass communication, her first real fieldwork assignment was a research survey under IIMC, New Delhi — going out into rural Guwahati to study whether Bharat Nirman's national advertising campaign had reached its intended audience. The campaign was well-funded, nationally distributed, part of a large government communication effort. And in the villages and outskirts of the city she walked through, most people she asked had not encountered it. They weren't opposed to it. They just hadn't seen it. That gap — between the campaign that existed and the audience it was built for — was the first professional data point of her career. She's thought about it since: at Doordarshan when she wondered whether her segments reached people who needed them, and at Vantage Circle when she writes for HR managers who are themselves trying to reach employees who haven't absorbed what was communicated to them. The distance between what was made and whether it landed is a constant across every medium she's worked in. She learned to ask that question early — from the people most honest about the answer.

### Source
September 2013, one-month surveyor for a Research Campaign on Reach and Access of Bharat Nirman Advertisements under IIMC, New Delhi — fieldwork in rural Guwahati. Entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. The earliest professional assignment in her career, pre-dating all subsequent roles. Distinct from all content-craft ideas: this is the fieldwork moment — interviewing an audience, not serving one — that first surfaced the gap between content made and content received. That gap runs as an unbroken thread through Doordarshan, Vantage Circle, and every content problem since.

### Resonance
Content and communications professionals who have made something intended to reach an audience and wondered whether it actually did. For her HR audience, it names the perpetual challenge of internal communications: you sent the message, ran the town hall, posted the update — but did it land with the person who needed it most?

## Idea 2 — The Month Between

### Hook
I interned at Doordarshan in June 2013. They offered me a show in August. I said yes before I had any real evidence I was ready.

### Angle
The production internship at Doordarshan ran two months — June and July 2013. In August, before she'd had any real runway as a practitioner, they offered her a talk show host position. The feeling of readiness wasn't there. The offer was. She took it. Nine years and five months of hosting followed. What she understands now that she didn't then: readiness is mostly a feeling, and the feeling tends to arrive after the fact — after the camera is on, after the live segment has aired and didn't collapse. Waiting for the internal signal to confirm you're prepared is a strategy that rarely produces the preparation. The thing that prepares you is usually the thing you felt underprepared for. The month between the internship end and the host start was one of the shortest she can remember. There was no time to overthink it. She's carried that lesson since — and has tried to notice when the feeling of not-yet-ready is evidence to wait, and when it's just the feeling.

### Source
Doordarshan Kendra production internship (June–July 2013), immediately followed by Talk Show Host hire (August 2013) — a two-month internship that ended and, one month later, became a full hosting role. Entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. No idea has examined the earliest days of the Doordarshan career: the moment of the hire, the compressed transition from trainee to on-air host, and what that early experience of being moved forward faster than readiness installed. Distinct from "The Partial Map" (June 10), which examines her current IC-to-manager transition: this is the first major transition, decades earlier, and how its shape has informed every professional move since.

### Resonance
Anyone who has said yes to a role before they felt fully ready — and discovered afterward that the role was the preparation. For her HR audience, the post touches a live question in talent development: where does the signal for readiness actually live — inside the candidate, or in the work itself?

## Idea 3 — Theory Last

### Hook
In 2014, I enrolled in a course on how public image is built. I was already on television. The theory named things I already knew. That was the surprise.

### Angle
She started the PG diploma in PR and Image Management at Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan in 2014, a year into her run as a Talk Show Host at Doordarshan. The classroom taught frameworks for how public perception is shaped: message consistency, managing visibility, the distance between intent and reception. She recognized all of it — not analytically, but practically. A year of being on camera had already built an instinctive knowledge of how you present publicly. The theory didn't create that knowledge; it organized it. What she hadn't expected was how useful it is to receive the formal language for something you've been doing by feel. The classroom made visible what had been running on instinct. And the instinct kept the theory honest — she could test every framework against things she'd already done and feel the difference when the model didn't quite fit. She'd assumed formal study was how you built competence. That year clarified something different: sometimes the study comes after the competence, and what it gives you isn't skill but legibility. You understand what you've been doing. The practice was already there. The language for it arrived late.

### Source
PG Diploma in Public Relations/Image Management, Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan (2014–2015), pursued while already working as Talk Show Host at Doordarshan (August 2013–December 2022) — formal study of image and communication management occurring one year into active broadcast practice. Distinct from "The PR Ghost" (June 15), which examined the downstream effect of the PR training on current content work: the frame that arrived before content strategy was named, and runs under it still. This is a different position: the specific experience of studying formally what you're already doing, and what the reversed sequence — practice first, theory second — gives you that the conventional order doesn't.

### Resonance
Professionals who've pursued formal education or certification in fields where they already had practical experience — and discovered that receiving the framework after the fact is a different, specific kind of useful. For her HR audience, this maps directly: practitioners who've developed sophisticated instincts about organizational behavior before encountering the academic model that names what they've been doing all along.
