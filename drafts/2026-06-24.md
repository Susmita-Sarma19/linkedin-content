# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-24

## Idea 1 — The Expertise Trap

**Angle:** Six years of writing about employee recognition builds real depth — and a specific risk: the longer you know a subject, the easier it becomes to stop asking the beginner's question that still hasn't been properly answered.

**Hook:** At some point, you stop asking "why does this matter?" because you already know. That's when the writing starts to flatten.

**Outline:**
- Depth is real — six years means she can write on autopilot, anticipate objections, cite the right research without looking it up
- But expertise has a shadow: you start writing from inside the frame instead of around it
- The questions that regenerate a subject aren't the advanced ones — they're the ones that feel too basic to ask by now
- The discipline: approaching something you know deeply as if you're explaining it for the first time to someone who isn't convinced yet

**Tone:** Quietly self-aware — a practitioner catching herself mid-drift

## Idea 2 — The Unglamorous Work

**Angle:** Behind every "thought leadership" post is a working day of CMS glitches, broken redirects, keyword spreadsheets, and email sequence fixes — the operational reality the professional persona on LinkedIn never shows, and what gets lost when it stays invisible.

**Hook:** My LinkedIn profile says "Digital Marketing." It doesn't say "spent three hours this week debugging a broken redirect in the CMS."

**Outline:**
- The polished professional persona and the actual working day are two different things — and the gap is almost never discussed
- Digital marketing involves a lot of unglamorous operational work: analytics audits, tool migrations, broken automations, platform quirks
- We post about strategy and insight because that's what the platform rewards; the plumbing is invisible and gets treated as not worth mentioning
- What stays hidden: an honest picture of what a career in content and marketing actually looks like for people early in it — the part that's skilled but not aspirational-sounding

**Tone:** Dry and grounded — a raised eyebrow at the performance, not a complaint about it

## Idea 3 — The Camera Commits

**Angle:** Nine years of live broadcasting — where there's no undo, no second take, no editing after the fact — permanently changed how she thinks about the moment you press publish. What an irreversible medium teaches about commitment.

**Hook:** Nine years of live television taught me one thing: you don't get to go back. That changed how I approach every piece of content I put out now.

**Outline:**
- Live broadcasting is irreversible — the mistake and the brilliant moment both exist permanently in the second they happened; there's no version history
- That constraint forces a specific kind of preparation: you have to be ready to mean it before you open your mouth, because correction isn't coming
- Digital content has no equivalent constraint — everything is editable, revisionable, deletable — but that flexibility can become a way of never quite committing to the thing
- What she borrowed from the camera: if it's worth saying, say it like you mean it the first time; the edit doesn't fix the hesitation underneath

**Tone:** Considered, precise — a craft observation from someone who's worked in both registers
