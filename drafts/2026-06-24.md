# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-24

## Idea 1 — The Metro Chapter

### Hook
Moving to a bigger city is supposed to expand you.
Two years in Kolkata made me smaller in the best way — more particular, more specifically from somewhere.

### Angle
She moved to Kolkata in 2015 — first for a five-month stint at Subway's development office, then two years at releaseMyAd. Kolkata isn't hostile to people from the Northeast. It simply doesn't ask. Nobody wanted to know what Guwahati was like, not because they were dismissive but because the question hadn't occurred to them. For the first time, being from Assam was something she had to explain to someone — and explaining it surfaced how much she'd never needed to examine it while she was inside it.

You don't know what your perspective is made of until the context that produced it is no longer available. Two years in a city that doesn't know yours exists is an accelerated version of that lesson. The Northeast voice, the specific orientation she'd always had, wasn't generic Indian — it was something. She knew this the same way you know the air in a room once you've stepped outside.

She returned to Guwahati in 2018. The city she came back to hadn't changed. Her relationship to it had. She came back knowing where she was from — not as a fact, but as a perspective. That's not something she could have learned inside it.

### Source
releaseMyAd.com, Media Relations Associate, August 2015–July 2017, Kolkata — the only sustained metro period in her career, approximately 2.5 years away from Guwahati (Subway, March 2015 through releaseMyAd, July 2017). Her return to Guwahati in 2018 (SmartCoach) followed by building her career permanently from there. Entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. Related idea "The City Nobody Markets To" (June 16) examines being outside the assumed marketing persona as a current Guwahati resident; "The Northeast Voice" (CLAUDE.md, in progress) addresses representation in a metro-dominated industry. Neither examines what the Kolkata chapter specifically clarified — the identity knowledge that becomes available only from outside the place that produced it, and why she came back.

### Resonance
Anyone who has left a non-metro Indian city for a larger one and returned — or had the experience of having their background become visible precisely by being somewhere that couldn't see it. For her HR audience, this maps to a real organizational dynamic: you often cannot understand the culture you're inside until you've temporarily left it, and the re-entry brings a kind of clarity that internal immersion alone doesn't produce.

## Idea 2 — The Double Life

### Hook
From 2019 to 2022, two versions of me were working at the same time.
One had a face on television. The other had no name in the byline.

### Angle
When she joined Vantage Circle in late 2019, she hadn't left Doordarshan yet. For more than three years she ran both simultaneously: Talk Show Host on regional television in Guwahati — face, voice, recognizable — and anonymous B2B digital marketer writing HR content for an audience who had no idea she existed. Same city. Same person. Entirely different professional registers.

The broadcast self required legibility: you were a known entity, trust was earned through sustained visibility, presence was the instrument. The content self required precision: the work had to carry its own authority, no face to vouch for it, no name in the room. These are not the same skill set, and they were not reinforcing the same professional habits. Broadcast was asking, constantly, whether she was showing up fully. Content was asking, constantly, whether she was saying something worth showing up for. Both questions were good. Neither one asked itself in the other medium.

What she didn't anticipate: the division wasn't exhausting, it was clarifying. Having two modes of professional existence — one named, one not — revealed that professional identity is not singular. You don't have *a* professional self. You have the self the work requires. The two versions of her, running simultaneously in Guwahati from 2019 to 2022, were both accurate.

### Source
Overlap of Talk Show Host, Doordarshan Kendra, Guwahati (August 2013–December 2022) and Web Content Writer / Digital Marketing Executive, Vantage Circle (September–October 2019 onward) — more than three years of simultaneous employment in two entirely different professional modes. Entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. "What the Camera Trained" (June 16) examined skills transfer from broadcast to writing; "The Room That Talks Back" (June 20) examined the feedback loop difference. Neither addressed the specific experience of operating both in parallel — the two professional identities running simultaneously in the same city, the different demands each made, and what holding them at the same time revealed about professional selfhood.

### Resonance
Anyone who has held two genuinely different professional identities at once — people mid-career transition, practitioners who also write or teach, freelancers building something new while employed elsewhere. For her HR audience, this lands on a real gap in how organizations understand people: a job title is one version of a person; it is almost never the whole one. The professional carrying two identities knows this more clearly than most.

## Idea 3 — The First Survey

### Hook
The first real research I did on mass communication was going door-to-door in rural Guwahati to ask people if they'd seen the government's ads.
Most of them hadn't.

### Angle
September 2013. She was finishing her MA in Mass Communication at Gauhati University, had recently started as a production intern at Doordarshan, and was about to be hired as a host there. Under IIMC New Delhi, she went into rural areas outside Guwahati to survey whether Bharat Nirman campaign advertisements — a government rural development initiative — were reaching the people they were designed for. The answer, repeatedly, was no.

The campaigns weren't badly made. The gap wasn't in the production. It was in the distance between a message designed in a brief and the person standing at a door who had never encountered it.

She has spent the better part of a decade since then writing HR content — recognition programs, employee engagement frameworks, manager training material. The gap that keeps appearing is identical. Well-designed campaigns that don't land. Evidence-based frameworks that don't transfer. Sincere investments in employee experience that miss the specific person they were meant to reach. Not because the work is careless. Because building something for a persona is not the same as building it for the person.

She didn't know in September 2013 that she was getting the foundational lesson of her professional life. She was just a graduate student with a survey form, standing at a door in rural Assam, writing down what someone told her about a message they'd never received.

### Source
Research Campaign on Reach and Access of Bharat Nirman Advertisements, under IIMC New Delhi — Surveyor, September 2013, rural Guwahati. A one-month fieldwork assignment during her MA in Mass Communication, assessing whether government advertising was reaching its intended audience in rural Assam. Entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. No prior idea has examined this specific experience — the earliest career data point in her biography on the gap between communication intent and communication receipt — or traced the through-line from that September 2013 survey to the HR content question she's been working inside ever since.

### Resonance
HR practitioners and communications professionals for whom the gap between the program designed and the one received is a persistent live reality — the recognition initiative that didn't land, the training that didn't transfer, the engagement program that missed the room it was built for. The post grounds that gap in a specific early memory rather than a framework, which is the kind of grounding that makes an abstract professional problem feel real again.
