# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-02

## Idea 1 — The Headphone Listener

**Angle:** Moving from television to podcast production meant unlearning something specific about how attention works — the camera and the microphone ask for fundamentally different things from a host.

**Hook:**
On television, the camera gives you somewhere to put your face.
A microphone doesn't. And the listener is closer.

**Outline:**
- TV hosting trained her to manage presence visually: posture, eye contact, what to do with silence when it stretches; the viewer is at a distance, often in a room with other things going on
- Podcast listeners are wearing headphones — they are inside the audio, closer than any studio audience ever was; the relationship is intimate in a way that television rarely is
- What this required her to unlearn: the television habit of using visual cues to carry the pause, of filling space with expression; audio-only is unforgiving of performance that depends on being seen
- What it taught her instead: that clarity and warmth in the voice are not the same as warmth you project at a lens; they're built differently, held differently, and they land differently

**Tone:** Precise, quietly wondering

## Idea 2 — The Person Who Remembers

**Angle:** Six-plus years at one company means she is now often the person others ask when they want to know what happened the last time something was tried — and that is not a small thing.

**Hook:**
At some point in the last year, I became the person people ask.
Not for my title. For what I remember.

**Outline:**
- Institutional memory is not glamorous — it doesn't appear on a skills section and it's not what recruiters call when they're looking for something specific; it accumulates quietly and then suddenly it's useful
- Most workplaces have faster turnover than the projects they run; the person who was there when the first version of a strategy was tried, who knows why a particular decision was made and what it produced, is rarer than anyone admits
- She can say "we tried something like this in 2021" and mean it — not as a reason not to try again, but as actual data about how the organization moves and what it needs
- This is what staying builds that switching, no matter how many moves you make, cannot buy: the context that makes the new idea legible, the ability to tell the difference between a genuinely new problem and a recurring one in a different form

**Tone:** Grounded, quiet authority

## Idea 3 — What Actually Sticks

**Angle:** After years of writing about employee recognition for HR practitioners, she has a very specific observation: the recognition moments that stay with people almost never look like the programs designed to produce them.

**Hook:**
I've written more words about employee recognition than about almost anything else.
The ones that stay with me — the moments I actually remember — none of them came from a program.

**Outline:**
- Recognition program design focuses on structure: frequency, visibility, criteria, consistency — all of which matter and none of which is what a person remembers years later
- What people remember is specific and small: a manager who named the exact thing they did and said why it mattered; a colleague who noticed the quiet part of the work, the part that doesn't usually get counted
- The gap between what organizations build and what lands isn't a design failure — it's a category error; programs create infrastructure, but the actual moment of recognition is interpersonal and unrepeatable and cannot be scheduled
- She has spent years making the case for recognition to people who make these decisions; what she believes now is that the program's job is to create conditions, not moments — the moments happen between people, and no dashboard will ever capture whether they did

**Tone:** Honest, a little hard-won
