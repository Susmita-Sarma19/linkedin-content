# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-30

## Idea 1 — The Commerce Detour

### Hook
My undergraduate degree is in Commerce. My career is in communication, content, and marketing.
I spent years treating this as a redirect. I'm not sure it was.

### Angle
Gauhati Commerce College, 2007 to 2012. Five years studying how businesses work — how value is created, how money moves, how an organization sustains itself. Then Gauhati University, 2012 to 2014: MA in Mass Communication. A deliberate turn toward media, toward stories, toward what it means to make something for an audience.

The pivot felt, at the time, like a correction. She'd figured out what she actually wanted. The Commerce degree was the pre-history that didn't quite fit the narrative.

What she understands now: when she sits across a content brief at Vantage Circle, she is solving a business problem, not a writing problem. The commercial logic underneath the campaign — why this content needs to exist, who it is changing the mind of, what the company needs to happen downstream — is visible to her in a way that her Mass Communication training didn't build. The BCom built that. She didn't know she was still using it until she noticed she couldn't imagine doing the work without it.

The pivot wasn't a correction. It was an accumulation. She didn't leave commerce behind. She took it somewhere that didn't know to expect it.

### Source
Bachelor's degree, Business/Commerce, Gauhati Commerce College (2007–2012), followed by MA in Mass Communication, Gauhati University (2012–2014) — a deliberate academic shift from business to media. The five years of commerce education, and what that background contributes to a career in content marketing and digital strategy, is entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. Distinct from "The Media-to-Marketing Origin Story" (CLAUDE.md ideas bank #7), which examines the broadcast-to-marketing career arc. This is specifically the pre-media academic background: what studying organizations, value, and commercial logic before studying communication actually gave her — and how long it took to recognize it as an asset rather than a detour.

### Resonance
Professionals whose undergraduate degree feels like the thing they moved away from, who then discover it's been operating quietly inside the career they built after it. For her HR audience: the post speaks directly to how non-obvious academic backgrounds show up in communication and marketing roles — the BCom who can feel the business problem underneath a campaign, the engineer who writes with unusual precision about systems. The preparation that looks like a mismatch is sometimes the most specific one. Especially strong for L&D professionals who think about curriculum and career trajectory: the person often can't see the value of a prior chapter until they're deep enough into the next one.

## Idea 2 — The Other Side of the Airtime

### Hook
For a while I made television. Then I went to work for a company that helped people buy advertising space on it.
It took me some time to see what was strange about that sequence.

### Angle
August 2015. She moved to Kolkata and joined releaseMyAd — a platform that let advertisers book print, radio, and TV ad slots digitally. Media Relations Associate. For two years she helped clients get their campaigns into the places viewers would see them.

She was, at the time, simultaneously still hosting at Doordarshan.

This means she was part of the content that made the slot worth buying — and simultaneously working for the infrastructure that placed the purchase. The show that ran at 7 PM was what advertisers were paying for the space around. She was both.

She didn't put this together clearly at the time. You rarely do, while you're inside it. What the releaseMyAd years clarified — and what she carries into every content decision at Vantage Circle — is a specific understanding of what content value actually is. The advertiser isn't buying the airtime. They're buying the attention the content earned. Someone had to hold that attention first. The commercial infrastructure is downstream of the creative one. They're in the same transaction, on opposite ends of it.

She's been on both ends. The content she makes now looks different from that vantage point.

### Source
Talk Show Host, Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (August 2013–December 2022, overlapping with the Kolkata period), and Media Relations Associate, releaseMyAd.com (August 2015–July 2017), Kolkata — a digital platform for booking advertising space across print, radio, and TV media. The reversal of position from content-maker to the commercial infrastructure built around content — and what working both sides of the media transaction reveals about the relationship between attention and value — is entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. June 24's "The Metro Chapter" examined the Kolkata years through the lens of identity and geographic perspective. No prior draft has examined the releaseMyAd role itself, or the specific conceptual turn of working in ad-buying while simultaneously making the content that advertising dollars were built around.

### Resonance
Content creators, marketers, and communicators who have seen both sides of the creative-commercial divide — and who understand, from having been inside both rooms, that they're in the same building. For her HR audience: the post surfaces something live in how organizations think about content investment. Earned attention is not the same as ad spend. The person who has held both positions understands why the distinction matters, and can make the case for content quality in terms that the commercial logic will actually hear.

## Idea 3 — Both Sides of the Signal

### Hook
In September 2013, I was one month into my first television role.
That same month, I walked through villages outside Guwahati asking people whether they'd seen a government ad on TV.

### Angle
Research Campaign on Reach and Access of Bharat Nirman Advertisements. Under IIMC, New Delhi. September 2013. Rural Guwahati area. She was a surveyor — clipboard, doorsteps, questions about what reached people and what didn't.

She had been a Talk Show Host at Doordarshan for one month.

The specific doubling of those two facts didn't register fully in the moment. What registered: the person at the doorstep and the broadcast were not automatically the same thing. She was standing in a village asking whether the signal that she was now part of making — that went out from a studio not far from where she stood — had arrived. Sometimes it had. Sometimes it had arrived in form without arriving in meaning. The content had transmitted. Something else was required for it to be received.

This is not a technical problem. The frequency reached the antenna. What the research campaign clarified, on the ground, one month into broadcasting: transmission and reception are two separate events, and you can have the first without the second. A message goes out. Whether it lands is a different question, with different conditions, that the sender has to care about separately.

She has been working with that distinction — in broadcast, then in digital content, then in HR writing about recognition and engagement — for twelve years. Every piece of content she makes is an attempt to close the gap between the two. She learned there was a gap from a doorstep in rural Assam, a month into the career that would run on either side of it.

### Source
Research Campaign on Reach and Access of Bharat Nirman Advertisements (under IIMC, New Delhi), Surveyor, September 2013, Guwahati rural area — one month into her Talk Show Host career at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (August 2013–December 2022). The specific experience of being a broadcaster and simultaneously a researcher measuring whether broadcasts reach people — and what that double position in her first professional month clarified about transmission vs. reception — is entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. No prior idea has examined the IIMC surveyor role, or the conceptual ground it built: the distinction between a signal going out and a message arriving, which sits underneath her entire subsequent career in content, media, and HR communications.

### Resonance
HR practitioners, internal communicators, and L&D professionals who have sent the message and discovered it didn't land — and who work with the gap between transmission and receipt every time they design a program, write an all-hands, or launch an engagement initiative. The post names the gap not as a failure but as a structural condition: the signal leaving the studio and the signal arriving in a living room are always two separate events. You have to care about both, separately, to close the distance between them. Strong for anyone building communication strategies at scale who knows the distinction between "we shared it" and "they received it."
