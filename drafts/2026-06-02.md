# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-02

## Idea 1 — The Two-Medium Problem

**Angle:** TV builds presence; podcasting demands proximity — and those are opposite skills, even though they look the same from the outside.

**Hook:** "Television taught me presence. Podcasting taught me proximity. They look like the same skill. They're not."

**Outline:**
- Nine years of live broadcast wire you to project outward — to be bigger than the room
- A podcast is almost the opposite: the listener is alone with earbuds in, responding to intimacy, not performance
- The fluency she had to *unlearn* wasn't confidence — it was the polished variety of confidence that broadcast builds
- Expertise in one register doesn't transfer to a different one; sometimes it actively gets in the way

**Tone:** Counterintuitive, specific, quietly self-aware

## Idea 2 — The Theory That Arrived After the Practice

**Angle:** Studying PR a year into a broadcast career changes what you hear in every lecture — and carries a risk the standard sequence doesn't.

**Hook:** "I learned how perception works after I'd already been shaping it on television for a year. The theory hit differently."

**Outline:**
- Walking into a PR classroom with real experience means you're recognising patterns, not absorbing principles fresh
- The risk: practitioners stop asking *why* because they think they already know — beginners ask better questions
- The discipline she built: treat formal education as a mirror for experience, not just a credential stacked on top of it
- The diploma didn't teach her to communicate — it taught her to read her own communication as a skeptic would

**Tone:** Reflective, a little contrarian, honest about the trap

## Idea 3 — The Handover

**Angle:** The hardest part of moving from specialist to manager isn't the new responsibilities — it's learning to feel pride in work you didn't directly shape.

**Hook:** "For six years, the work I was most proud of had my fingerprints on every sentence. In April 2025, that changed."

**Outline:**
- As a specialist, ownership of craft *is* the measure of good work — six years of internalising that standard
- Management asks for the opposite: build conditions where someone else produces excellent work
- Nobody talks about the specific grief of that handover — not failure, not departure, just quiet
- Still learning to measure herself differently; the post ends without resolution because that's the honest version

**Tone:** Understated, specific, incomplete on purpose

## Idea 4 — The Interviewer's Habit

**Angle:** Nine years of getting paid to ask questions does something quiet to how you listen — and that posture shows up in everything downstream.

**Hook:** "The best thing hosting a talk show taught me wasn't how to speak. It was what to listen for."

**Outline:**
- Good questions are ones you're willing to throw away the moment the conversation moves somewhere more interesting
- When a guest dodges something, where they go instead is usually the actual story
- That habit shows up now in how she reads audience data and content briefs — not "what does this say" but "what is it not saying"
- Listening as a professional skill almost nobody formally develops, and why it changes the quality of everything downstream

**Tone:** Reflective, specific, quietly practical

## Idea 5 — The Invisible Draft

**Angle:** The HR content that actually changes how a manager treats their team is the piece that sounds like it required no effort — and took the most.

**Hook:** "The piece a manager used verbatim to recognise someone on their team — I rewrote it four times before it stopped sounding like content."

**Outline:**
- Good HR content hides its work; it has to sound natural enough to be spoken out loud in a real moment
- The performance of "authentic, unfiltered" writing is its own kind of pretense — polish and pretense are not the same thing
- The invisible draft is where actual thinking happens; effortlessness is the finish, not the method
- Writing for someone else's voice: it succeeds when it disappears completely

**Tone:** Craftsperson, understated, a little contrarian

## Idea 6 — When "Relatable" Is a Trap

**Angle:** Most content marketing treats relatability as a given — write what feels natural to you, and it lands. Writing across four languages for audiences she's not always part of broke that assumption early.

**Hook:** "I've written content in four languages for audiences I'm not always a part of. That changes what you think 'relatable' means."

**Outline:**
- Being multilingual means never being fully inside any single culture's reference points — you're always translating, always checking your assumptions
- Digital marketing often mistakes "sounds natural to me" for "lands with them" — those are very different things
- Writing across Assamese, Bengali, Hindi, and English trained a habit: what am I assuming is universal here? What is actually just familiar?
- What genuine relatability requires — not mirroring, but actually knowing who is on the other end and what they bring to the reading

**Tone:** Provocative, culturally specific, confident
