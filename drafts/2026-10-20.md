## Idea 1 — The Return

### Hook
Most people I know who left Guwahati for work never came back. I did. I'm not sure that made sense to anyone at the time — including me.

### Angle
Two years in Kolkata: media relations work, then a development office role, the particular atmosphere of a city that moves like its scale is proof of something. She learned real things. She also noticed, without it being said, what staying would eventually require — becoming the kind of professional that city is structured to produce.

She came back to Guwahati. Took a job that turned into six years, a body of work, and a career she built on her own terms. The premise that returning is settling is worth examining, because it rarely gets examined. Most career narratives treat proximity to the centre as evidence of seriousness. She tested that premise. The career she built at home turned out to be more interesting than the one she would have kept building in Kolkata — not because the city is wrong for everyone, but because she could see it clearly enough to know it wasn't right for her.

The work you do is not made better by the longitude you do it at. She's had six years to confirm that.

### Source
Kolkata years: releaseMyAd.com, Media Relations Associate (August 2015–July 2017); Subway Systems India Pvt. Ltd, Coordinator (March–July 2015). Return to Guwahati: SmartCoach, Creative Associate (January–September 2018); Vantage Circle, September 2019–present. Over two years in Kolkata, followed by a deliberate return and a career that grew significantly in Guwahati. Completely unexplored across all prior drafts and the ideas bank.

### Resonance
For professionals from smaller cities and non-metro regions — especially from the Northeast, where the pull toward metros is constant and the pressure to leave is social as much as economic — the decision to return is rarely told as the ambition story it actually is. For anyone who has built something worth building at home rather than migrating to the centre, this names a choice that doesn't often get named.

## Idea 2 — Before the Content

### Hook
Before I wrote content for a living, I spent a month going door to door in rural Assam asking families if they'd heard of Bharat Nirman. Most hadn't.

### Angle
September 2013. A government rural development scheme. A research mandate from IIMC New Delhi. A month in rural Guwahati with a survey form and a single question: did the communication reach the people it was built for? For most of it, the answer was no.

She has been writing about that gap ever since — in a different form, for a different audience, about a different kind of communication. Employee recognition content is, at its root, asking the same question: does this reach the person it was meant for? Does the message arrive, or does it circulate without landing?

She didn't understand it in those terms at twenty-two. She understands it now. The fieldwork wasn't a detour before the career. It was the intellectual origin of everything she's been doing since — the early encounter with the gap between what gets made and what gets received, long before she was the one doing the making.

### Source
Research Campaign on Reach and Access of Bharat Nirman Advertisements, under IIMC New Delhi — Surveyor, September 2013, Guwahati rural area. Bharat Nirman was a Government of India rural development communication initiative; the research examined whether its messaging was reaching intended rural audiences. Completely unexplored across all 149+ prior drafts. The connection between this early fieldwork and her later HR content work — both centered on whether communication actually lands — is a new frame entirely.

### Resonance
For content creators, communication professionals, and HR practitioners — the question every communication effort is really asking (did this reach someone, did it land?) is the one that gets asked last, if at all. The person who was measuring reach before they learned to produce it has a different relationship to the work. For professionals interested in purpose and origin stories that aren't linear, this surfaces a biographical detail that reframes the entire arc.

## Idea 3 — The PR Frame

### Hook
I studied public relations. The official name for what I do now is "content marketing." I've spent six years wondering if anyone told those two disciplines they're the same job.

### Angle
Public relations is the practice of managing how an organization is perceived over time — by its communities, its audiences, its own people. The PG Diploma in PR and Image Management she completed was not, technically, training for a content role. And yet.

Every piece of employee recognition content she writes is, underneath, a PR problem. How does this organization want to be seen by the people who work here? And does what they're actually doing match that claim? The gap between an organization's stated values and its employees' real experience is a reputation gap before it's an HR one. She writes into that gap every week.

Most content marketers think in traffic, ranking, conversion, reach. She does too. But below that is a question she learned to ask in a classroom about perception and trust: is the organization's reputation with its own people earned? The PR training never left. It just quietly changed what it was managing.

### Source
PG Diploma, Public Relations/Image Management, Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan's Rajendra Prasad Institute of Communication and Management, 2014–2015. Six-plus years producing employee recognition and engagement content at Vantage Circle (2019–present). The connection between her PR/Image Management academic formation and her HR content work — the disciplinary overlap between reputation management and employee recognition — has never been examined in any prior draft or ideas bank entry. Fresh angle.

### Resonance
For communication and marketing professionals who trained in adjacent disciplines (PR, journalism, advertising) — the formation you got in a different field is still shaping the field you landed in, usually without your naming it. For HR practitioners and content creators in the recognition space — the idea that employee recognition content is a reputation management problem reframes the work in a way that most platform-level conversations miss entirely.
