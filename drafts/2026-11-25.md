# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-11-25

## Idea 1 — The Year-End Recognition Myth

**Angle:** Recognition that arrives on a December schedule is ceremonial, not meaningful — and most companies can't tell the difference.

**Hook:** Every December, the same thing happens. Companies remember they haven't said thank you all year.

**Outline:**
- The annual awards cycle tells employees something unintentional: that gratitude is a Q4 budget item, not a daily practice
- Recognition science is unambiguous — frequency matters more than magnitude; a small, timely acknowledgment does more than a staged annual ceremony
- What leaders ask themselves in November ("did we recognize people enough this year?") is the wrong question; the right one is "did they feel it when it happened?"
- The year-end review season is a good time to audit the gap between what a company says it values and what it has actually made time for in the other eleven months

**Tone:** Quietly provocative

## Idea 2 — The Language I Think In

**Angle:** Moving between four languages daily isn't a communication skill — it's a way of stress-testing ideas from angles a monolingual mind can't reach.

**Hook:** When I'm drafting, I write in English. When I'm stuck, I think in Assamese.

**Outline:**
- Not all four languages carry the same cognitive texture — some are built for precision, some for texture, some for the relationship between speaker and listener
- The word that exists in one language and has no clean equivalent in another reveals something true: the concept itself is culturally held, not universal
- Code-switching mid-thought isn't confusion — it's reaching for the frame that holds the idea most honestly
- What years of this builds: a suspicion of the first translation, an ear for the word that almost fits but doesn't, a habit of checking the sentence in a second language before trusting it in the first

**Tone:** Reflective, curious

## Idea 3 — What Hosting Taught Me About Being a Guest

**Angle:** Nine years of holding the microphone, then sitting on the other side of it — the career that trains you to ask questions doesn't automatically teach you how to answer them.

**Hook:** For nine years, I was the one with the microphone. The first time someone turned it around, I didn't know what to do with my hands.

**Outline:**
- The interviewer's training is entirely about the other person: managing pace, listening past the first answer, holding silence long enough for something real to surface — none of which prepares you to be held
- What she had to unlearn: the instinct to redirect, to pivot toward the more interesting question, to rescue a moment that's going nowhere — all of which are exactly wrong when you're the guest
- The best interviewers she'd watched didn't perform ease; they created the conditions for it — a skill that looked nothing like what she'd been practicing
- Both sides of an interview are generosity work; she just hadn't practiced the direction that required her to give rather than receive

**Tone:** Warm, self-aware
