# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-11-14

---

## Idea 1 — The Off-Season Argument

**Angle:** The case for recognition that happens outside the annual performance cycle — and why December is the wrong month to care about it.

**Hook:** Every recognition program I've studied has one thing in common. It peaks in December. And that's the problem.

**Outline:**
- Recognition that arrives at year-end is processed as accounting, not acknowledgment — the employee knows when it was scheduled, and that changes what it means
- The moments employees actually remember — the late March deadline they carried alone, the August project no one noticed — happened in months that don't have an awards ceremony
- What de-seasonalizing recognition requires: not more programs, but managers who've been trained to notice in real time
- The HR practitioner's uncomfortable admission: a lot of recognition infrastructure exists to satisfy the organization's need to have *done recognition*, not the employee's need to feel seen

**Tone:** Quietly provocative

## Idea 2 — The Space Before the Answer

**Angle:** What nine years of live television interviewing taught about silence — and what the podcast edit undoes.

**Hook:** The best thing I ever learned to do in an interview was stop talking before the guest was done.

**Outline:**
- In live TV, silence is not dead air — it's the space where the guest finds the real answer, the one underneath the prepared one
- Nine years of Doordarshan talk shows trained her to read the pause: when it means "I'm still forming this" vs. when it means "I've said what I'll say"
- Podcast editing tends to optimize that pause away — tighter, denser, more efficient; what gets cut is often the moment the guest became themselves
- The discipline she's tried to hold: leave enough silence that the guest has somewhere to go that isn't the script they walked in with

**Tone:** Craft-reflective, specific

## Idea 3 — The Meeting That Justified the Job

**Angle:** The specific moment when an employee feels their work mattered — and how rarely recognition programs are designed to create it.

**Hook:** The best recognition I ever received wasn't an award. It was a meeting where something I'd built changed how we approached the problem.

**Outline:**
- Recognition programs are built to signal appreciation — which is real, but not the same as the feeling that your work was *consequential*
- Six years of writing about employee engagement have kept returning her to this gap: employees want to know their work connected to something, not just that it was noticed
- What she's observed: the moments employees hold onto are rarely about the ceremony, almost always about a specific, unrepeatable instant when their thinking changed the room
- The design challenge for HR practitioners: appreciation is easy to systematize; consequence is not — and confusing the two is why a lot of recognition lands flat even when it's sincere

**Tone:** Human-first, quietly searching
