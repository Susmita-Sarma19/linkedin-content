# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-04

## Idea 1 — The Unnamed Skill

**Angle:** The most transferable thing she gained from nine years of TV hosting — the discipline of drawing someone out past their prepared answer — has no standard job title attached to it, and that invisibility carries a quiet professional cost.

**Hook:** "The most useful thing I learned at Doordarshan doesn't have a job title attached to it."

**Outline:**
- Nine years of hosting built one central skill: making it safe for someone to say something true instead of something polished — and there is no LinkedIn category, no resume section, no job description field for that
- Skills without recognizable names tend to get spoken around rather than claimed; over time you undercount them in your own accounting of what you actually bring
- In podcast production, this unnamed skill is the whole work — the preparation that makes a guest stop delivering talking points, the follow-up question that arrives from whatever they actually said instead of whatever you planned
- The cost isn't that the skill doesn't matter; it's that you can carry something genuinely useful for years without ever learning to name it clearly enough to own it

**Tone:** Precise, with a quiet edge

## Idea 2 — The Register Problem

**Angle:** In six years of HR content, the variable she adjusts more than topic, length, or keyword strategy is emotional register — and almost no one teaches it.

**Hook:** "I've killed perfectly accurate pieces because the tone was wrong. Not the topic. Not the information. The way it felt when it landed."

**Outline:**
- HR content fails at the register level more than any other: technically correct, strategically reasonable, and somehow off — written for the org chart, not for the actual person reading it on a Tuesday afternoon
- Content education covers keywords, distribution, repurposing, SEO — almost nothing about the emotional temperature a piece needs to reach the reader it's actually meant for
- Her broadcast background gave her an early version of this instinct: a host can say a true thing and lose the room entirely based on how it was said; the content equivalent is the same failure in a different medium
- Where she's landed: before "is this accurate?" she now asks "what does this piece need to feel like?" — not sentiment, but register; the same fact can land as empowering or condescending depending on how you answer that question first

**Tone:** Diagnostic, practitioner-level — naming a gap others recognize but haven't quite articulated

## Idea 3 — What Belonging Looks Like Here

**Angle:** The HR industry's "employee belonging" frameworks were largely built for a particular kind of workplace — and growing up in Northeast India gives her a different baseline for what those words actually describe.

**Hook:** "Most of the belonging frameworks I write about every day were designed for workplaces that don't look like the ones I grew up around."

**Outline:**
- Corporate belonging content tends to assume a set of defaults: English as the working language, formal top-down hierarchy, urban geography, individualist career logic — not wrong, just presented as universal when it isn't
- Workplaces in Northeast India, particularly outside metros, hold different structures: the boundary between office and household is more porous, seniority dynamics are more contextual, the definition of "team" carries different weight and different obligations
- What that gives her: a sharp sense of when "culture" advice is actually specific-culture advice dressed as generic — and the ability to write for professionals whose real experience the standard playbook was never quite designed for
- She doesn't always surface this explicitly in the work. Sometimes she just uses it to adjust the frame. But it's present in every piece she writes that doesn't quietly assume the reader is in Bangalore.

**Tone:** Culturally grounded, undefensive — illuminating rather than correcting
