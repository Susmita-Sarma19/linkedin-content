## Idea 1 — The Expert Who Still Gets It Wrong

**Angle:** She writes about employee recognition for a living — the science, the structure, the language organizations use to see their people. She is also an employee. What it actually feels like to know exactly what recognition is supposed to do, and still find it complicated to receive.

**Hook:** I know more about why recognition matters than most people I'll ever work with. That doesn't make me better at receiving it.

**Outline:**
- Six years of writing about recognition — the research, the frameworks, the conditions that make it land vs. fall flat
- The particular awareness you carry: when someone recognizes you, you can observe the structure of it happening, notice whether it's specific or generic, feel the gap between performed and genuine
- That awareness doesn't make you more gracious — sometimes it makes you harder to reach
- Reflection: writing about what people need at work and actually experiencing it are closer than they look, and also further apart

**Tone:** Wry, self-aware

## Idea 2 — The Tier-2 Advantage

**Angle:** Everyone talks about what you sacrifice by not being in a metro. After six years of building a digital marketing career from Guwahati, she has a different set of observations.

**Hook:** Every year or so, someone in my industry asks if I've thought about relocating. I've thought about it. I've also thought about what I'd have to stop knowing.

**Outline:**
- What metros give: density of opportunity, event access, proximity to decision-makers, the feeling of being in the room
- What staying in Guwahati gave her: a different feedback loop, a career built on work rather than proximity, the absence of a commute as a tax on attention
- Digital work changed the terms — the question of where you are matters less than it did; what you produce travels
- Quiet point: she doesn't write for the Delhi reader or the Bangalore reader. She writes clearly. That's a Guwahati education.

**Tone:** Clear-eyed, quietly assertive

## Idea 3 — What Listening Teaches You

**Angle:** Running a podcast means spending hours listening before any content gets made. The specific craft of listening for what a guest is actually saying versus what they came prepared to say — and what that teaches about writing.

**Hook:** The best episodes I've made are the ones where I barely spoke.

**Outline:**
- Hosting a podcast isn't mainly talking — it's knowing when not to, holding space for the answer the person didn't plan to give
- The skill: distinguishing prepared statements from the thing the person actually thinks; both have value, they're not the same
- How deep listening shapes what she writes afterward — the HR concept that just came from a practitioner's offhand sentence, the framing that never would have appeared in research literature
- Reflection: everything she's written that she's most proud of started with someone else's words, properly heard

**Tone:** Contemplative, craft-focused
