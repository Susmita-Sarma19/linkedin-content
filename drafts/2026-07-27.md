# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-27

## Idea 1 — The Narrow Territory

**Angle:** Six years of writing exclusively about employee engagement and recognition without going stale — what specialization in a tight topic area actually demands, and why the work gets harder, not easier.

**Hook:** I have been writing about employee engagement, in some form, since 2019. Same territory. Roughly the same core questions. If anything, the focus has gotten narrower, not wider. And the writing has gotten harder, not easier.

**Outline:**
- The common assumption: that deep expertise eventually makes writing easier — you know the field, you know what's worth saying, the work flows
- What actually happens after a few years: the easy observations are taken, the surface angles are mapped, every new piece has to clear a higher bar because the prior work set it
- What "not going stale" actually requires: not chasing what's new in the field, but going deeper on what's unresolved — the contested claims, the practitioner experience that doesn't match the research, the question everyone assumes is settled but isn't
- Six years in, the discipline is harder because the territory is better understood; that's what specialization is supposed to produce, not a sign something is wrong

**Tone:** Quietly counterintuitive, honest, specific

## Idea 2 — The Doordarshan Formation

**Angle:** Nine years at a public broadcaster — whose mandate is reach and public service, not ratings — left a permanent frame for what communication is actually for, one that sits oddly inside digital marketing.

**Hook:** Doordarshan isn't chasing your attention. That's the institution's premise, not a design flaw. Nine years of working inside that logic changed what I thought communication was for — and I haven't been able to unlearn it since.

**Outline:**
- What public broadcasting actually optimizes for: not audience retention or time-on-platform, but the quality of what's offered to the audience it's mandated to serve — a different success criterion that lives in every production decision
- The working question at Doordarshan wasn't "will this perform" — it was closer to "does this deserve the time of the person watching" — and those questions lead you to different places
- The dissonance when she moved into digital: a landscape organized entirely around attention metrics, where "deserving to be watched" was almost never the framework
- The Doordarshan logic didn't leave when she left the institution; it runs underneath the metric-facing question — not just "did they watch" but "was it worth their time" — and that's a harder standard to meet and a more honest one

**Tone:** Structurally curious, genuine, not nostalgic

## Idea 3 — No Industry Here

**Angle:** Building a digital marketing career from Guwahati means never having been in the room where the informal knowledge transfer happens — and accounting honestly for what that absence cost and what it forced her to develop instead.

**Hook:** There is no marketing industry in Guwahati. There are marketers. But no conferences, no co-working spaces full of peers building adjacent things, no informal knowledge transfer over lunch with someone who learned the same way. You figure out what you know by working, not by being surrounded.

**Outline:**
- What professional isolation actually produces: you work from first principles more often than you'd choose to, because borrowing frameworks from people around you isn't an option when those people aren't there
- Most professional development content has a geography built into it that doesn't name itself — the conference talks, the case studies, the career-advice posts were made by and for metro contexts, and the gap shows
- What it produces in the actual work: a kind of independent judgment that metro professionals sometimes read as confidence; it isn't confidence — it's the absence of social proof, the result of making decisions about what's worth doing without a community to validate them
- The cost that doesn't get named: it's slower, lonelier, and you never fully know whether what you've built would hold up in a room full of people who learned differently — that uncertainty doesn't go away, you just keep working anyway

**Tone:** Honest, undefensive, specific — not a grievance narrative
