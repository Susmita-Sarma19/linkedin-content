# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-11-08

## Idea 1 — The Visible and the Invisible

**Angle:** Nine years on-camera as a woman in regional TV versus six years invisible behind content — what each form of professional visibility demands, and what it costs.

**Hook:** The first time I appeared on television, someone told me to smile more. The first article I published at Vantage Circle, most readers had no idea who wrote it. I've spent my career on both sides of that tradeoff. The pressures are completely different. So is what you have to build inside yourself to survive them.

**Outline:**
- On-camera visibility as a woman in regional broadcasting: you are watched, assessed, and held to standards that have nothing to do with whether your content is good
- The shift to written content work — professionally invisible, behind the byline, evaluated by what the piece does rather than what you look like doing it
- What each kind of visibility teaches: on-camera taught resilience and presence; invisible-behind-content taught whether the work could actually stand on its own
- The question she still turns over: which version of professional visibility was she actually building toward — and whether that question has an answer

**Tone:** Quiet, observational, slightly wry — not a complaint, a reflection

## Idea 2 — Recognition Is Not a Universal Language

**Angle:** Most employee recognition frameworks were designed with a specific worker in mind — and writing about recognition while being from outside that assumed center is a vantage most recognition content doesn't have.

**Hook:** Every recognition program I've ever written about was designed to make employees feel seen. Most of them were designed by someone with one specific idea of what "being seen" means.

**Outline:**
- The standard recognition playbook: public praise, award ceremonies, shoutouts in all-hands — built around visibility as universally desirable
- What recognition research doesn't often surface: the employee for whom public acknowledgment is uncomfortable, or culturally coded differently, or simply doesn't land the way the program expects
- Her position: six years writing recognition content for a "national" audience while being from a region those frameworks weren't designed for — she can see the gap between who the program serves and who it claims to
- What it would take for recognition to actually be personal: not just adding more programs, but questioning the assumptions about acknowledgment that are already built in

**Tone:** Incisive, calm — practitioner conviction without lecturing

## Idea 3 — What Podcast Guests Say in the Last Two Minutes

**Angle:** Hosting a podcast about HR and workplace culture for three years produced a specific kind of knowledge the research literature doesn't surface — what practitioners actually believe about work, said when the conversation stops performing.

**Hook:** The most useful thing I've heard about employee recognition in three years of podcasting, a guest said in the last two minutes of recording. Almost as an afterthought. We'd already wrapped the formal part.

**Outline:**
- The difference between what HR leaders say in keynotes and what they say when a recording has been running for 45 minutes and they've stopped monitoring themselves
- What podcast hosting (as distinct from TV hosting) produces: the intimacy of audio, the absence of cameras, the long-form conversation that creates space for honesty without performance
- The pattern she's noticed: the candid observation — the one that actually helps — almost always comes when the guest thinks they're winding down
- What this tells her about the gap between HR best practice as formally communicated and HR reality as lived: the gap is real, it's known, and practitioners are more honest about it than the content they publish suggests

**Tone:** Warm, insider — the person who has heard things the public version of this conversation doesn't include
