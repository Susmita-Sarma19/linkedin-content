# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-11

## Idea 1 — The Bridge

### Hook
I have a role on my resume that doesn't fit any narrative arc I could tell. Nine months. A local firm in Guwahati. Between Kolkata and everything that followed. I've been meaning to explain it.

### Angle
After two years doing media relations in Kolkata, she came back to Guwahati in 2018 and took a Creative Associate role at SmartCoach — content, branding, and advertising work at local scale, with none of the industry visibility the Kolkata chapter had offered. On a conventional career graph, it's a lateral move at best, a step back at worst. But before she committed to the next long chapter — six-plus years building something at Vantage Circle — she had nine months of quiet testing: can I do this kind of work here, without the ecosystem, without the cluster? The answer the nine months gave her was enough. Career narratives jump from the high points to the next high points. The bridge in between — unglamorous, specific, provisional — gets skipped. The post sits with what the bridge is actually for: not proving yourself, but finding out whether you want to get to the other side.

### Source
Creative Associate, SmartCoach, January–September 2018, Guwahati. Content, branding, and advertising. Nine months immediately after the Kolkata chapter (releaseMyAd, 2015–2017) and preceding Vantage Circle (September 2019). The 2026-05-22 "Kolkata Chapter" mentioned SmartCoach only as context for the return decision; this is the first idea to make SmartCoach itself the subject — the transitional role as its own thing, not a stepping stone to something more important.

### Resonance
Anyone currently in a role between chapters — the short, unglamorous stint that doesn't make the story legible from the outside. The post reframes the bridge job not as time to endure until the real thing arrives, but as the specific place where conviction gets tested rather than displayed. Short roles aren't always failures of commitment; sometimes they're exactly the right length.

## Idea 2 — Theory Last

### Hook
I studied PR and communication formally in 2014. I'd been a talk show host for a year. Every time the textbook explained how something works, I already had a specific episode to confirm it — or argue with it.

### Angle
Most professional education is designed to precede practice: theory first, then application. She reversed this. One year into hosting at Doordarshan, she enrolled in a PG Diploma in Public Relations and Image Management — formal study of communication from inside an active communication practice. The frameworks didn't arrive to be filed away for later. They landed against experience she could immediately run them against. What that reversal produces is a specific kind of learning that pure classroom study rarely does: you stop accepting theory on faith, because every abstraction either fits a case you already know or doesn't. The things that held up, held up harder. The things that didn't, she questioned in a way she couldn't have if she'd arrived without the evidence. Theory absorbed after practice is filtered through experience you're already carrying. What you retain is different — and what you push back on is different too.

### Source
PG Diploma, Public Relations/Image Management, Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan's Rajendra Prasad Institute Of Communication And Management, 2014–2015. Enrolled one year after beginning as Talk Show Host at Doordarshan Kendra in August 2013. A deliberate return to formal education from inside an active professional practice. The 2026-06-05 "Image Manager's Blind Spot" used the PG Diploma as context but focused on applying the expertise to others vs. to herself. This is the first idea to examine the timing itself: what changes when study comes after practice rather than before it.

### Resonance
Professionals who've returned to study from inside a career already underway — or who've wondered what formal education offers once you have real cases to test it against. The post names something almost never discussed: the direction of learning matters. Classroom before field, and field before classroom, are genuinely different educations. For anyone who felt that the most useful theory arrived after the experience it was meant to explain, this will land precisely.

## Idea 3 — The Unexpected Curriculum

### Hook
I've run enough podcast interviews to know there's what someone says when they're prepared, and what they say after they've stopped being careful. That gap is where I've learned most about employee experience.

### Angle
The Vantage Circle podcast exists to amplify HR voices — and that's what it does. But there's a transfer of knowledge that runs the other direction, which never gets talked about: what she has learned from the HR managers, people ops leads, and practitioners she puts on the mic. Research papers describe what the literature says. HR practitioners, past the prepared answer and into the honest part of the conversation, describe what's actually true in a room on a Tuesday morning. Dozens of those conversations have built a specific calibration for what HR professionals are actually carrying — what they believe in and what they're tired of, what the frameworks miss and where the real difficulty sits — that no literature review replicates. When she writes about employee experience without being an HR practitioner, this is part of what the writing is drawing on. Not the credential. The curriculum.

### Source
Ongoing podcast production and interviewing at Vantage Circle — her role producing and conducting interviews with HR practitioners across episodes. The 2026-06-08 "Person Behind the Voice" examined the editorial inversion (from on-camera host to off-camera producer drawing out others' expertise). The 2026-06-10 "Writing from One Remove" examined her epistemic position as a non-practitioner writing authoritatively about HR. This is the first idea to examine the specific, concrete knowledge that sustained podcast interviewing has built — the curriculum that flows in from the guests, not the platform she gives out.

### Resonance
Writers, journalists, and B2B content practitioners who build genuine domain knowledge through sustained qualitative engagement with practitioners. The post names something real about what repeated, honest interview work actually produces: a kind of knowing that isn't formal expertise and isn't the credential, but isn't nothing. For her HR audience specifically, it offers a perspective they don't usually see — what the person writing about their field has actually absorbed from talking to them.
