# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-15

## Idea 1 — What Good Recognition Actually Sounds Like

**Angle:** The difference between recognition that lands and recognition that doesn't is specificity — and specificity is hard because it requires you to have actually been paying attention.

**Hook:** A "great job" from someone who watched you do it is worth twenty "great jobs" from someone who read the report.

**Outline:**
- Most recognition messages fail not because people don't care, but because they default to generic language — and the person receiving it can feel the difference immediately
- What "specific" actually means: naming the behavior, the context, the impact *you personally observed* — not the output, but the thing you watched happen
- Why this is harder than it looks: specificity requires the recognizer to have been present and paying attention, which is the real ask
- The quiet irony in recognition technology: platforms help scale the *frequency* of recognition, but scaling the gesture doesn't solve the specificity problem — it can make it worse

**Tone:** Direct, warm, grounded in domain expertise

## Idea 2 — The Pause Before the Question

**Angle:** The most underrated skill in a podcast interview is knowing when not to speak — and it took years of television to unlearn the instinct to fill silence.

**Hook:** Television trained me to be afraid of silence. Podcasting taught me that silence is where the real answer lives.

**Outline:**
- TV has a pathological relationship with dead air — nine years of live broadcast wired her to bridge gaps, keep energy moving, never let a pause breathe
- Audio-only format forced her to relearn: a guest who pauses before answering is almost always about to say something true
- The practical thing she now does: count to five internally before asking a follow-up — what surfaces after that pause is almost always more honest than what came before it
- This isn't just a podcast skill — the same instinct to rush past silence shows up in feedback conversations, performance discussions, any moment someone needs space to say the real thing

**Tone:** Precise, a little wry, craft-focused

## Idea 3 — When the Numbers Say One Thing

**Angle:** When engagement scores go up and the open-text comments still say "I feel invisible," you've measured the wrong distance.

**Hook:** We hit one of our highest engagement scores in Q3. The open-text section told a different story entirely.

**Outline:**
- Engagement scores measure responses to survey questions — they're not the same as measuring how people actually feel about their work
- The open-text field is where the contradiction shows up: the thing someone finally decided to say, after they filled in the scale questions the way they thought they should
- Practitioners who use engagement data well treat scores as a starting point, not a finding — the number tells you where to look, not what you'll find
- What this means for recognition and culture programs: if your program improves the metric without changing the underlying experience, you've optimized the measurement, not the workplace

**Tone:** Quietly provocative, professionally grounded
