# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-11-06

## Idea 1 — What Recognition Actually Sounds Like

**Angle:** Six years of writing about employee recognition research has taught Susmita one thing the frameworks consistently miss — the moments employees cite when describing feeling genuinely seen are almost never the ones the program was designed to produce.

**Hook:** The recognition story that's stayed with me longest from six years in this field didn't involve a ceremony, a leaderboard, or a gift card. It involved a manager who remembered something small.

**Outline:**
- The gap between what recognition programs are built to deliver (consistency, scale, documented impact) and what employees actually describe when asked about a time they felt genuinely valued
- The specific pattern that keeps appearing across practitioner interviews: the moments that land are small, immediate, and show evidence that someone paid attention — not that someone followed a process
- What this means for how organizations design recognition vs. how they measure it — you can run a program that scores well on every metric and still not produce the experience the program was for
- Reflection: the best recognition might be un-programmable, which is uncomfortable news for a field that makes software for it — and interesting context for what her work is actually trying to accomplish

**Tone:** Quietly honest, substantive

## Idea 2 — When the Concept Doesn't Translate

**Angle:** Working in a professional field (HR tech) that operates almost entirely in English, while thinking and living across Bengali, Hindi, Assamese, and English — some core concepts she works with every day don't have clean equivalents in her other three languages, which means they're more culturally constructed than the literature admits.

**Hook:** I work with "psychological safety" every day. I've written about it, researched it, explained it to practitioners. It doesn't have a clean equivalent in any of my other three languages. That's worth paying attention to.

**Outline:**
- The specific concepts from HR and employee engagement — psychological safety, belonging, recognition, engagement itself — that resist clean translation across the four languages she works in daily
- What the absence of a word reveals: not that the experience doesn't exist in those cultures, but that the concept is sliced and packaged differently — which means every "best practice" is more culturally located than the universal framing implies
- How this shapes the way she reads research claims: when a study says "employees feel more engaged when X," she asks whose employees, thinking in which language, in what kind of organizational culture
- Reflection: multilingualism isn't just a communication advantage — it's a built-in epistemological check on claims that assume their own universality

**Tone:** Curious, quietly provocative

## Idea 3 — The Expert Outside the Room

**Angle:** She writes about workplace culture and employee experience from Guwahati — where most of the reference cases (the corporate HR cultures of Mumbai, Bangalore, Delhi) are places she studies but doesn't inhabit daily. That specific outsider position produces a different kind of expertise than the one you get from being inside any of those rooms.

**Hook:** Most of the workplaces I write about look nothing like the city I write in.

**Outline:**
- The practical reality: she produces content about corporate workplace culture, employee experience, and HR practice for organizations whose environments — office culture, scale, industry composition, workforce demographics — are very different from the professional context she works in in Guwahati
- What this outside position actually requires: she can't rely on ambient observation or cultural proximity to the reference market; she has to build her picture entirely from deliberate research, practitioner interviews, and primary literature
- What that requirement produces: a writer who is never coasting on "this is what it's like" — she can't. Everything she claims has to be grounded in something she actually examined, because her daily environment doesn't confirm or deny it for her the way a Bangalore office-goer's might
- Reflection: the expert outside the room is not at a disadvantage — but they're doing a different kind of work than the expert who can step out for lunch and observe their subject matter. That work has its own standards, its own discipline, its own specific humility.

**Tone:** Honest, grounded, low-key confident
