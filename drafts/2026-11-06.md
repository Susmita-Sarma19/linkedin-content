## Idea 1 — The First Research Question

### Hook
My first paid research job was a one-month survey in rural Assam.
I was asking strangers whether a government ad had reached them.
I'm still asking the same question — just in a different domain.

### Angle
In September 2013, while finishing her postgrad at Gauhati University, Susmita spent a month in rural Guwahati conducting field research under IIMC supervision — a campaign studying the reach and access of Bharat Nirman government advertisements. The question the study was built around: does this communication actually reach the people it's supposed to? Not "is this content good?" Not "did we publish it?" But: did the signal find its receiver?

That is structurally identical to the question she works with every day at Vantage Circle. Does this recognition program actually reach the employee who needs to feel seen? Does this content reach the manager who doesn't know what to do differently? The domain changed. The question didn't.

The post reflects on what it means to discover your career's core question only in retrospect — that she didn't set out to specialize in whether institutional communication reaches the people it claims to serve. It's just the question she kept returning to, in every format and every audience, from the time she was 22 and walking door to door in rural Assam with a clipboard.

### Source
Research Campaign on Reach and Access of Bharat Nirman Advertisements, under IIMC, New Delhi, September 2013, Guwahati (rural) area. Her one-month role as Surveyor — the only academic/research credit in her background and the first professional engagement that preceded her Doordarshan career. Never referenced in any prior draft or the ideas bank.

### Resonance
The professional who can name their career's core question often discovers they were holding it from the beginning — before they had the vocabulary, before they had the strategy. That coherence, recognized in retrospect, is both grounding and clarifying. For her HR audience: the gap between "did we publish it" and "did it reach them" is precisely where most recognition programs fail. Someone whose first research question was about that gap has been trained to see it.

## Idea 2 — The Return

### Hook
In 2018, I came back to Guwahati.
Most career narratives I read are about leaving.
Mine is about what became possible when I stopped.

### Angle
After two years in Kolkata at a commercial media company, she returned to Guwahati — not because she had to, but because she chose to. The standard career script reads: build your credentials in a metro, grow your network there, stay because that's where careers compound. She read that script and came home anyway.

What the return made possible: she built her most sustained, most substantive professional work at a company where she didn't have to perform a metro-professional identity she didn't hold. She was writing from a place she actually understood, for audiences she could see without flattening — because she wasn't embedded in any single slice of corporate culture and wasn't spending energy fitting into one.

The post asks a quiet version of the question: what does it actually cost you, professionally, to build your career somewhere that isn't the center? And in her case, the answer was: nothing she needed. The work she's most proud of — six years of content, a podcast, an audience across the HR practitioner community — was built in Guwahati. The metro wasn't necessary. It was a chapter. Home was where the work happened.

### Source
Susmita's full geographic career arc: Guwahati (education, Doordarshan production intern, early Talk Show Host) → Kolkata (Subway Systems 2015, releaseMyAd 2015–2017) → Guwahati (SmartCoach 2018, Vantage Circle 2019–present). The decision to return after the Kolkata chapter, and what it enabled professionally, has never been examined in any prior draft or the ideas bank. Distinct from all Northeast Voice, Northeast Address, and regional representation angles — this is a geographic career-choice post, not a representation post.

### Resonance
For professionals navigating geographic career decisions — especially those from smaller cities considering whether to leave or stay: the periphery that career advice says you should leave is also the place where some people do their clearest work, because they're not allocating energy to performing the center. The return isn't a retreat. For some people, it's a selection — the place where the conditions are actually right to build something that lasts.

## Idea 3 — The Public Mandate

### Hook
For nine years, I made content that wasn't trying to convert anyone.
Doordarshan doesn't have a growth team.
That turned out to matter a lot.

### Angle
Doordarshan Kendra is public broadcasting. The mandate isn't to acquire users, move numbers on a growth dashboard, or optimize for conversion. The mandate is to serve — to be present for communities that have a right to broadcast in their own language, about their own region, on their own terms. That is a different relationship between content and audience than any commercial context produces.

She held that relationship for nine years before she moved into digital marketing — a field organized almost entirely around performance metrics, attribution, and funnel movement. The adjustment wasn't just technical. It was a question of who content is fundamentally for: the institution that publishes it, or the person who receives it.

The nine years in public broadcasting left her with a measure that most content professionals don't carry into their careers: before "does it convert," she asks "does it serve." Those two questions usually point in the same direction. When they don't — and sometimes they don't — knowing which one she trusts matters. It's the question she brings into every piece she writes now. And it came from nine years at a broadcaster that never had a reason to ask anything else.

### Source
Talk Show Host, Doordarshan Kendra, Guwahati (August 2013 – December 2022) — nine years in public broadcasting under a public interest mandate, followed by her digital marketing and HR content career at Vantage Circle. The specific contrast between a public broadcasting audience relationship (serve, don't convert) and commercial content's performance logic, and what that embedded in her as a practitioner standard, has never appeared in any prior draft or ideas bank entry. Entirely distinct from all existing Doordarshan angles: the Northeast Address, the Parallel Act, comfort vs. trust, the interview craft posts, the no-retake instinct.

### Resonance
For content professionals and marketers: the person who learned to ask "does this serve its reader" before "does this convert" carries a different standard into every dashboard. For her HR audience: internal communications, recognition programs, and culture initiatives face the same question — does this serve the employee it claims to be for, or does it serve the organization's interest in appearing to do that? Those are not identical, and you need a measure outside the metrics to tell the difference. Nine years in public broadcasting is one way to build that measure.
