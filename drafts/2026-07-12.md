# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-12

## Idea 1 — The Field Survey

### Hook
In September 2013, I had been at Doordarshan for one month.
That same month, someone sent me into rural Guwahati to find out whether people out there were watching government television.

### Angle
The IIMC, New Delhi commissioned a field research campaign on the reach and access of Bharat Nirman advertisements — government-funded development advertising intended to reach rural audiences. She was the surveyor. She went into villages in rural Guwahati and asked people whether they were receiving the message. She had just started making the message.

What those surveys documented wasn't a technical failure. The signal was reaching the television sets. What wasn't reaching was the frame the content assumed — the viewer the ad was speaking to, living in a context the production room had imagined but not verified. Technically present. Not actually landing.

She went back to Doordarshan and made her next episode. She's been thinking about the gap between those two things ever since. Every piece of HR content she produces carries the same question from that month: is it reaching, or is it just being broadcast? The difference is not in the transmission. It's in whether the work was designed for the person actually receiving it.

### Source
Research Campaign on Reach and Access of Bharat Nirman Advertisements, under IIMC, New Delhi — Surveyor, September 2013, rural Guwahati. One month into her Talk Show Host career at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati. Entirely unexplored across all prior drafts. The biographical specificity is precise: she was simultaneously making public service broadcast content and conducting field research into whether public service broadcast content was reaching its intended audience. The meta-irony of that simultaneity, and what the field work clarified about the gap between production logic and reception reality, has never appeared in any form.

### Resonance
For HR practitioners, internal communicators, and anyone producing content meant to reach a specific audience: the gap between "we published it" and "it landed" is the foundational problem. A recognition program, a manager communication guide, a training module — technically distributed through all the right channels, and still somehow not getting through. The post names that gap from someone who first understood it at 22, in a field survey in rural Guwahati, one month into her first real media job.

## Idea 2 — The Amplifier

### Hook
My job title says "Amplifying HR Voices."
I spent nine years being the voice. Choosing to swap sides was not a small thing.

### Angle
At Doordarshan, the camera was on her. The episode bore her presence. The professional recognition, such as it was, came attached to her face.

At Vantage Circle, she builds and hosts a podcast where the voices are HR practitioners, researchers, and people leaders. She is the one who designed the show, developed the questions, produced the audio. She also, deliberately, put other people at the center of what listeners remember. Her work is in the structure. Someone else's insight is in the transcript.

The line on her profile — "Amplifying HR Voices" — reads as a job description. It also records a choice she made about what to do with nine years of on-camera experience. She didn't build a platform for herself. She built infrastructure for other people's expertise to be heard more clearly.

She's not certain she chose this consciously the first time. She knows she's chosen it again every time since, including the times she could have positioned it differently. There is a specific kind of professional satisfaction in making someone else's insight more legible than they could have made it on their own. It doesn't look like the nine years before it. It's harder to point to. She thinks it's more useful.

### Source
Her role producing and hosting the Vantage Circle podcast (2019–present) — specifically the decision to build a show that centers the expertise of HR practitioners as guests, coming directly from nine years as a recognizable on-camera talent at Doordarshan. The identity shift from visible broadcaster to invisible amplifier — the professional meaning of that choice, and why someone with substantial broadcast presence would build something where other people's voices are the deliverable — has not appeared in any prior draft. Distinct from "The Discipline of Staying Out of the Way" (June 27), which examined the craft of skilled self-effacement in production, and "The Format That Changed" (July 6), which examined medium differences. Neither examined the identity choice itself.

### Resonance
Content professionals, communicators, and practitioners who have shifted from being the subject of attention to building the infrastructure that makes someone else the subject. For her HR audience: the internal communicator, the HR business partner, the L&D professional — every professional whose work is making other people more effective and heard. The amplifier identity is theirs too. The post names what it means to choose it deliberately, rather than arrive at it by default, and what that choice looks like from the inside.

## Idea 3 — The Return

### Hook
I left Guwahati for Kolkata in early 2015.
I came back two and a half years later. People read it as circumstances. It was a decision.

### Angle
March 2015 to July 2017. Two cities, two professional contexts, two years of calibrating herself against a metro that moved at a different speed and spoke in a different professional register. The experiences were real — the franchise development work, two years in media relations, the particular thing Kolkata teaches a professional from the Northeast about scale, network, and what the center of an industry actually feels like.

Then January 2018: she came back.

The conventional professional logic available at the time suggested staying. Kolkata had the organizational density, the career ladder rungs, the proximity to how decisions in her sector got made. Guwahati had less of all of it, in that accounting.

What two and a half years in Kolkata clarified was not that the metro was wrong. It clarified the shape she wanted a career to take — the depth, the rootedness, the particular relationship between place and work that she was trying to build. Some of that becomes visible only from a distance. She came home knowing something she hadn't been able to see while still there.

The six years she's built from Guwahati since are the argument for the decision. She can't make it the other way. She can only say that some choices look like retreat from the outside until they reveal themselves, from the inside, as foundation.

### Source
The Kolkata period (March 2015–July 2017, Subway and releaseMyAd) and the deliberate return to Guwahati (January 2018, SmartCoach), followed by everything built at Vantage Circle (September 2019–present) from Guwahati. While "The Metro Chapter" (June 24) examined geographic identity and professional register during the Kolkata years, and "The Gap That Isn't There" (July 10) examined the five-month unrecorded interval after Kolkata, neither has examined the decision itself — what the distance clarified, and what coming home meant as a deliberate professional choice rather than a circumstantial one.

### Resonance
Professionals who have left their home city for a metro and returned — and who have had that return read as concession rather than choice. For her HR audience on talent and retention: the professionals most likely to stay are not the ones with nowhere else to go. They're the ones who looked at somewhere else closely enough to understand what they were choosing when they came back. Retention that depends on limiting options is not the same as retention that reflects a genuine choice. The post names the difference from someone who made that choice, clearly, in January 2018.
