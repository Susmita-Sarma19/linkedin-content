# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-12

## Idea 1 — The Interviewer's Ear

**Angle:** Nine years of conducting TV interviews built a muscle most content and marketing people never develop — listening past the first answer.

**Hook:** I spent nearly a decade asking people what they really meant. Turns out, most people don't say it on the first try.

**Outline:**
- On television, the answer to your first question is almost never the real one — you learn to wait, probe, sit with silence
- In marketing, the brief that lands in your inbox is rarely what the client actually needs; the real need surfaces two conversations in
- In HR content, what employees put in a survey is just the beginning — the texture is underneath
- The discipline of interviewing isn't about asking smart questions; it's about listening past the polished answer to the one that matters

**Tone:** Quietly observant

## Idea 2 — The Empty Award

**Angle:** Working inside employee recognition every day gives a clear view of what passes for appreciation — and why the performative kind can do more damage than silence.

**Hook:** The biggest mistake companies make with employee recognition isn't that they don't do it. It's that they do it while thinking about themselves.

**Outline:**
- Recognition that's designed for the company's comfort — the public ceremony, the generic certificate — not for what the employee actually needs
- The specificity problem: "great job" acknowledges output; naming *what* someone did and *why* it mattered acknowledges a person
- The timing problem: recognition that arrives weeks late, or as a formality tied to an annual cycle, lands differently than a moment of genuine noticing
- What real recognition requires isn't a platform or a policy — it's attention, and attention is hard to scale

**Tone:** Practitioner-honest

## Idea 3 — What Guwahati Taught Me That Bangalore Couldn't

**Angle:** Building a digital career from a Tier-2 city isn't a compromise. It turns out to be a structural advantage that metro workers don't always see they're missing.

**Hook:** People used to ask if I'd ever consider moving to a metro for my career. I used to wonder if I should.

**Outline:**
- When you're not surrounded by the same industry echo chamber, you develop an instinct for what's real versus what's trend-chasing
- The slower pace in a smaller city isn't lower productivity — it's the condition for deeper thinking, which digital work actually rewards
- Building relationships and a reputation without the shortcuts that density gives you forces you to be more intentional
- The irony of digital-first careers: geography matters less and less, but the perspective you carry from your place matters more and more

**Tone:** Grounded, gently contrarian
