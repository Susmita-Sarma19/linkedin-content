# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-20

## Idea 1 — The Format That Teaches You

**Angle:** Writing lets her edit. Podcasting doesn't. That asymmetry — the inability to tidy up a guest's thinking after the fact — turns out to be the most revealing tool she has for understanding what practitioners actually know versus what they've been trained to say.

**Hook:** Writing about recognition lets me edit. Podcasting doesn't. I didn't realize how different that would make the knowledge.

**Outline:**
- In writing, she shapes ideas after they form — restructures, cuts, clarifies; the finished piece doesn't show the thinking, only its cleaned-up result
- In a podcast recording, she watches a practitioner think in real time, uneditable, including the moments they reach for a word and don't find it
- What lives in those unedited moments is different from what appears in polished frameworks: the pause before "honestly," the sentence a guest starts and redirects — this is where the tacit knowledge leaks out
- The constraint of the format isn't a limitation to manage; it's a precision instrument for finding what someone knows before they've had the chance to package it

**Tone:** Craft-curious, a little revelatory

## Idea 2 — Eight Hundred Times

**Angle:** She has written the word "culture" approximately eight hundred times across six years of HR content. She still doesn't think most people using it mean the same thing — and that problem sits at the centre of why she does the work.

**Hook:** I have written the word "culture" approximately eight hundred times. I still don't think most people who use it are describing the same thing.

**Outline:**
- "Culture" is the most used and least defined term in her field — a word that once named something specific (how things actually work around here, not how they're described in a handbook) and has been stretched to cover everything from values posters to Glassdoor ratings
- What happens to a word when it becomes a dashboard category: it loses the friction that made it useful; the ambiguity gets smoothed over rather than resolved
- She keeps writing about it anyway, because the thing it originally named — the lived experience of being inside an organization — is still real and still matters, even when the term for it has gone slack
- The work of a content person inside that inflation: write with enough specificity that the word recovers its traction for the reader, at least paragraph by paragraph

**Tone:** Wry, editorially honest, a little tired in the right way

## Idea 3 — The Gap Between Your Training and Your Work

**Angle:** She studied mass communication. She now spends most of her professional thinking on why employees feel recognized or don't. No one handed her a map for the distance between those two things — and it turns out the distance is the interesting part.

**Hook:** I studied mass communication. I now spend most of my time thinking about why employees feel seen or invisible at work. Nobody gave me a map for the space between those two things.

**Outline:**
- Her formal training was in mass communication and PR — not organizational psychology, not HR, not SaaS product marketing; the field she works in now was never a straight line from where she started
- The gap forced a kind of translation she's been doing for six years without always naming it: taking what she knows about audiences, attention, and message reception and applying it to a subject — the workplace — that operates by different rules
- What traversing an unplanned distance produces: a specific humility about category assumptions, because she arrived from outside and still remembers what the field looks like before you absorb its vocabulary
- The planned career is efficient. The unplanned one teaches you which of your instincts travel and which don't — which is different, and sometimes more useful, knowledge.

**Tone:** Honest, exploratory, quietly confident
