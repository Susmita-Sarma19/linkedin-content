# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-11-07

## Idea 1 — The Gender of Expertise

**Angle:** Building professional authority as a woman in a metro-dominated industry takes longer than the research says it should — and the process itself teaches you something the credential never could.

**Hook:** No one questions how I know what I know. That's a recent development.

**Outline:**
- For years, the expertise came first; the credibility came later — and the gap between them was not random
- What it actually takes to build authority outside the standard credentialing systems: not just the right degree, but the visible track record, the consistent presence, the patience with being right before someone calls you right
- The specific compound of being a woman + being from Northeast India in a professional space that wasn't built around either — and what it teaches you about how authority actually works (it's relational, not just meritocratic)
- What you gain from that slower road: a clearer eye for how credibility gets distributed in organizations, and why it matters for the HR content she writes about recognition and belonging

**Tone:** Quietly fierce

## Idea 2 — The Recognition Gap

**Angle:** She has written about the science of employee recognition for six years — and has had to notice, more than once, the gap between knowing what good recognition looks like and actually doing it in her own daily work.

**Hook:** I know what good recognition looks like. I still have to remind myself to do it.

**Outline:**
- The irony of being a content expert on something you still find difficult: knowing a practice and practicing it are two different skills
- What "recognition" looks like inside a small team vs. what it looks like in the research literature she's been reading and writing about for years
- The difference between content that informs and content that actually changes behavior — and what that tells her about the limits of what she produces
- Recognition as a daily habit, not a program or a ceremony — and what that realization costs her professionally as someone who has spent years making the case for the programs

**Tone:** Honest, a little rueful

## Idea 3 — The Failure Interview

**Angle:** Hosting an HR podcast means you eventually interview someone who explains, in careful detail, how they got something completely wrong — and that conversation teaches you more than a hundred conversations about best practices.

**Hook:** The most useful thing I've learned about employee engagement came from someone explaining how they failed at it.

**Outline:**
- The specific nature of what a podcast interview can hold that written content can't: real-time consequence, the natural hesitation before an admission, the shape of how someone tells a failure story
- One conversation (unnamed) where a practitioner described a recognition program that backfired, and why it did — and how that mapped against the research she'd been reading and citing for years
- What it revealed: best-practice content papers over the cases where the practice didn't hold. The failure cases are where the complexity lives.
- What she now listens for in every interview — not the moment someone says what worked, but the small signal of what didn't

**Tone:** Curious, quietly wry
