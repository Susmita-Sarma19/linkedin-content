# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-09-11

## Idea 1 — The Question Behind the Question

**Angle:** Nine years of live television interviews taught her that the most useful question is never the one she wrote in her prep notes — it's the one that arrives from actually listening. That skill transferred completely into HR content research and podcasting.

**Hook:** The question I wrote in my prep notes was never the best question I asked.

**Outline:**
- On television, a rehearsed question gets a rehearsed answer — closed, polished, useless for the viewer. Real questions arrive when you've listened long enough to hear what hasn't been said yet.
- She brought this into content work: the brief says "write about recognition," the practitioner she's talking to says something in minute twelve that's the actual post — but only if she was listening for it.
- The difference between researching a topic and researching a person's experience of that topic. The second one always wins.
- What broadcasters call "active listening" is really something simpler: patience with silence, and not filling it with your next prepared point.
- This is the Doordarshan skill she uses every day in her digital work — it just doesn't show up on any job description.

**Tone:** Warm, precise

## Idea 2 — The View From the Edge

**Angle:** Working in HR tech from Guwahati puts her at a deliberate remove from the cities where the industry's conversations are organized — which turns out to be an analytical advantage, not a handicap. From the periphery, you can see the frame.

**Hook:** Most of the HR thought leadership I read was produced by people within twenty kilometres of each other.

**Outline:**
- Bangalore, Mumbai, Delhi host the events, the roundtables, the LinkedIn-live recordings. The industry's conversation has a geography. Guwahati is not on that map.
- From outside that geography, certain assumptions become visible: that "modern workplace" means an open floor plan, that engagement problems look the same everywhere, that the companies most worth writing about are the ones with branded campuses.
- The organisations she knows in the Northeast are navigating recognition and culture in contexts that look nothing like that — different salary structures, different cultural expectations of acknowledgement, different relationships between manager and team.
- This isn't a complaint about metros. It's what distance from the centre makes possible: you see the assumptions when you're not inside them.
- What she tries to do with that in her writing: reach past the research that was mostly conducted in large tech companies and speak to the practitioner whose reality doesn't match the case study.

**Tone:** Grounded, quietly incisive

## Idea 3 — Writing the Room You're Not In

**Angle:** She has spent six years writing authoritatively about workplace culture without being an HR practitioner or a manager. There is something specific — and worth examining — about being one step removed from the decisions you're writing for.

**Hook:** I write about how managers should recognise their teams. I have never been a manager.

**Outline:**
- Most HR content comes from one of two places: consultants who've left practice, or practitioners who are too inside it to see the shape of it clearly. She's neither, which is an odd position to write from.
- Being removed from the decision means she can't lean on "in my experience managing." She has to do the actual research — sit with the evidence, interview the practitioners, hold the complexity rather than resolve it early.
- What the outsider position makes possible: no practice to defend, no default to fall back on. Which means she can write toward what's genuinely uncertain rather than what confirms the approach she already uses.
- The discipline required: authority built on rigour, not on experience. Every claim has to earn its place, because there's no "trust me, I've done this" to stand behind.
- The tension she lives with: some practitioners want to hear from someone who's been inside the room. What she offers instead is the view from just outside the door — which sometimes sees the room more clearly.

**Tone:** Honest, reflective
