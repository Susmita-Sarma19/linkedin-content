# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-15

## Idea 1 — The Listening Room

**Angle:** Nine years of broadcast interviews trained her to listen, not perform. The most transferable skill from television wasn't presence — it was what she learned to hear in a pause, a hesitation, an answer that changes shape mid-sentence.

**Hook:** Nine years on television. The skill I use most every day had nothing to do with being on camera.

**Outline:**
- Talk shows run on questions, but the real work happens in the pauses — when a guest's voice shifts, when the prepared answer gives way to the honest one
- Moving to content work, she expected the challenge would be losing her voice. The harder adjustment was training listening into a solitary practice — reading what questions an audience keeps asking, following the thread a brief doesn't name
- Listening in writing looks different from listening on air, but the instinct is the same: what did this person almost say?
- The best content isn't the answer you prepared — it's the response to what actually got asked

**Tone:** Reflective, quietly counterintuitive

## Idea 2 — The PR Ghost

**Angle:** Her formal training in PR and Image Management (Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan) installed a frame that has been running underneath her content work for years without being named: everything an organization does is already a message. She learned this before she knew what content strategy was.

**Hook:** Before I understood content marketing, I was trained to believe that everything your organization does is already sending a message. The course was in PR. The lesson never left.

**Outline:**
- PR at its foundation isn't spin — it's coherence: does what you say match what you do, and does either match what your audience actually experiences?
- That frame arrived before she ever wrote a content brief, and it runs underneath every piece of HR content she's produced: the question isn't just "what are we publishing?" — it's "is this consistent with what the organization is actually doing?"
- Six years of HR content at a recognition company means the coherence question is live and daily: you can't write about employee recognition meaningfully without asking whether the organization doing the recognizing has earned that conversation
- The training you think you've moved on from is sometimes the one running quietly under everything you built after

**Tone:** Warm, professionally surprising, a little wry

## Idea 3 — The Podcast Gap

**Angle:** Producing HR podcast episodes for six years has made her an expert in one specific thing: the gap between what HR professionals say on record and what they actually believe. That gap is the most honest signal available to anyone writing content for this field.

**Hook:** The most useful thing a guest ever told me came two minutes after we stopped recording.

**Outline:**
- Running a podcast means living in the on-record / off-record gap — what practitioners will say publicly in their domain versus what they're actually wrestling with, which is often entirely different
- It isn't dishonesty. It's the distance between the professional frame and the real experience — and that distance is consistently where the field is genuinely stuck, not where it's solved
- The discipline is in using that signal without breaching the confidence: what can be said, translated and generalized, that actually reaches the real question?
- For HR content specifically, this matters more than in most fields — practitioners are trained to manage how things sound. The podcast produces the version before that management kicks in.

**Tone:** Wry, specific, a little conspiratorial in a trustworthy way
