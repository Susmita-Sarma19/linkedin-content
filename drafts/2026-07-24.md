# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-24

## Idea 1 — The Double Shift

### Hook
For three years, I had two job titles, two professional identities, and two completely different definitions of what "being good at my job" meant.
One had a camera. The other had a content calendar.

### Angle
From September 2019 to December 2022, she was simultaneously a digital marketing professional at Vantage Circle and a Talk Show Host at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati. Three years and four months of parallel professional lives — building the HR podcast and learning content strategy on one end, and going to the studio for a live broadcast on the other.

They didn't cross-contaminate cleanly. The broadcast version of her was known locally, visible, expected to hold the room by presence alone. The Vantage Circle version of her was writing for readers she'd never meet, building a format from nothing, learning what SEO was and why it mattered. Two entirely different feedback loops. Two entirely different measures of whether the work had landed.

What the overlap forced was a discipline she didn't expect: the ability to hold two professional orientations at once and not let either collapse into the other. Doordarshan didn't make her a better content strategist. Content strategy didn't make her a better host. What holding both simultaneously did was clarify, eventually, which one she was actually building toward. Not because the other one failed — but because the quality of investment she was bringing to each had quietly become unequal.

She knew before she was ready to name it. The last episode aired in December 2022. By then, she'd already been building something more durable at Vantage Circle for over three years. The exit wasn't a door she closed — it was a direction she'd already been moving in, from the moment she started both at once.

### Source
Her overlapping tenure at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (Talk Show Host, August 2013 – December 2022) and Vantage Circle (started September 2019), meaning she ran both professional roles simultaneously for three years and four months. Prior drafts have examined the transition from Doordarshan to Vantage Circle as a career pivot ("The Double Career," July 16), the craft difference between broadcast and podcast, and what ended when the Doordarshan chapter closed ("After the Last Broadcast," July 22). None has examined the overlap period itself — what three years of holding two simultaneous professional identities required, and how the balance shifted before any formal decision was made.

### Resonance
For professionals navigating a planned exit from a long professional identity while actively building the next one — the specific discipline of holding both until you don't need to anymore. For her HR audience: the employee who is "transitioning out" in their head before they've given any formal signal is one of the hardest retention problems to detect. The dual-investment asymmetry shows up in the quality of their attention before anything else does. Understanding what it looks and feels like from the inside changes what questions you'd think to ask.

## Idea 2 — Going Back to Class

### Hook
In 2014, I was already hosting a television show at Doordarshan.
Then I enrolled in a postgraduate programme on image management in Kolkata.
I was going back to be a student while already doing the thing the credential would certify.

### Angle
She had been hosting at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati for over a year when she left for Kolkata to do a PG Diploma in Public Relations and Image Management at Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan. She already had the microphone. She went to get the theory.

The inversion is strange to explain. Going back to a classroom, in a different city, for a discipline she was practicing weekly — not because the job required it, and not because she was stuck. She went because there were things she was doing on camera every week that she didn't yet have language for. The instincts had formed. The framework hadn't.

What Kolkata gave her wasn't just the credential. It was the specific experience of sitting inside a formal discipline she already had working knowledge of — and discovering exactly where the textbooks were precise and exactly where they stopped short of what the camera actually required. The theory held in the places it was built for. The live broadcast was a different environment, and the programme couldn't fully account for it. That gap was the most useful thing she learned to see.

She came back to Guwahati with a clearer map of what she knew by instinct and what she now knew by design. Those are different kinds of knowledge, and they sit differently in the professional self. Having both — even imperfectly, even incompletely — changed the quality of what she could explain about her own work, to others and to herself.

### Source
Her PG Diploma in Public Relations/Image Management at Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan's Rajendra Prasad Institute of Communication and Management, Kolkata (2014–2015), which she pursued while having already begun her career as a Talk Show Host at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (August 2013). Prior drafts have examined the student-practitioner tension from the perspective of her Mass Communication degree and from the Doordarshan institutional years ("The Student on Camera," July 17). None has examined this specific biographical moment: returning to formal education in a different city, for a discipline she was already practicing, to get language for something instinct had already built. The Kolkata chapter specifically — what she sought there, what the credential did and didn't provide, and what she brought back — is unexplored.

### Resonance
For professionals who've gone back to formal education not out of credential requirement but out of the hunger to understand what they were already doing — and who found the experience less straightforward than a degree-then-practice sequence would have been. For her HR audience: formal learning is most useful, and most disorienting, when it meets an existing practice and has to reckon with it. Practitioners who design learning and development often assume a blank slate. The person who already has the instinct and goes back for the framework is a harder student to design for — and arguably learns more.

## Idea 3 — The Outside Expert

### Hook
I have published more than I can count on what managers should do when an employee is disengaged.
I have never been a manager.

### Angle
Six years of writing about employee recognition, belonging, and engagement — for HR directors, people managers, CHROs. She's covered what good recognition looks like, what it costs to get it wrong, what the research says about manager behavior in moments of disengagement. She's done this with genuine authority, built piece by piece through six years of reading studies, interviewing practitioners, and learning to ask questions that surface what the standard playbook misses.

She is not an HR professional. She has never designed an engagement program, run a performance conversation, or been accountable for a team's survey scores.

That position — expert communicator adjacent to a practice she's never held — is a specific kind of authority with specific limits, and she's learned to hold both. The limit: she doesn't have the practitioner's texture of what it actually felt like when the program didn't land. What she has instead is the breadth of having talked to hundreds of people who do — the accumulated picture that no single practitioner has, because no single practitioner has lived inside all of those organizations.

What the outside expert brings isn't better knowledge. It's different knowledge. The synthesis across cases the practitioner never has time to read, the question from outside the daily pressure that reveals something the daily pressure makes hard to see. The practitioner knows the room. The outside expert has read all the rooms, and learned to ask what they have in common.

She's clear-eyed about the trade. The authority is real, but it has a shape. The practitioner who knows she's the communicator, not the practitioner, trusts her differently — and she thinks, more accurately — than one who doesn't.

### Source
Her six years at Vantage Circle writing HR content (Web Content Writer, Digital Marketing Executive, Senior Digital Marketing Specialist, Assistant Manager — September 2019 to present) as someone with a background in Mass Communication and broadcast media, not in HR practice or people management. She writes authoritatively about a domain she has never been a practitioner within. Prior drafts have examined her content practice in depth — SEO vs. utility ("Writing For the Person Behind the Query," July 14), the shift from accurate to genuinely useful ("The Tuesday Behind the Data," July 17), the limits of frameworks from outside a culture ("The Framework and the Room," July 22). None has examined her specific epistemic position as an expert communicator writing about a practice she has never held — and what that position offers and honestly costs.

### Resonance
For content professionals, communicators, and journalists who build genuine expertise in fields they've never practiced: the authority is real and so are the limits. Knowing which is which is the discipline. For HR practitioners reading her content: the outside expert who knows they're outside is a more useful resource than the one who doesn't. The communicator who has read every study and interviewed every practitioner, and is honest about what that is and isn't, is giving you a different thing than the expert practitioner — and a thing that's genuinely hard to build inside a single organization.
