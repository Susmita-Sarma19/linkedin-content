# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-28

## Idea 1 — The Reach Counter

### Hook
In August 2013, I was broadcasting. In September 2013, I walked into rural villages in Assam asking people if they'd seen an ad. I was doing both jobs in the same autumn. I didn't have language for what that taught me. I do now.

### Angle
September 2013. One month into her career as a Talk Show Host at Doordarshan, she was a Surveyor on an IIMC research campaign measuring whether Bharat Nirman government advertisements had reached rural audiences around Guwahati. She went door to door asking whether people had seen the ad, whether it registered, what they understood it to mean.

In August, she had been on the creation side — in the studio, in front of the camera, producing broadcast. In September, she was on the reception side — walking the actual ground where broadcast signals arrived, or failed to. Most content professionals spend entire careers on one side of this question. She was on both sides in sequential months, at the very beginning of her career, before she understood what she was learning.

What it built — quietly, untheorized — was an early sense that the gap between what you broadcast and what an audience receives is not a gap in the data. It's the actual problem. Everything she's done in content and marketing since has been, in some form, an attempt to take that gap seriously.

### Source
IIMC Research Campaign on Reach and Access of Bharat Nirman Advertisements, September 2013, rural Guwahati — a one-month role that appears as a single line in her profile and has never been examined in any prior draft. The timing — one month after becoming a Talk Show Host — put her simultaneously on both sides of a broadcast signal: the producer and the measurer of reception.

### Resonance
For content professionals: most people who create content and most people who measure its reach are different people in different functions, often in different departments. The one who has held both understands the gap in a way neither specialist alone can. For her HR audience: the recognition program designers and the people measuring whether employees actually feel recognized are almost never the same people. That structural gap is the problem. September 2013 is what it looks like when one person holds both sides.

## Idea 2 — The Late Textbook

### Hook
In 2013, I was hosting a talk show while finishing a master's in Mass Communication. My professor was explaining interview technique. I had already done twenty episodes. The order was wrong. What it produced wasn't.

### Angle
The standard model: formal training precedes professional practice. Credentials authorize entry. She had the sequence backwards. Her MA in Mass Communication at Gauhati University ran from 2012 to 2014. Her professional broadcasting career started in August 2013 — while she was still in the middle of the degree. Her credential didn't precede her career. It followed it, catching up a year later.

What the classroom gave her when she already had the job: not preparation, but language. She'd spent months learning by doing what it felt like when an interview question landed, when a segment's pacing worked, when a transition between topics held the viewer. Then she sat in a seminar on the theory of interview. And the theory didn't feel beside the point. It felt like a vocabulary for something she'd already built by feel.

The credential that arrives after the practice is a different object than the one that arrives before it. It doesn't authorize the work — the work is already running. What it does is help you explain to yourself and other people what you've been doing. The theory held accountable by experience from its first encounter is more precisely understood than the theory applied in advance of any.

Most formal education systems are designed for blank slates. She knows what it feels like to arrive at the seminar with the texture of the thing already in her hands, because that's what she lived.

### Source
MA in Mass Communication, Gauhati University (2012–2014), overlapping with Production Intern, Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (June–July 2013), and Talk Show Host (August 2013 onward). The chronological fact that she was practicing the profession before completing its credential — and what that sequencing produced — has not appeared in any prior draft. Prior drafts have examined the intern-to-host transition (July 20, July 25) but not the simultaneous formal study running through the same period.

### Resonance
For professionals who returned to formal education mid-career, or completed a credential while already inside the discipline it names: the theory arrived late for you too, and it was more useful for that. You had experience to test it against. For her HR audience: onboarding designed for people with no prior context is a different programme than onboarding designed for someone arriving with the job's substance already formed. The learner who is already inside the thing being taught is the most overlooked learner in most training design.

## Idea 3 — The Wrong Reader

### Hook
I write most of my HR content for managers and HR leaders. For six years, I've watched it get read by the people they manage. I'm still working out how to write for both without betraying either.

### Angle
The intended reader: the HR practitioner or manager who designs recognition programs, sets the culture conditions, makes the decisions that shape whether people feel seen at work. She wrote for that person. Her topics, structure, and search strategy were built to reach the professional who manages the function.

Then the emails came from the people on the other side of those decisions. Junior employees who found the article on "why recognition programs fail" while trying to understand why they felt invisible. Individual contributors using HR content to make sense of their own situation — not as a tool for design, but as a mirror. The person who wasn't the intended audience but for whom the piece worked for different reasons, often better ones.

This happens more than she expected. Six years of it has changed how she writes. When she describes what managers should do, she's always had to imagine the practitioner who will implement it. She's also started imagining the person the practitioner is implementing it for — because that person is reading too, just differently. The piece serves both readers or it quietly fails one of them.

She can't control who shows up. She can write so that whoever does finds something honest.

### Source
Six years of HR content production at Vantage Circle (September 2019–present), including direct feedback — emails, comments, messages — from readers who don't match the intended audience persona: individual contributors and employees reading content designed for their managers or HR functions. The experience of practitioner-facing content landing with the people the practitioner manages, and what that does to how she writes, is entirely unexplored across all prior drafts.

### Resonance
For content professionals in practitioner or B2B fields: the intended reader is a planning assumption, not a guarantee. The piece that earns both the decision-maker and the person affected by the decision requires understanding what each needs from the same content — and they need different things from it. For her HR audience: the recognition program designed for how HR imagines employees will experience it, and the program as actually experienced, are not the same object. The feedback that closes the gap almost always comes from the people the program is designed for, not from those designing it.
