# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-06-29

## Idea 1 — The First Draft Problem

**Angle:** Writing for time-poor HR practitioners taught her that the first draft is almost always written for the writer — too complete, too careful, too academic — and the real work is stripping it to what a manager with 11 open tabs and a 2pm meeting can actually use.

**Hook:** My first drafts are never for the reader. They're for me. The second draft is where the real editing begins.

**Outline:**
- How HR content often passes editorially but fails practically — correct, cited, thoughtful, but unreadable in the moment it's meant to matter
- The discipline of stripping scaffolding: the context, the caveats, the hedging that writing naturally accumulates
- The specific question that cuts through: "What does a manager do differently on Monday because they read this?"
- Why writing for practitioners is harder than writing for thought leaders — practitioners need to use what you write, not just agree with it

**Tone:** Craft-focused, precise

## Idea 2 — The City in the Room

**Angle:** Being the only voice from Northeast India in many professional conversations trained her to hear what a room doesn't say — the contexts assumed, the audiences not considered, the professional map drawn without the Northeast on it.

**Hook:** A lot of professional conversations happen in rooms that assume everyone grew up reading the same newspapers.

**Outline:**
- The specific experience of joining a pan-India digital marketing or HR discussion and watching the defaults go unexamined — metro case studies as "India," Delhi/Mumbai as the implied reader
- What this teaches you to do: check your own assumptions before publishing anything, because you know what it feels like to be left out of someone else's assumption
- How building for a non-default context becomes a marketing advantage — audience perspective as survival skill, not optional empathy exercise
- The quiet case for more geographic diversity in how the Indian professional world talks about work, growth, and ambition

**Tone:** Grounded, quietly pointed

## Idea 3 — The Manager in the Middle

**Angle:** After years in employee recognition, she keeps returning to the same gap: the manager is the most powerful force in whether recognition actually lands — and typically the last person anyone trains.

**Hook:** Your recognition platform is only as good as the manager who decides whether to use it before or after the quarterly review.

**Outline:**
- How recognition in practice flows through one person: the direct manager, not the platform or the program
- The structural irony — companies invest in technology, culture initiatives, and company-wide programs while the manager who interacts with the employee daily often has no real guidance on what "good" looks like
- What good recognition actually requires: specific, timely, untied to performance ratings — none of which a platform enforces
- The training gap is not a technology problem, and technology has been quietly obscuring that fact for years

**Tone:** Honest, practical, mildly diagnostic
