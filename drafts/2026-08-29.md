# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-08-29

## Idea 1 — The Simultaneous Life

**Angle:** For years she wasn't switching careers — she was running two at the same time. The TV host and the digital marketer weren't sequential selves. They coexisted, which is a different kind of demand entirely.

**Hook:** There's a version of my career timeline that reads like a pivot. It wasn't a pivot. For most of the decade, I was doing both at once.

**Outline:**
- The reality of holding a live talk show at Doordarshan while building a content and marketing career — not sequentially but simultaneously, for years
- What it actually demanded: not just time, but the ability to inhabit two completely different professional registers in the same day
- What each gave the other — the discipline of broadcast sharpened the writing; the writing work gave the on-camera work something to think about
- What finally made it possible to step away from broadcasting — not a pivot, but a quiet resolution of something that had been running in parallel long enough

**Tone:** Candid, corrective

## Idea 2 — The Price of Attention

**Angle:** Two years at a media placement company taught her the exact commercial value of an impression. That knowledge followed her into content marketing — and changed how she thinks about earning reach versus buying it.

**Hook:** I spent two years pricing media space — print, radio, TV, digital. I knew the cost of putting something in front of someone. Then I became a content marketer, which means I spent the next six years learning what it takes to earn that instead.

**Outline:**
- releaseMyAd: the work of mapping and pricing media attention across formats — what a classified ad costs versus a prime-time slot, how reach is quantified and negotiated
- The shift in mental model when you've seen attention sold: you never lose the sense of what you're asking someone to spend when they read your piece
- Content marketing as the bet that earning reach is worth more than buying it — and what backs that bet up (authority, trust, long-term search equity)
- What changes in your writing when you've priced the alternative — a certain sobriety about whether the piece actually earns what it's asking for

**Tone:** Clear-eyed, grounded in economics

## Idea 3 — Before the Program

**Angle:** She's spent six years producing content about recognition programs. Here she argues what all the platforms and frameworks can't replace — and names the thing that recognition was before it became an industry.

**Hook:** The most effective recognition I've ever seen at work didn't happen inside a program. It happened because a manager paid attention.

**Outline:**
- Recognition as an industry now has platforms, frameworks, taxonomies, budget line items — and most of it is genuinely useful
- But the premise underneath all of it is that a specific human being noticed something specific about another human being, and said so
- What a program can do: remove the friction, prompt the moment, create a habit, build a culture over time; what it cannot do: see the person for you
- The question for HR leaders isn't whether to have a program — it's whether the program is replacing the noticing or enabling it; the answer is usually obvious once you look

**Tone:** Quietly provocative, earned from the inside
