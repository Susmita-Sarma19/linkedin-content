# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-14

## Idea 1 — The Two Years Buying Attention

**Angle:** After two years placing ads at a media planning firm, she spent the next six years writing content meant to earn attention — and what that reversal taught her about what "engagement" actually measures.

**Hook:** At releaseMyAd, my job was to buy the right slot in the right place for the right audience.
It changed how I read every engagement metric I've seen since.

**Outline:**
- Two years in Kolkata doing media planning — not creating the message, deciding where it lands. Who is paying attention here, and what are they paying attention *for*?
- What you learn sitting on the buying side: attention is cheap in some contexts and stubbornly resistant in others, and most people confuse presence (the ad ran) with reception (the message landed)
- The reversal when she moved to HR content: no media budget, no guaranteed slot. Either practitioners find it useful enough to keep reading, or they don't. No amount of distribution fixes content that doesn't earn its place.
- The media planning instinct she carried forward: audience question first, always — not "what do we want to say" but "who is in this space and what brought them here"

**Tone:** Dry and precise — professional insight, not inspiration

## Idea 2 — The Interviewer's Discipline

**Angle:** Podcasting made her realize the skill nine years of TV had actually built was not talking — it was asking the question that lets someone say what they actually think.

**Hook:** I thought what Doordarshan gave me was stage presence.
The podcast told me otherwise. The real thing was quieter.

**Outline:**
- Nine years of talk show hosting built visible professional confidence in front of a camera — which can look, from the outside, like the skill of talking
- Producing and hosting an HR podcast revealed the other skill: listening in a way that lets a guest reach past their prepared answer to the thing they actually mean — and staying out of the way long enough for that to happen
- What a good interview question actually does: specific enough to cut through the rehearsed response, open enough to let the real thing through — and the person who asks it is mostly quiet afterward
- The same discipline carries directly into managing people, running a one-on-one, or diagnosing what's actually happening on a team. The manager who listens like an interviewer — not gathering ammunition, just holding space for the real answer — gets a different quality of information than the one who asks and then talks

**Tone:** Quietly surprised — a discovery framed as a corrective to a held assumption

## Idea 3 — The Practitioner in the Room

**Angle:** Six years of writing HR content has revealed a category she stays out of deliberately: technically accurate advice that quietly assumes the reader hasn't thought of the obvious thing.

**Hook:** The worst HR content is not wrong.
It's correct in a way that manages to condescend.

**Outline:**
- What the category tends to get wrong: packaging things competent practitioners already do as if they're surfacing something new. The list of recognition best practices that any functional manager applies before lunch, framed as insight.
- The asymmetry that good HR content has to hold: the writer is outside the room describing it. The practitioner has been living it. That gap should shape everything — level of assumption, specificity of example, what you choose not to explain
- The discipline she's developed before publishing: would a capable HR manager feel talked to or talked at? The answer determines whether the piece needed to exist in the form it took.
- Not a critique of the people producing it — most are writing from genuine care. It's a calibration problem. The writer's expertise is real; so is the practitioner's. The content fails when the writer forgets which kind of knowledge they actually have

**Tone:** Direct and quietly sharp — a practitioner's internal standard made visible, not a lecture
