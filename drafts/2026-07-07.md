# LinkedIn Ideas — 2026-07-07

## Idea 1 — The Public Mandate

### Hook
For nine years, I made a show for India's national public broadcaster.
Nobody asked if it was performing. That turned out to be instructive.

### Angle
Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati operates under Prasar Bharati — India's public service broadcasting mandate. Public funding. A stated purpose: to serve India's public, including the people who live in places no commercial broadcaster has economic reason to reach.

She spent nine years in that institutional context. The question was never "is this performing?" in the commercial sense. It was something slower and harder: is this actually useful to the person watching it?

When she joined Vantage Circle, the metrics arrived. Impressions. Organic clicks. Keyword rankings. She learned to use them, and uses them well. But the public service years had already built a prior question: before the metric, what is this actually doing for the person who encounters it? Does it help them in the situation they're actually in?

Commercial metrics can approximate this. A high-ranking article might genuinely serve the HR manager who found it. Often it does. But the metric and the value are not the same thing, and the longer she worked in content, the more clearly she noticed how easily one substitutes for the other.

The public service years built the first question before the commercial years built the shortcut. She still runs the first check before she runs the second. She thinks the order matters.

### Source
Nine years as Talk Show Host at Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati — a Prasar Bharati regional centre, publicly funded with a national public service mandate, institutionally distinct from commercial broadcasters in both purpose and accountability. No prior draft has examined the institutional character of Doordarshan specifically. All prior references treat it generically as "television" or "broadcast" without examining what kind of broadcasting it is: state-funded, mandate-driven, not answerable to ratings in the commercial sense. Entirely unexplored territory.

### Resonance
HR practitioners, internal communicators, and L&D professionals who feel the gap between "we ran the program" and "the program worked." The public service question — is this actually useful to the person at the other end? — is their question every time they design a training, write a policy, or launch a recognition initiative. Metric substitution for genuine service is the occupational hazard of anyone measuring things that are supposed to help people. The post names the prior question, and argues for running it before the metric.

## Idea 2 — Writing on the Moving Surface

### Hook
In 2022, I published a guide on what makes employees feel valued.
That year, global employee engagement hit its lowest recorded level.

### Angle
From 2021 to 2023, HR practitioners were navigating one of the most contested periods in the field's recent history. Mass resignations. Workforce expectations shifting at speed. The stated commitments of organizations being tested against actual practice. Every conversation about recognition, engagement, and what employees need was louder and less settled than it had been in years.

She was writing about it the whole time. Articles, frameworks, the content calendar kept moving because the audience needed it, even as the research this content was built on was being actively complicated by what the workforce was doing. The 2021 Gallup data was grim. The 2022 data was grimmer. She was writing "what good recognition looks like" while the people she was writing for were navigating a fundamental renegotiation of what work was supposed to offer them.

The instinct was to wait for the field to settle. She didn't, and she thinks that was right. Not because the frameworks were immune to the disruption — they weren't — but because the HR manager reading in 2022 needed something to hold onto more than they needed perfect certainty.

What she learned: content during disruption has a different job than content during stability. The audience isn't looking for the settled answer. They're looking for someone who is thinking clearly in the same conditions they're in. The credibility wasn't in the framework. It was in the honesty about what the framework didn't yet know.

### Source
Six years of HR content writing at Vantage Circle (2019–present), spanning the pandemic period (2020), the Great Resignation (2021–2022), the hybrid renegotiation (2022–2023), and subsequent disruptions — a sustained run of writing with authority about employee engagement and recognition while the field itself was in active upheaval. The specific experience of producing HR content during maximum uncertainty in the domain being written about — what it requires, what it changes about how you write, and what it teaches about what content is for during disruption — has not appeared in any prior draft. "The Scaffolding Problem" (June 28) examined the HR tech honesty challenge; "The Time Capsule" (June 16) examined the podcast archive across those years. Neither examined the content writer's position during the disruption itself.

### Resonance
HR practitioners, L&D professionals, and internal communicators who had to run programs and make the case for employee experience during 2021–2023 — while the evidence was complicated and the workforce was moving. The post names what good professional judgment looks like when the field is unsettled: not waiting for certainty, but thinking clearly in its absence. For her content audience: writing for a disrupted field from inside it produces something the retrospective account can't. The reader in the middle of it knows the difference.

## Idea 3 — The Second Answer

### Hook
I've been interviewing people about their work for fifteen years.
The most useful answer almost never comes first.

### Angle
Nine years of live television talk shows. Then podcast interviews about HR, leadership, and employee experience. A few hundred conversations, across two formats that couldn't be more different: studio lights and rundowns, then a microphone in a quiet room.

One thing has never changed across either medium. The first answer a guest gives is almost always the answer they prepared. Well-constructed. Professionally sound. Already told to someone else. It's what they came with.

The second answer — the one that comes after you've stayed with the first, and asked something that doesn't let the preparation stand unchallenged — is where the real conversation starts. Not always. But consistently enough that she treats the first answer as the entry point, not the destination.

This doesn't make the preparation bad. It means the preparation is a starting position. Guests arrive knowing what they'll say. The conversation is what happens when they encounter someone who actually listened to it.

Most people know more than their first answer contains. Not because they're withholding, but because nobody has asked in a way that asks for the rest. The most useful interviewing skill isn't the question. It's the patience to wait for what the first answer didn't include. Almost everyone has a second answer. Very few conversations create the conditions for it to surface.

### Source
Nine years and five months as Talk Show Host, Doordarshan Kendra Guwahati (August 2013–December 2022), plus podcast production and interviewing at Vantage Circle (2019–present) — fifteen-plus years of sustained professional interviewing across two distinct media. The longitudinal patterns — what you only see across hundreds of conversations, not a few well-executed ones — have not appeared in any prior draft. Prior ideas examined: the conditions before the first question (July 3), real-time listening precision (June 23), on-camera vs. audio craft differences (July 6), and skilled self-effacement in production (June 27). None examined what accumulates over a body of interviews: the recurring pattern that a single skilled conversation, however good, cannot produce.

### Resonance
HR practitioners who conduct interviews — exit conversations, engagement check-ins, skip-levels, performance reviews — and who know the experience of receiving an answer that is correct and complete and somehow doesn't tell them what they needed to know. The second answer is what good HR conversation practice is supposed to create. The post names what it takes to make it available: not a technique, but a willingness to stay with the first answer without accepting it as the final one. Fifteen years of interviewing is how she learned this. One careful conversation is enough to practise it.
